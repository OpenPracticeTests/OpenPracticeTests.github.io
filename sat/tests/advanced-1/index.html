Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

Clay tablets from the Old Babylonian scribal schools were meant to be temporary. A student's exercise could be scraped down and the surface rewritten so long as the clay stayed damp, and only a tablet that dried before anyone reused it survives to be read now. The texts recovered from the schoolroom floors are therefore a ____ record of the curriculum: what endured did so because nobody got around to erasing it.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The final clause explains that a tablet survived only because "nobody got around to erasing it," which makes survival a matter of chance rather than of selection. "Fortuitous" names exactly that. "Prescriptive" would describe a record that dictates what should be taught, a claim the text never makes. "Systematic" and "meticulous" both credit the record with a care that the passage spends its whole length denying.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

A lichen has no roots to draw on and no waxy cuticle to seal it off, so it absorbs whatever the air delivers. Where sulfur dioxide is heavy, the branching species disappear first, and a wall that once carried a dozen forms is left with an ____ crust of one or two. Surveyors have learned to read such a wall the way a chemist reads a precipitate.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The sentence contrasts a wall of "a dozen forms" with one holding "one or two," so the word must mean reduced in variety. "Impoverished" carries that sense. "Variegated" means various, which reverses the contrast the sentence is built on. "Unblemished" would praise the depleted wall. "Obstinate" describes a temperament, and the survivors' persistence is not what the sentence is measuring.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 3 of 27

A choreographer's score cannot fix a dance the way a composer's score fixes a melody. Notation captures the position of a limb and the count that carries it there, and a company can reconstruct a work from those marks decades after its last performance. Yet the quality that distinguishes one dancer's arm from another's remains ____, present in the room and absent from the page.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The final clause defines the blank: something that exists in performance but escapes the notation. "Fugitive," in its sense of fleeting or hard to capture, is that word. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 4 of 27

The vault of a Gothic nave pushes outward as much as down, and the higher it rises the harder its walls lean away from it. Builders first met that thrust with mass, thickening the walls until the windows they wanted became impossible. When the cracks opened anyway, masons threw arches over the aisle roofs to brace the upper wall from outside. The flying buttress, now read as the signature of the style, began as a repair.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

The text moves in three steps: outward thrust is the problem, thickened walls are the answer that failed, and the external arch is the fix that became the style's emblem. The sound on a wax cylinder lives in a groove a few thousandths of an inch deep, and the stylus that plays it back grinds the groove a little further away. The cylinder is read once, at a distance, and never touched again.

The text names a problem, that playback destroys what it plays, and then explains the optical method that avoids it, ending on the point that the cylinder is never touched. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 6 of 27

Text 1

A moth holds its course by keeping a distant light at a fixed angle to its body, a rule that works when the light is the moon and fails when it is a street lamp thirty feet up. Shield the lamps so that no light escapes sideways, an engineer proposes, and the moths above a town will steer past it rather than spiral into it.

Text 2

When a county replaced its white lamps with amber ones, the moths caught in traps beneath them fell by two thirds. The decline was as large under the unshielded amber lamps as under the shielded ones, and shielding the older white lamps, on its own, had changed almost nothing. What the moths were responding to was the light's color.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the proposal in Text 1?

Text 2 reports that shielding alone changed almost nothing while the switch to amber cut captures by two thirds under shielded and unshielded lamps alike. So the author disagrees with the proposal, which fails on its own terms, and grants only that the lamps must change: the reason is their color rather than the direction of their light. Captures beneath the amber lamps plainly did decline, by two thirds, and the shields made no difference to that either way. Text 2 says nothing about how a moth treats the moon, so it is not arguing about which rule the moon calls up. And it reports the opposite of the last option: shielding did less than replacing, not more.

A cuttlefish settling onto gravel reproduces its pattern within a second, though every test of the animal's eyes says it cannot distinguish red from green. It would be reasonable to conclude that the match is a lucky average, a compromise the gravel happens to accept. But a cuttlefish set on a printed checkerboard reproduces the checker's scale, and the photoreceptors scattered through its skin respond to wavelength on their own. The eye is not the only organ that can be said to see.

Which choice best describes the function of the last sentence in the text as a whole?

The checkerboard result and the skin's photoreceptors are findings about one animal; the final sentence lifts them into a claim about what seeing is and where it can happen. That is a generalization. Peregrine falcons hunt by stooping onto prey from above, which is why the species nested historically on cliffs and why it took so readily to the ledges of tall buildings. But a city pair fledges more young than a cliff pair, and the difference is not the ledge. Pigeons feed on spilled grain year round, so the falcons' prey does not thin in winter, and the young of a city pair leave the nest into a food supply that a cliff cannot match.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The text grants that the ledge explains why falcons will nest on a building, then says plainly that "the difference is not the ledge" and locates the advantage in a prey supply that never thins. Nothing says the birds abandoned cliffs; the text compares pairs in both places. Whether a falcon can tell a building from a cliff is never raised. And pigeons are named as one reliable prey, not as the only one.

According to the text, why does the antlion larva dig its pit to the steepest angle the sand will hold?

The text says a grain on such a wall is on the point of moving, so an ant stepping on the rim starts a slide it cannot stop, and the final sentence confirms that a gentler wall would let the ant escape. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 10 of 27

Cells accumulate damage to their DNA, and the odds that one of them turns cancerous rise with the number of cells and the years they live. A bowhead whale carries a thousand times the cells of a person and lives twice as long, so it should be riddled with tumors, and it is not. Biologists sequencing bowhead tissue have found extra copies of genes that repair double-strand breaks, and cultured bowhead cells mend such breaks faster and more accurately than human cells do. This evidence suggests that the whale's longevity ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The paradox is that cell count and lifespan predict tumors the whale does not get, and the evidence offered is extra repair genes and faster, more accurate repair. The completion must join those: repair keeps the damage from accumulating as the arithmetic predicts. The whale has more copies, not fewer. Cell count is what makes the whale's health surprising, not what limits its life. And to say longevity has no bearing on damage discards the passage's opening premise.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 11 of 27

A small spider climbs to a leaf tip, raises its abdomen, and releases silk until the strands lift it into the air. The behavior has long been credited to wind. But the atmosphere carries a vertical electric field, silk leaves the spinnerets negatively charged, and a strand entering that field would be pushed upward whether or not the air moved. Researchers have proposed that the spider senses the field itself and launches only when it is strong.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' proposal?

The proposal makes two claims: the spider detects the field, and the field alone can launch it. A chamber with no moving air isolates the field, and takeoff that follows the switch tests detection as well as lift. Farther travel on windy days supports the wind account the proposal is arguing against. A negative charge on the silk is already granted in the text and shows nothing about sensing. And failing to launch from the wrong perch speaks to posture, not to the field.

The cubes she cut left mortar behind and were the ones she kept prying up; by noon she reached only for what the floor supplied. That is a preference formed by the morning's work. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 15 of 27

Among the fragments recovered from the floor of the kiln ____ a seal impressed with the potter's own mark.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The sentence is inverted: its subject is "a seal," which follows the verb, and a singular subject takes "was." The plural options are drawn to "fragments," which is the object of the preposition "among" and can never be the subject of the sentence. A verb agrees with its subject, not with the nearest noun.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 16 of 27

The two panels ____ were cleaned last spring now hang at the far end of the gallery, where the light is steadiest.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The clause identifies which two panels are meant, so it is essential to the sentence and takes no commas. An essential clause is introduced by "that." Setting the clause off with a comma before "which" would make it supplementary, implying the gallery holds only two panels. Pairing a comma with "that" is never correct, and a comma after "which" separates the relative pronoun from its own verb.

The ledger of the guild's tanners, a volume of nearly four hundred leaves bound in calfskin and annotated by three generations of clerks, ____ the price of hides in every year but one.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 18 of 27

The bridge carried carts across the gorge for two centuries ____ it was never built to bear the weight of a locomotive.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

"However" is a conjunctive adverb, not a conjunction, so it cannot join two independent clauses on its own. A semicolon before it does the joining, and a comma after it sets it off. Using only a comma produces a comma splice, and using no punctuation at all runs the two clauses together. A colon promises an explanation or a list, and the second clause qualifies the first rather than explaining it.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 19 of 27

The commission concluded that the harbor works had been designed not only to shelter the fleet but also ____ the silt the river carried down each spring.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The correlative pair "not only ... but also" must join grammatically parallel elements. The first is the infinitive "to shelter," so the second must be an infinitive, "to trap." A participle, a prepositional phrase, and a full clause each break the parallel, and the clause additionally leaves "it" without a clear antecedent.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 20 of 27

The cooper on ____ the brewery relied for forty years never once worked from a drawing.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The pronoun is the object of the preposition "on," which is fronted ahead of it, and only the objective form can follow a preposition directly: "on whom." That is what settles it. In the bare relative clause "the cooper ___ the brewery relied on," object "who" would pass in ordinary modern usage, but "on who" does not. "Whoever" is subjective too, and "which" refers to things rather than people.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 21 of 27

The mason who had rebuilt the same chimney twice in a single decade ____ that the mortar, not the brick, was at fault.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Nothing may separate a subject from its verb. The subject is "the mason," extended by an essential clause naming which mason, and the verb follows it directly with no punctuation. A comma before the verb, after it, or on both sides each cuts the sentence at its one unbreakable joint.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 22 of 27

A mature street canopy holds the afternoon air several degrees below that of a bare street a block away. Shade alone does not explain the gap: a canvas awning of the same area cools the pavement beneath it and leaves the air above it unchanged. ____ the water a tree lifts from the soil and releases through its leaves carries heat away with it, and an awning lifts none.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

"Rather" is what a writer uses after rejecting one explanation to supply the one that replaces it, and the awning sentence has just rejected shade. "Likewise" would claim the tree and the awning behave alike, which is the opposite of the point. "Nevertheless" would concede the awning result and press on with shade anyway. "For instance" would make transpiration an example of shade rather than an alternative to it.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 23 of 27

Groundwater under the valley moves so slowly that a contaminant spilled this year might not reach the town's wells for decades, and a utility cannot manage a supply by waiting to learn what is in it. ____ hydrologists pour a harmless dye into the ground at the spill site and time its arrival at each well, turning a question about the distant future into a measurement they can finish this season.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

The first sentence rules out waiting; the second supplies the practice that takes its place, so the transition must mark a substitution. "Instead" does that. "Nonetheless" would signal that hydrologists pour the dye in spite of the slow movement, when in fact they pour it because of it. "In addition" would make the dye a second thing utilities do while waiting. And "Similarly" would liken the two, though one replaces the other.

A sentence emphasizing a similarity must state something true of both methods, and the notes offer exactly one such fact: each locates a pattern from the sample inside a reference pattern. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 27 of 27

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Odessa Ilyin cut her printing blocks from the end grain of boxwood rather than from the side grain.
  • End grain resists splitting, so a cut line can turn sharply without breaking away.
  • Ilyin's prints carry lines as fine as those of a metal engraving.
  • Some critics first took her prints for engravings on metal.
  • She exhibited the uncut blocks beside the finished prints.

The student wants to explain why Ilyin's prints could carry such fine lines. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

The question asks for a cause, so the sentence must join the material to the effect it makes possible. Only one option does: end grain resists splitting, and a line that does not break away can be cut finely. The critics' mistake is evidence of how fine the lines were, not of why. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 1 of 27

Iron gall ink bites into the paper it is written on, and a page of it can be sound on one side and lace on the other. Conservators once bathed such leaves in an alkaline solution, which arrests the acid and buys the page a century. The treatment leaves the writing ____, however: fixed where it stands, and a shade paler at every stroke.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The colon spells out the blank: the writing survives but is "a shade paler at every stroke," which is a weakening rather than a loss. "Attenuated" names it. "Obliterated" overstates, since the writing is still there and still fixed. "Unaltered" understates, since the ink has faded. "Consolidated" describes the fixing but ignores the fading, and the sentence signals with "however" that something is given up.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 2 of 27

The first English translators of the Norse sagas smoothed the verse into fluent prose, and their versions were read admiringly for a century. A modern translator ____ that fluency. She keeps the knotted compounds even where they stall the line, on the principle that a reader is owed the difficulty the original made, not a difficulty removed on the reader's behalf.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The next sentence shows the translator keeping the hard compounds rather than smoothing them, so she gives up the fluency the earlier translators achieved. "Forgoes" means exactly that. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 4 of 27

For most of a century the meter was a bar of platinum kept in a vault outside Paris, and every other meter in the world was a copy of a copy of it. In 1960 the definition moved to a count of wavelengths of light emitted by an isotope of krypton, which any well-equipped laboratory could reproduce. In 1983 it moved again: the meter became the distance light travels in a fixed fraction of a second. Each change asked less of the object and more of the constant.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

The text lists the platinum bar, the krypton wavelength, and the speed of light in order, then closes by naming what the sequence has in common: each definition leans less on a particular object. Nothing is weighed against its costs, no committee rejects anything, and no controversy or contending positions appear; the three definitions succeed one another without anyone disputing them in the text.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 6 of 27

Text 1

A glued joint is judged on a bench: two pieces are clamped, pulled apart, and the load at which the glue line lets go is written down. A finger joint carries more glue line than any other joint a workshop cuts, and on that bench it outlasts every rival by a margin nothing else comes near. Renwick's group opened eleven hundred chairs brought in for repair over nine years. Not one joint had been pulled apart. Renwick's own bench trials reproduced Vasquez's figures exactly. The chairs came back at much the same rate whatever joint held them together.

Based on the texts, how would Renwick's group (Text 2) most likely characterize Vasquez's claim in Text 1 that the finger joint is the strongest joint in furniture?

Renwick's group reproduced Vasquez's bench figures and disputes none of them, so every fact behind the claim survives; what it refuses is the word. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 7 of 27

The surveyor's chain laid across a slope measures the slope, not the ground beneath it, and a map drawn from such chains gives a hillside more acres than a level field of the same extent. Eighteenth-century tax rolls are full of this error. It would be convenient to treat the surplus acreage as fraud. Which choice best describes the function of the last sentence in the text as a whole?

The fourth sentence floats fraud as a convenient reading, and the last sentence answers it: the hill parishes paid extra rather than gaining by the error, and nobody defrauds themselves. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 8 of 27

Harbor works poured two thousand years ago from volcanic ash and seawater still stand, while piers cast from modern cement crumble within fifty years. Tests on cores show the ancient mix was not stronger when new; it was weaker. Seawater working through it dissolved the ash and grew interlocking crystals in the voids left behind. The intrusion that opens a modern pier is the process that closed the ancient one.

The final two sentences carry the idea: seawater dissolves the ash and grows crystals that fill the voids, so the same water that ruins a modern pier knits the ancient one together. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 9 of 27

Some shrubs of fire-prone country hold seed that will not sprout in a wet year, a warm year, or a bare year, but sprouts within weeks of a burn. According to the text, what causes the seed of these shrubs to germinate?

The text names the trigger directly: a molecule formed when plant matter smolders, carried to the seed by the first rain. It then rules out heat by reporting that oven-heated seed stays dormant. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 10 of 27

Cavefish populations isolated in separate caves have each lost their eyes, and biologists long read that loss as the simple decay of a structure no longer used. But the gene whose disabling shrinks the eye also widens the taste buds arrayed across the fish's head, and a fish with more taste buds finds more food in the dark. In every population examined, the eye and the taste buds changed together. The blindness, then, ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The text sets up decay as the received reading, then reports that one gene governs both the shrinking eye and the widening taste buds, which help a fish feed in the dark, and that the two always change together. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 11 of 27

An earthwork on the ridge encloses four hectares behind a bank and a ditch. Because the bank stands ten feet above the ditch floor, an archaeologist has concluded that the earthwork was built to be defended, with the ditch stopping an attacker and the bank giving the defenders their height.

Which finding, if true, would most directly undermine the archaeologist's conclusion?
