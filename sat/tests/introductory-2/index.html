Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 1 of 27

A lighthouse keeper trims the wick once an hour through the night, taking a little off each time. A wick left alone burns down, and the flame goes down with it, so a lamp lit at dusk would be weak by morning. Trimming keeps the flame ____ instead: the same at four in the morning as it was at ten at night, which is what a ship out at sea is steering by.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The text says what the trimmed flame does: it is the same at four in the morning as it was at ten at night, hour after hour. "Steady" names that sameness. "Patient" describes a person, and in this text it is the keeper who waits, not the flame. "Brilliant" picks up the brightness the passage mentions, but the point is that the brightness does not change rather than that it is high. "Economical" fits the little taken off each hour, yet the reason given for trimming is the light a ship steers by, not the wick saved.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 2 of 27

Every seven years the parish walks its boundary, and at each stone the warden checks the cutting. Weather rounds off the edge of a letter a little at a time, until a walker has to crouch and trace it with a finger to be sure of it. When the warden finds a stone in that state, a mason is sent out to cut the same name and the same date deeper, so that both stay plain to anyone passing at walking pace.

As used in the text, what does the word "plain" most nearly mean?

The last sentence says what the recutting is for: the name and the date stay readable to somebody passing at walking pace, rather than only to somebody crouching with a finger on the stone. That is legibility. Lack of ornament is a sense the word carries elsewhere, but nothing here says the stone is undecorated; what is at issue is whether the letters can be made out. Blunt speech is a sense that belongs to what a person says, not to cut lettering. And calling the stone ordinary would make it unremarkable, which the parish's seven-year round argues against.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 3 of 27

The stream that feeds the mill pond runs off plowed fields, and it carries fine soil down with it. Moving water holds that soil; still water does not. So the pond is dug wide and the sluice at its far end is opened only a little, which keeps the water in it slow. Given a few hours of that, the silt has time to settle, and the water that reaches the wheel runs clear.

As used in the text, what does the word "settle" most nearly mean?

The sentence is about soil in water, and the pond is built wide and slow so that the soil has somewhere to go. What it does is drop out of the water and stay on the bed of the pond, which is why the water reaching the wheel runs clear. Ending a dispute is a common sense of the word and there is no dispute anywhere in the text. Making a home in a new place is a sense used of people and animals, not of silt. And growing calm would describe the water, which the passage says is slow already, rather than the silt the sentence is about.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 4 of 27

A tide mill ground only while the pond it had filled at high water ran back out to sea. That gave the miller two spells of work a day, and they came an hour later every day, as the tide did. Wexley brought its grain whenever the wheel was turning, and a boy went round the village to say when that would be. An electric motor has driven the mill since 1948 and could turn the stones at any hour. The two spells and the boy's round have both been kept.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

The tide set a limit: the mill could grind only while the pond was running out. The village worked to that limit, bringing grain in the two spells and sending a boy round to announce them. Then the motor removed the limit, and the two spells and the boy's round stayed anyway. The correct answer names those three steps in that order. Nothing in the text recommends the routine to any other mill. Nothing is presented as a puzzle either, since the tide is given in the first sentence rather than withheld and revealed later. And the mill has not declined: it still grinds, on a motor.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 5 of 27

A rope is laid up by twisting its yarns together along their whole length at once, so every yarn has to be stretched out to the full length of the finished rope before the twisting starts. A cable a hundred fathoms long needs a hundred fathoms of clear run. That is why a rope walk is built as a single shed a quarter of a mile long and barely wide enough for two men to pass. The work does not need a large building. It needs a long one.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

A rope has to be stretched to its full length before it can be twisted, so the building that houses the work has to be as long as the longest rope made in it, and the last two sentences say so outright. The opening does say how a rope is laid up, but two clauses on twisting are not a step-by-step account of spinning, and hemp is never mentioned. Nothing in the text asks for a rope walk to be protected. And one rope's strength is never set against another's; the hundred fathoms are there to fix a length, not to rate a cable.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 6 of 27

Glass taken straight from the furnace into the open air is not annealed, and it cracks: the surface cools fast, the middle slowly, and the two pull against each other. Annealing is the slow, controlled cooling that brings the whole thickness of a sheet down to room temperature together. An annealing oven does nothing else, holding a sheet at a falling temperature for hours. The schedule is set by thickness, since a thick sheet needs a longer fall than a thin one. A sheet that skips it splits weeks later under its own weight.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence in the text as a whole?

The first sentence uses the word "annealed" and never says what it means. The second sentence supplies the meaning: slow, controlled cooling that brings the whole thickness down together. Everything after it depends on that definition, since the oven, the schedule and the split sheet are all about how the cooling is managed. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 7 of 27

Text 1

A road verge cut in July loses its flowers before they have set seed, Renard argues, and next summer's flowers are only the seed this summer put on the ground. Cutting late, once September has come, therefore always leaves more flowers the following summer, because by then every plant on the verge has already dropped its seed where it stood.

Text 2

Rautio counted the seed reaching the ground on eleven verges the council had cut in September and on the verges it had cut in July. The council leaves the cut grass where it falls, and under a mat of it a week old the seed heads rot before they open. Her September count came out well below her July count, and on the July verges the cut is short enough that the seed already set falls straight through to the ground. Late cutting, on these verges, left fewer flowers for the next summer rather than more.

Based on the texts, how would Rautio (Text 2) most likely respond to Renard's claim in Text 1 that cutting a road verge late always leaves more flowers the following summer?

Renard's rule is that a late cut always leaves more flowers, because by September every plant has dropped its seed, and next summer's flowers are this summer's seed. Rautio counts the very thing that rule predicts. On the eleven verges she measured, the September cut put less seed on the ground than the July cut did, because the cut grass is left lying and the seed heads rot under it. So the rule fails where she looked, and rejecting it as it stands is what her count supports. Reading her as adding a condition about cutting in October credits her with a comparison she never made; her verges were cut in September and measured against verges cut in July. Reading her as agreeing and supplying a second reason turns her count the wrong way round. And taking her result as an argument against cutting the verges at all goes far past what she measured, which is when to cut and not whether to.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 8 of 27

A hunting kestrel can hold one spot in the air above a field edge for a minute at a time. It does this by facing into the wind and flying forward at the speed the wind is pushing it back, so the ground below it does not move. The bird is working hard the whole time, and going nowhere on purpose. On a still day it cannot do it at all, and hunts from a post instead.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The middle of the text explains the trick and the last sentence gives its cost. The bird flies forward exactly as fast as the wind pushes it back, so on a still day there is nothing to fly against and it hunts from a post. That pairing is the main idea. The text never says how much either way of hunting catches, so calling the post more successful goes past the evidence. Beating the wings hard enough to lift its own weight would describe climbing, not holding still against a wind. And the field edge is where the bird happens to be, not a place the text says the wind is stronger.

A student claims that large-print loans at the Marlow branch more than doubled between 2018 and 2023.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

To show that a figure more than doubled, the support has to give both ends of the change, and 1,020 is more than twice 450. Citing Marlow in both years does that. Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 10 of 27

The clock on the shelf at the lock cottage was wound every Sunday and kept good time. Nell's mother put the kettle on when the top gates began to groan, and supper waited on the last boat of the evening, whether that came at six or at nine. The night a pair of boats came up at midnight, Nell heard her father go out to them and heard him come back in, and nobody in the house thought it worth a word.

Based on the text, what does the narrator suggest about the household at the lock cottage?

The clock keeps good time and nothing in the house happens at an hour it names. The kettle goes on when the top gates groan, supper waits on the last boat whether that comes at six or at nine, and a pair coming up at midnight is not worth a word. Those are hours kept by the traffic. The household cannot be running on the clock, since every event in the passage is timed by a boat instead. Nine o'clock is the late end of a range that also runs to six, not a fixed hour for supper. And one pair of boats on one night says nothing about how the lock's traffic divides between day and night.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 11 of 27

A crustose lichen spreads outward from its center, and a churchyard offers a rare check on how fast, since every headstone carries the year it was cut. A team measured the largest patch on each stone in one churchyard and found diameter rising with the age of the stone. They hypothesize that a patch grows at a steady rate from the day the stone is set, so a diameter can be read back as an age. The rival they must rule out is that the older stones stand in the sheltered corner of the yard.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

The hypothesis has two parts: growth at a steady rate, and a diameter that therefore reports an age. The finding that helps is one where the year cut predicts the size and the position in the yard does not, since position is exactly what the sheltered-corner rival relies on. Same year, same size, anywhere in the yard, does both jobs at once. Repeating that the oldest stones carry the largest patches restates the observation the hypothesis was invented to explain, so it cannot choose between the two readings of it. Which face a lichen prefers is a fact about aspect and settles nothing about age or shelter. And measuring each patch twice tests the ruler rather than the growth.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 12 of 27

Mussels on the harbor wall at Cray grow in a band about a meter deep, with bare stone above it and bare stone below. A mussel out of the water cannot feed, so the top of the band is set by how long the tide leaves it dry. Divers who work the stone below the band find whelks over it all day. A whelk drills a mussel and eats it, and a whelk cannot follow the mussels up into the air, so the lower edge of the band marks ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The text supplies a predator that works the bare wall below the band and cannot leave the water. The bottom of the band is therefore where the whelks have to stop, and the mussels above it survive because a whelk cannot stay up there. A change in the wall's facing is never mentioned, and it would not explain why the whelks are found on exactly the bare part. Temperature is never raised at all. And the highest reach of the tide belongs to the top of the band, which the text has already accounted for with drying time.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 13 of 27

A swarm hangs from a branch while its scouts go out, and every scout that finds a possible cavity comes back and dances for it on the face of the swarm. A scout dances longer for a better site. Other bees follow the longer dances out, look for themselves, and come back to dance in their turn. Poor sites lose their dancers one by one, the best site keeps gaining them, and the swarm lifts off when almost every dancer is dancing for the same place.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The text describes a contest between dances rather than a decision taken by any one bee. A better site draws a longer dance, a longer dance draws more visitors, and those visitors come back dancing, so support builds on the best site until almost all of it is there. Nothing suggests the scouts split the ground between them, and the dancing deliberately sends several bees to the same site. Nothing turns on which scout returns first either, since the swarm waits until the dancing has nearly all gone one way. And the queen hangs in the swarm and is never described choosing anything.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 14 of 27

A watermark is a figure in wire sewn onto the mold, so every sheet lifted on that mold carries it. A mold was worked until it wore out, which could take years, and a mill kept old molds in service beside new ones. An archivist argues that a dated watermark therefore fixes the year the mold was made, and not the year the sheet was.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the archivist's argument?

The archivist's claim bites only if one mold went on making sheets long after the year sewn into it, and sheets with the same watermark used eleven years apart show exactly that. That no two wire figures are alike helps an archivist tell which mold made a sheet, not when the sheet was made. A change of watermark at a change of ownership ties a design to an owner, which leaves the length of a mold's service untouched. And another way of dating a sheet says nothing about what the watermark itself records.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 15 of 27

The tuning shop at the far end of the foundry's casting sheds ____ a set of forks older than anyone now working in it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The subject is the tuning shop, one shop, and the words between it and the blank are a prepositional phrase: at the far end of the foundry's casting sheds. A phrase like that never changes the number of the subject, so the verb has to be singular. The three plural forms are all pulled into agreement with "sheds", the noun standing nearest the blank, and that noun is the object of a preposition and cannot be the subject of anything.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 16 of 27

The night bus keeps a timetable nobody at the depot can quite explain ____ the market square at ten past every hour until three in the morning, and then not again until six.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Both halves of this sentence are complete statements: the bus keeps a timetable nobody can explain, and the bus leaves the square at ten past every hour. A comma is not strong enough to join two statements, and a semicolon is, so the semicolon followed by a subject and its verb is the one form that works. Starting the second half with a participle instead leaves it with no subject and no finite verb, which cannot stand as a sentence after a period and cannot follow a semicolon either.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 17 of 27

A harbor crane spends most of the day going nowhere: the trolley runs in and out along the jib, and ____ turning is left to one small motor at the base.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The blank is a possessive standing before the noun "turning", which is the turning of the crane, and the possessive form of "it" carries no apostrophe. The contraction slips past a reader here because "turning" can be read as a verb, so "it's turning" sounds for a moment like a sentence under way; read on, and the clause is left with no verb of its own. The plural possessive is wrong because the crane it stands for is one crane. And the form with the apostrophe after the s is not English at all, since a pronoun does not take the ending a plural noun takes.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 18 of 27

The locker under the after thwart holds ____. The coxswain checks all three before the season starts.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Three items are listed and none of them contains a comma of its own, so commas separate them and one comma comes before the "and" that closes the list. Each item carries an internal "of", which is what makes the list easy to mispunctuate. A comma after "a tin" breaks that item in two and leaves "of fresh water" attached to nothing. Semicolons separate items only when an item already holds a comma, and none here does. And dropping the comma after the first item runs it into the second, so the list reads as two things rather than three.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 19 of 27

The village choir hires a coach for the county festival every June and pays ____ own way to every other engagement in the year.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The choir is one choir, and the sentence has already treated it as one: it hires, and it pays. The possessive that matches a singular subject is "its". The plural possessive is the pull the sentence sets up, because a choir is made of many people, but the noun is singular and both verbs beside it are singular too. "There" names a place and cannot own anything. And "they're" is "they are", which would leave the sentence with a spare verb and no possessive at all.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 20 of 27

That winter the ewes stayed on the low ground until March. Snow lay in the hollows of the hill into the middle of the month, and the shepherd who kept them ____ down twice a day with hay on his back.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The whole account sits in one past winter: the ewes stayed, and the snow lay. The verb in the blank belongs to that same stretch of past time, so it takes the simple past. The bare plural form agrees with "them", the noun standing nearest the blank, but the subject is the shepherd, one man. The present singular fixes that agreement and leaves the tense in the present, which a winter already described as past has ruled out. And the present perfect reaches from the past up to now, while the winter reported here is over and dated.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 21 of 27

The town's weighbridge ____ has not taken a load since the cattle market closed.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The naming phrase sits between the subject and its verb, and the sentence has already identified the weighbridge as the town's, so the phrase adds information rather than picking out which weighbridge is meant. A supplement like that is fenced with a comma at each end. A comma at the front and none at the back runs the phrase straight into the verb. A comma at the back and none at the front does the same damage at the other end, cutting the subject off from what follows it. And no comma at all reads the phrase as part of the subject, which leaves two noun phrases side by side and nothing to show how the second is meant.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 22 of 27

A flat tile keeps the rain out only where the tile above laps over it, so a roofer sets the courses close together and hangs the tiles on battens a few inches apart. A pantile is molded with an S-curve, and each one laps sideways into its neighbor as well as over the course below. ____ a pantile roof is watertight with much less overlap between one course and the next, and its battens can sit nearly a foot apart.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

The sideways lap is the reason a pantile roof needs less overlap from course to course, and the smaller overlap is why the battens can be set further apart. The transition has to mark that result. "Nevertheless" would set the last sentence against the one before it, when the last sentence follows from it. "Instead" would offer the pantile roof in place of the sideways lap, when the lap is what gives that roof its advantage. And "In the meantime" would place the two sentences at the same moment, which is not the relation between a cause and what it causes.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 23 of 27

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • An orchard ladder has two legs at the front, joined by rungs, and a single pole at the back.
  • Three feet always meet the ground at once, even where the ground is uneven.
  • A four-legged stepladder rocks as soon as one foot lands in a dip.
  • The single back pole slides in among the branches, which brings the ladder close to the fruit.
  • Orchard ladders are made of aluminum now and were once made of wood.

The student wants to explain why an orchard ladder does not rock on uneven ground. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

The goal is the ladder's steadiness, and two notes together give it: three feet always meet the ground, and four feet stop doing so as soon as one of them lands in a dip. The correct choice sets those two notes side by side. What the ladder is made of is true to the notes and has nothing to do with rocking. The back pole reaching in among the branches is a real advantage of the three-legged shape, but it is about getting close to the fruit rather than about standing still. And describing the ladder's parts says what the shape is without saying what the shape does.

Reading and Writing · Module 1 · Question 24 of 27

A hedge left to grow tall goes thin at the bottom, and a gap at the bottom is a gap a sheep will find. To close it, a hedger cuts most of the way through each stem near the ground and bends the stem down along the line of the hedge until it lies almost flat. ____ the stem does not die. A hinge of living wood is left at the cut, and it carries enough sap up from the roots to keep the stem alive and throwing new shoots along its whole bent length.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

A stem cut most of the way through and bent to the ground is a stem that ought to die, and the text says it does not. The transition has to mark that contrast. "Therefore" would make the stem's survival the result of the cutting, when it survives in spite of the cutting. The goal asks why one hand sufficed, so the choice has to join what the dial could tell to what its readers needed. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 1 of 27

A waller does not trust a course because it looks straight. He walks the length of it and presses hard on each stone in turn. A stone that rocks under his hand rests on two points where it needs three, and the weight of every course above will find that gap. He builds on only when the course is firm under his whole weight.

As used in the text, what does the word "firm" most nearly mean?

The test the waller applies is weight: he presses on each stone, and a stone that rocks will let the courses above it settle into the gap. A course that passes that test holds under a load and does not give way when the wall grows above it. A business run by partners is the noun sense of the word, and no company appears here. Fixed in advance and not open to change is the sense the word takes with a date or a price, and nothing in the passage is being agreed. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 2 of 27

A new wheel is built up on the bench from a hub, its spokes, and the curved felloes that make the rim. Before the iron tire goes on, the wheelwright spins the wheel against a fixed pointer and watches the gap. Where the gap opens, a felloe stands proud of the circle. He shaves it back until the rim runs ____ all the way round.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The wheelwright is holding the rim to a line: the pointer marks a circle, and he shaves the wood until the rim keeps to it at every point. The word for a shape held exactly to what it is measured against is true. Accurate describes a measurement or a report that matches the facts, and a rim makes no report. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 3 of 27

The ferry crosses on the hour all day, with one exception. Twice a day the tide has stopped running in and has not yet begun to run out, and for about twenty minutes the water in the narrows is slack. The skipper holds the boat at the pier until then. In slack water he can put the bow where he wants it, and no current will carry the stern round.

As used in the text, what does the word "slack" most nearly mean?

The passage says what the word covers: the tide has stopped coming in and has not started going out, so for twenty minutes the water is not moving, and the skipper crosses because no current will push the stern round. Stillness for a short while is the sense. A rope that is not pulled tight is the loose sense of the word, and nothing here is under tension. Doing less work than the job calls for is the sense the word takes about a person, and the skipper is described as waiting rather than shirking. Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 4 of 27

For sixty years the watch at Bryn Head was kept by eye. Two people in the tower swept the bay with glasses every twenty minutes and wrote down what they saw. The log books show the flaw only when they are read together: a small boat that stayed inshore was written up on some watches and missed on others, because a sweep at fixed times cannot see what passes between them. The station did not close. It now sweeps at times drawn at random.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Three moves, in order: a sweep by eye every twenty minutes, a fault that only shows when sixty years of log books are read together, and a station that stayed open and changed its timings. Only one option carries all three. There are not two rival ways of keeping watch on offer here; there is one way, and one fault found in it. No objections from other stations appear anywhere in the text, and the station is said not to have closed. And no history of a tower is traced, because the text names no year and reaches no abandonment.

Reading and Writing · Module 2 · Question 5 of 27

A hop kiln dries its crop on a slatted floor above a fire, and the wet air has to leave faster than it will rise on its own. The roof carries a cowl, a hood on a spindle with a long vane behind it. The wind pushes the vane round until the hood turns its back to the wind, and the draft across the open mouth draws the wet air up. A fixed opening would face the wind half the time and blow it back down.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Every sentence works toward the last one: the wet air has to be pulled out, the hood turns its back to the wind so that the draft crosses the mouth, and an opening that cannot turn would blow the air back down half the time.
