Test strategy

Pacing and time strategy

The SAT has four modules, and each one is timed on its own. When a module's time runs out you move on, and you cannot go back to it. So there is no single pace for the whole test. Reading and Writing runs at one pace, Math at a slower one, and neither lends time to the other.

The test's shape below is College Board's: how many modules there are, and how long each one runs. We read that from How the SAT Is Structured on 2026-09-07. If that page and this one ever disagree, theirs is right.

What a question is worth

Divide the time by the number of questions. That is your budget for one question.

Both modules in a section are the same length, so one budget covers both. The last row is the same sum read backwards: the question you should be on when half the time has gone, if every question took the same.

The average is the module's time divided by its number of questions.

A budget is not a target

Questions do not all take the same time. A grammar question can go in a few seconds once you see which rule it tests. A question with two passages or a chart usually takes longer. The budget tells you how far over you are, and where the extra time has to come from.

Work fast on the questions you find easy and keep the time you save for the ones that need it. Take the average on every question and you have nothing left when one needs double.

Check the halfway row once. A question or two behind it is nothing. A long way behind it is, and reading faster will not fix that.

Aim to reach the end with two or three minutes left rather than exactly on zero. Those minutes are what you answer your marked questions with.

Watch the gap between the two columns. Reading and Writing comes first, and you spend over an hour at its pace. Math gives you more time per question. Carry the old pace into Math and you rush questions you did not need to rush.

What the timer does

From College Board's description of the testing app:

  • Each module is timed on its own, and the timer shows the time left in the one you are in.
  • You can hide the timer. It comes back with an alert when five minutes are left.
  • Inside a module you can move back and forth between questions until its time runs out.
  • A question menu shows what you skipped and what you marked, and takes you to any of them.
  • You can bookmark a question to come back to it.
  • You can cross out choices you have ruled out, and undo that.
  • Once you leave a module, you cannot go back to it.

Saved time does not carry over. Minutes you save in the first Reading and Writing module are not available in the second. And once you leave a module, any question you skipped in it is gone.

Use the question menu to get back to questions you skipped. Bookmarking a question does not answer it, so pick an answer before you move on.

This site adds a pause button and times each question. The real test has neither. Pause for interruptions. Do not pause to think, because the pressure is the part you came here to practise.

Never leave a multiple choice question blank

College Board's advice: for most students, on a question they are really trying, guessing is better than leaving it blank, and better again once you have ruled out one or two choices.

So pick an answer before you leave a question, then bookmark it. If you never get back to it, you have a one in four chance of being right. Leave it blank and you have none.

It works differently on the typed-answer questions. About a quarter of the real Math section asks you to type the answer instead of choosing one, so there is nothing to eliminate and a guess is far less likely to land. Every Math question on this site is multiple choice, so that quarter of the format is the one thing you cannot practise here.

When to give up on a question

Use a trigger rather than a feeling. A hard question and a slow question feel the same while you are inside one.

  • Reading and Writing: if you cannot say what the question wants, or which two choices are the real contest, after about forty-five seconds, answer it, mark it, and move on.
  • Math: if you have no equation, no diagram, and no route after about a minute, do the same.
  • Either section: a second read of the same passage, a second run at the same algebra, and a third thing typed into a calculator all mean one thing. More time is not going to help.

Keep going when you are one step from the end. The triggers are for when you are not.

The second pass

Do not work your marked questions in number order. Start where you had already done most of the work: two choices gone, or the equation set up and no time to finish it, or a question you think you misread. Leave the ones needing a fresh start until last, and only if the time is there.

Change an answer only when you can say what changed your mind: you found the sentence you missed, you spotted the rule, you found the slip in the arithmetic, you misread what was asked. Do not change one because it feels too easy, or because three answers in a row came out the same letter.

The order the questions come in

Neither section shuffles its questions.

Reading and Writing covers its four topic areas in the same order every module: Craft and Structure, Information and Ideas, Standard English Conventions, Expression of Ideas. Inside an area, questions on similar skills sit together and get harder as you go. Conventions works differently: it also gets harder, but the grammar rules are mixed up, so every question starts with working out which rule applies.

So the questions get harder, then reset at the start of the next area. If a question late in an area beats you, leave it. The next area starts easy again.

Math mixes its topic areas together and gets harder from start to finish. There is no reset. Time you lose early comes out of the hardest questions in the module. Save time in the first third.

When you are behind

Do not speed up on everything. You will lose questions you already had, for a small chance at ones you still will not get.

Change what you spend the time on.

  • Do the quick questions first. In Reading and Writing that usually means the grammar questions, which are short. In Math it usually means the ones with no story to read and no figure.
  • Do not read a passage a third time. Rule out what you can, pick an answer, bookmark it, move on.
  • Leave the charts and the paired passages until you know you have time for them. They usually take longer to read, and a question is worth the same whether you answer it first or last.
  • In the last two minutes, stop working and answer every blank. Then go back to your bookmarks.

This will not save a module you were never going to finish. It turns an unfinished module into a finished one with some guesses in it, which is better than blanks.

The second module, and the break

On test day the second module in each section is easier or harder depending on how the first one went. College Board says you can reach a wide range of section scores either way.

It does not change your pace. Same number of questions, same time, same budget. And do not try to work out during the test which second module you got. You cannot tell reliably, and it would not change what you do next.

The tests on this site are the same for everyone, not adaptive. What you are practising here is the pace.

There is a ten minute break between the two sections. Use it to reset your pace before Math. Do not spend it re-scoring Reading and Writing in your head, because those answers are locked and the attention is needed for Math.

How to practise this here

Take a full test with the timer on, and do not pause it. Then read the results screen. Under each module there is one bar per question, coloured by whether you got it right, wrong, or left it blank, and labelled with its share of the time you spent answering that module's questions. Below that, the same numbers in a table.

Do not ask whether you were fast. Ask what each bar cost the bars after it.

  • A tall bar you got right can still have been too expensive. Look at what happened in the questions that followed it.
  • A tall bar you got wrong is the clearest thing to fix. Those minutes buy back more than a few seconds saved on questions you already had right.
  • A run of short wrong bars at the end of a module usually means you were making up time. The fix is earlier in the module, not there.
  • Short wrong bars spread through the whole module are a different problem, and pacing will not fix it. Study the topic instead.

If the timer is what makes you rush, hide it. It still warns you before the end, here and on the real test.
