Sentence Boundaries

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

Boundary items put a blank between two chunks of text. Classify each side as an independent clause or a fragment, then take the mark that structure allows.

Key takeaways

  • An independent clause has a subject, a finite verb, and no subordinating word in front of it. In a command the subject is an understood you.
  • A clause opening with because or while cannot stand alone, however many verbs it holds.
  • Two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, or a comma before a coordinating conjunction, and a colon where the second delivers what the first promised.
  • A bare comma between two independent clauses is a splice; no mark at all is a fused sentence.
  • A fragment never sits across a period or a semicolon. It attaches to a clause instead.
  • A colon's first demand is a complete clause on its left, and its second is that what follows be the delivery the clause promised.
  • Punctuate by structure, not by where a pause sounds right.

What the SAT may ask

  • Join two independent clauses.
  • Attach a fronted dependent clause.
  • Choose the mark before a list or a rename.
  • Judge whether a long chunk is a clause.
  • Decide when no punctuation belongs.

The rule

Classify each side of the mark first. An independent clause has a subject, a finite verb, and no subordinating word in front of it; in a command the subject is an understood you. A finite verb carries tense: hums, hummed, has hummed. Humming and to hum do not, and anchor nothing. Verb Forms and Tense covers that.

A fragment is anything that could not stand alone: a phrase with no finite verb, or a clause held down by because, while, or until.

Left of the boundary Right of the boundary Legal marks
independent clause independent clause period, semicolon, or comma before a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so)
independent clause the payoff the clause promised colon
fronted dependent clause independent clause comma
independent clause supplementary phrase or clause comma
one subject its second verb no period, semicolon or colon; the comma is the writer's call

A fragment attaches to a clause; it never sits across a period or a semicolon. A phrase or a dependent clause is not an error in itself. It is an error when it is written as a whole sentence.

An -ing phrase has no fixed job. In [Waiting at the gate], she read the notice it describes the subject, so it is a fragment. In [Waiting at the gate] cost her the train it is the subject, cost is the finite verb, and the chunk takes a full stop.

A conjunctive adverb is not a conjunction. However, therefore, then, meanwhile and even so name a relationship and join nothing. A comma beside one is still a comma joining two sentences by itself.

Material the sentence could lift out without losing which thing it means is supplementary, and at a boundary like this it takes a comma. Punctuation Within Sentences carries the other marks a supplement can take.

A colon needs a complete clause on its left. Cover everything after the mark: what is left must be a sentence. The payoff may be a list, a phrase, or a clause. A comma is often legal where a colon fits, so never rule a comma out on principle.

A colon may not cut a verb off from its object. The kit contains: a splint, tape, and shears is wrong. The kit contains three items: a splint, tape, and shears is right.

A clause ending on a preposition can still be complete, because its object may stand in front of it. Read the whole clause, not its last word.

Where two marks are both legal, neither is the answer. English lets the writer decide whether a trailing because clause takes a comma, so a question with one answer cannot turn on that.

The comma can also settle a reading the bare sentence leaves open. She did not go because it rained can be taken either way; She did not go, because it rained can only mean the rain was her reason.

The semicolon has a second job, separating list items that already contain commas. Punctuation Within Sentences covers it.

Why the rule exists

Punctuation records structure, not breathing: a full stop says a thought has ended, a comma that it is still unfolding.

Worked examples

1. Which marks join two independent clauses

The storm passed ____ the crews reopened the road.

Task: Join two independent clauses.

Evidence: The storm passed stands alone, and so does the crews reopened the road.

Answer: A period, a semicolon, and a comma before and are all legal here, so a question with one answer offers exactly one of them. Find which.

Closest trap: The storm passed, however, the crews reopened the road. However names the relationship and cannot join clauses, so the comma is still splicing two sentences by itself. A bare The storm passed, the crews reopened the road splices in the same way, however smoothly it reads.

2. A long chunk can still be a fragment

The department store's original escalator sat idle for a decade. Rusting at every joint while the sales floors around it were remodeled twice.

Task: Decide whether the second chunk can stand across a period.

Evidence: Its own verb is rusting, which is not finite, and were remodeled belongs to the while clause inside it.

Answer: A fragment, so it attaches with a comma: sat idle for a decade, rusting at every joint.

Closest trap: reading were remodeled as the chunk's own verb. Length is not completeness.

Special cases

One subject, two verbs

The night crew [logs each oven temperature] and [files the sheet by dawn] is one clause, not two, so no period, semicolon or colon may cut it. Read from and onward and ask whether a subject is there: files the sheet by dawn has none.

Common traps

Splices, fused clauses, and stranded fragments

Trap Correction
comma splice by ear Two full sentences need a period, a semicolon, a coordinating conjunction, or a colon; however and then are none of those
fused clauses A missing mark is no join at all
stranded dependent clause A because clause cannot stand across a full stop
missing finite verb Find the chunk's own verb, not one inside a smaller clause
colon after a fragment Cover what follows the colon; the rest must be a sentence

Also on this page: an unmarked opener or unmarked supplement, a comma after the conjunction rather than before it, and a mark that cannot introduce a list.

The method: Classify both sides

  1. Find the clauses and decide which can stand alone. Check where the choices put the mark first, since two positions mean two boundaries. An independent clause has a subject, a finite verb, and no subordinating word in front of it, the subject being an understood you in a command. Anything else is a fragment at this boundary.
  2. Name the relationship and apply the joining table. Two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, a comma before a coordinating conjunction, or a colon on the table's terms. A fragment attaches with a comma or a colon, or with no mark where the clause needed it to finish or where a comma would change the meaning. One subject running two verbs is one clause, so no period, semicolon or colon may cut it.
  3. Match the choices to that pattern. At a boundary a semicolon does a period's work, not a comma's, so both its sides must stand alone. Where two choices carry the same mark in different places, position decides.
  4. Never choose by pause-feel; reread the whole sentence with the survivor in place. A comma splice sounds fine aloud and is still wrong.

The rink's resurfacing machine glides over the ice between morning ____ lays down a thin sheet of water that freezes into a smooth new surface.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: comma splice by ear. Each side is a complete sentence, and a comma alone cannot join two of them; it needs a coordinating conjunction beside it.
Correct. Each side has a subject and a finite verb, so this is two independent clauses, and a period is one of the legal joins for that pattern. It is the only legal join offered here.
Trap: fused clauses. Two complete sentences run together with no mark at all, so nothing shows the reader where the first thought ended.
Trap: comma splice by ear. Then is an adverb, not a coordinating conjunction, so the comma is still joining two complete sentences by itself.

The method, applied

Step 1: The rink's resurfacing machine glides over the ice between morning sessions stands alone, and so does it lays down a thin sheet of water. Two independent clauses. Step 2: a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a conjunction. Step 3: only sessions. It is one of those. Step 4: the commas sound smoother, which is not a reason.

Practice 2

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Introductory

Because the airfield's grass strip has no ____ mail plane that serves the valley lands only in daylight.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The opening chunk is a dependent clause and cannot stand alone, and a comma is the mark that attaches a fronted dependent clause to its sentence.
Trap: stranded dependent clause. A period demands a complete sentence on each side, and a Because clause promises a payoff that never comes.
Trap: stranded dependent clause. A semicolon makes the same demand a period makes, and the left side still cannot meet it.
Trap: unmarked opener. A fronted dependent clause takes a comma, and with nothing there has no lights the mail plane reads as one phrase.

The method, applied

Step 1: Because the airfield's grass strip has no lights leaves a listener waiting, so it is a fragment. The right side stands alone. Step 2: a fragment cannot sit across a period or a semicolon, and a fronted dependent clause takes a comma. Step 3: only lights, the offers one. Step 4: reread, and the setup hands off cleanly.

Practice 3

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Standard

The municipal fountain is drained in the first week of ____ sits dry under a plywood cover until all danger of a hard freeze has passed.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: comma splice by ear. The second clause opens with it, so the two feel like one continuing thought, but each side stands alone and a bare comma cannot join them.
Trap: fused clauses. With no mark between the clauses, the first week of November it sits dry runs two sentences into one string.
Correct. Both sides are independent clauses, and a semicolon is a legal join for that pattern. A pronoun subject is still a subject.
Trap: comma splice by ear. Afterward says when the second clause happens, but it cannot join clauses, so the comma is still splicing two complete sentences.

The method, applied

Step 1: The municipal fountain is drained in the first week of November stands alone. The right side opens with a pronoun, but it is a subject and sits is a finite verb, so it stands alone too. Step 2: two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction. Step 3: only November; it is one of those, and afterward is an adverb, not a conjunction. Step 4: the bare comma is the one your ear likes, and sense is not structure.

Practice 4

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Standard

The bowling alley's aging pin-setting machines jam at least once every league ____ owner keeps a bin of salvaged parts behind the front counter for exactly that reason.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: comma splice by ear. The clauses are linked as cause and consequence, which makes the bare comma sound sufficient, but a link in meaning is not a join in structure.
Trap: comma after the conjunction. The comma belongs between the clauses, in front of and. Parked after it, the comma cuts and off from the clause it introduces.
Trap: fused clauses. Two independent clauses never touch with no mark between them, and every league night the owner keeps a bin reads as one clause.
Correct. Both sides are independent clauses, and a comma plus the coordinating conjunction and is one of the legal joins for that pattern.

The method, applied

Step 1: The bowling alley's aging pin-setting machines jam at least once every league night stands alone, and so does the owner keeps a bin of salvaged parts behind the front counter. Step 2: period, semicolon, or comma plus a coordinating conjunction. Step 3: night, and the is that join. night, the has lost the conjunction, night and, the puts the comma on the wrong side of and, and the unmarked choice joins nothing. Step 4: reread with the survivor in place.

Practice 5

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Advanced

Restorers traced the chapel window's slow sag to its lead ____ lattice of soft metal channels that has carried the full weight of the glass and every push of the wind since the building went up.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Everything after the blank renames the cames and has no finite verb of its own. The sentence could lift it out and still say which thing is meant, so it is supplementary and takes a comma.
Trap: missing finite verb. The chunk's head is the bare noun lattice, and has carried is locked inside the that clause, so the period strands a fragment.
Trap: missing finite verb. Strip the that clause away and a lattice of soft metal channels is left, a noun phrase the semicolon cannot accept.
Trap: unmarked supplement. The rename needs a comma in front of it, and without one its lead cames a lattice reads as a single tangled phrase.

The method, applied

Step 1: Restorers traced the chapel window's slow sag to its lead cames stands alone. The right side is long and contains has carried, so it feels independent; classify it anyway. Its head is the noun lattice, and has carried belongs to the that clause, so nothing there asserts anything. Fragment. Step 2: a fragment cannot sit across a period or a semicolon, and this one is liftable and not needed to say which thing is meant, so it is supplementary and takes a comma. Step 3: only cames, a offers that comma. Step 4: reread, one clause and then a long rename.

Practice 6

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Advanced

For a hundred years the mill's recipe for good paper never varied, and every new hand at the pulping vat learned on the first morning exactly which three ingredients to ____ sorted by weave, lime to loosen their fibers, and clean river water to carry the pulp to the molds.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: wrong mark before a list. A semicolon cannot introduce a list, and this list is no sentence either: sorted is a participle, and to loosen and to carry never finish.
Trap: missing finite verb. The period strands the whole list as a sentence, and sorted, to loosen, and to carry are all unfinished, so nothing in it asserts anything.
Correct. Cover everything after the mark and a complete sentence is left, even though it ends on for, whose object which three ingredients stands in front of it. That clause promised three ingredients it has not named, which is the colon's job.
Trap: colon after a fragment. Stopping at look leaves the clause one word short, since here look reaches what it seeks through for, so there is nothing yet for a colon to deliver on.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices put the mark in two places, so classify the left side twice. Read as far as look for and it is finished; stop at look and it is one word short, since look reaches its object through for. The right side is a participle and two infinitives, a fragment either way. Step 2: no fragment sits across a period or a semicolon, and a colon may follow a complete clause that promised something, which this one has: three ingredients, not yet named. Step 3: the full stops go, and position separates the two colons. Step 4: reread, a finished sentence and then the three things it held back.
