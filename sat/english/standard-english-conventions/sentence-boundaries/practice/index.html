Question

  • Introductory

The marching band still rehearses on the school's one set of wooden ____ the shop class rebuilds every few summers out of lumber a mill on the county road donates.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and which commits everything after it to leaning on a noun somewhere else. The chunk has a subject and a finite verb and is a fragment anyway.
A colon delivers something the clause in front of it has held out, and what follows may be a phrase, a list, or a clause. What it may not be is a chunk still reaching backward: which is looking for the noun it belongs to, so the sentence is not delivering anything here, it is continuing.
Correct. The chunk is a relative clause, which makes it a fragment however finished its verb, and of the four marks here the comma is the one that attaches a fragment to the clause it hangs on.
The period strands the relative clause as a sentence of its own. Rebuilds is a finite verb, but which has already committed the whole clause to a supporting role, so a listener is left waiting for what it supports.

Why this answer

Classify the chunk after the blank rather than weighing it. Which the shop class rebuilds every few summers has a subject and a finite verb, and it is still a fragment, because which is the word that hands the whole clause back to a noun in the sentence it attaches to. A fragment can never sit across a period or a semicolon, and a colon introduces a delivery rather than a chunk still reaching backward for its noun. The comma is the mark left standing, and attaching is what it does.

Question

  • Introductory

The dive team spent a week inspecting the reservoir's intake ____ find that the crack everyone had blamed for the drop was a shadow on a thirty-year-old photograph.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Only to find is an infinitive phrase rather than a clause, so it attaches to the clause in front of it, and the comma is what marks the turn the phrase makes.
A period demands a complete sentence on each side. Nothing in the chunk is a finite verb: to find never finishes, and was belongs to the that clause buried inside it.
A semicolon makes exactly the demand a period makes, so it strands exactly the same fragment. A semicolon is not a softer full stop.
A coordinating conjunction joins things of a kind: two clauses, two predicates, two phrases doing the same work. What stands to the right of and here is an infinitive phrase and what stands to the left is a finished predicate, so there is no pair for it to join.

Why this answer

Read the chunk after the blank on its own and a listener waits: only to find that the crack everyone had blamed for the drop was a shadow has no finite verb of its own, since to find is an infinitive and was is locked inside the that clause. Fragment, so both full stops strand it, and the comma-plus-and join has no second clause to reach for. The plain comma is what attaches a phrase that turns the sentence against what it had just led you to expect.

Question

  • Introductory

The vendors do not unstrap the canvas ____ the market inspector has walked the whole row and initialed the morning sheet.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The period strands the until clause as a sentence. It has a subject and a finite verb, and until still commits the whole clause to a supporting role, so a listener is left waiting for what happens then.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and a clause opening with until cannot supply one however many verbs it carries.
A colon delivers what the clause in front of it promised, and what follows one has to be the delivery. This clause promises nothing and the chunk is not a delivery: it is a time limit on the verb unstrap, which is a modifier rather than an announcement.
Correct. Not ... until is one statement rather than two: the clause says when the unstrapping starts, so it belongs inside what the sentence denies and runs on with no mark at all.

Why this answer

Left side: The vendors do not unstrap the canvas awnings stands alone. Right side: until the market inspector has walked the whole row has a subject and a finite verb, and until subordinates it, so neither full stop may hold it, and a colon cannot introduce a chunk that is modifying the verb rather than delivering on a promise. That leaves the sentence running straight through, which is what not ... until wants: the denial and the time limit are one statement.

Question

  • Introductory

The corner store calls its bread order in before four every ____ unloads the van at the curb by seven.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A colon delivers what the clause in front of it promised. Nothing has been promised here, and what follows the mark is a bare verb continuing the same clause rather than a delivery of any kind.
Correct. Calls and unloads are two verbs sharing the subject the corner store, which makes this one clause with a compound predicate rather than two clauses, so none of the marks offered here belongs at the boundary.
The period strands And unloads the van at the curb by seven as a sentence, and it has no subject to be a sentence about.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side. What stands to its right is a bare predicate looking for the subject it left behind.

Why this answer

Look for a subject on each side of the blank. The left has one, the corner store; the right has only unloads, so the two verbs share a single subject and the sentence is one clause with two predicates rather than two clauses. Neither full stop may cut a clause in half, and a colon has nothing to deliver, so of the four marks on offer none belongs at the boundary and the sentence runs straight through the and.

Question

  • Introductory

By August the guards had come to trust the radio less than they trusted a single ____ plain red flag that meant the swim area was closed.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A period demands a sentence on each side, and The plain red flag that meant the swim area was closed is a noun phrase: meant belongs to the that clause inside it, so nothing in the chunk asserts anything.
A semicolon separates two things that could each stand alone. It cannot introduce anything, and what stands to its right here is still a noun phrase.
With no mark at all the flag runs into the clause in front of it, and they trusted a single signal the plain red flag reads as one tangled phrase before it collapses.
Correct. The clause on the left stands alone and has promised something, a single signal, that it has not yet named, and a colon is the mark that delivers on a clause's promise.

Why this answer

Cover everything after the blank and read what is left: By August the guards had come to trust the radio less than they trusted a single signal is a complete sentence, and a single signal is a promise it has not paid off, sitting right against the mark. That is the colon's contract, and it does not care what shape the payoff takes: a single noun phrase counts as much as a list does. Both full stops demand a complete sentence on the right and get a noun phrase instead.

Question

  • Introductory

Every October the refuge bases its whole seal ____ the animals hauled out along the gravel bar below the ranger station and the shingle spit that runs north from it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A period strands a pile of prepositional phrases as a sentence. The chunk names two stretches of shore and a landmark apiece, and it still has no subject and no finite verb: runs belongs to the that clause inside it.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and length is not completeness.
Correct. Bases cannot finish without saying what the count is based on, so the whole chunk is the verb's own complement rather than an addition to it, and the sentence runs straight through.
The comma cuts bases off from the only thing that completes it. Read the left side alone and it stops mid-thought, which is the test: what a verb requires is never fenced off from that verb.

Why this answer

Read the chunk after the blank alone: on the animals hauled out along the gravel bar below the ranger station and the shingle spit that runs north from it is a stack of prepositional phrases with no subject and no finite verb of its own, since runs belongs to the that clause. Fragment, so neither full stop may hold it. Then ask whether the sentence could lift it out, and it could not: bases is waiting on it. Length is the whole of the disguise here, and classification weighs nothing.

Question

  • Standard

The county salts the ridge road before anything else every ____ the first hard freeze, the mill lane and the two bridge approaches go onto the same run.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. After the first hard freeze is a preposition and its object, not a clause, so what follows the boundary is a whole second sentence with a phrase fronted onto it, and a semicolon is a legal join for two clauses this closely related.
A comma joining two complete sentences is a splice, and the opening phrase disguises it: read after the first hard freeze as attached to the first clause and the second is left with a comma for a join, read it as opening the second and the comma is joining the two clauses on its own.
With no mark the sentence reads before anything else every winter after the first hard freeze, one long stretch of time, until go arrives with a subject that has nowhere to attach.
The ordering is already in the sentence, in after the first hard freeze, so then is adding emphasis rather than a join. It is an adverb either way, and no adverb can license a comma standing between two complete sentences.

Why this answer

The second chunk opens with after, which is the word that makes it look dependent, so check what follows it. After the first hard freeze is a preposition plus its noun-phrase object, with no subject and no verb, which makes it a fronted phrase rather than a clause, and the words behind it, the mill lane and the two bridge approaches go onto the same run, are a complete sentence. Two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, and the semicolon is the only one offered.

Question

  • Standard

The practice keeps one veterinarian on call until ____ four hours after that belong to an emergency clinic two towns over.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A bare comma joining two complete sentences is a splice. The second clause opens with a noun phrase that leans back on the first, which is what makes the comma sound sufficient.
Correct. Both sides are independent clauses, and a comma plus a coordinating conjunction is one of the legal joins for that pattern. But is a coordinating conjunction, and the contrast it marks is the one the sentence is drawing.
With no mark at all, until midnight the four hours after that runs together, and the reader finds the boundary by tripping over it.
However is an adverb wearing a conjunction's coat: it names the contrast and has no power to join clauses, so the comma is still splicing two complete sentences on its own.

Why this answer

Both sides stand alone. The practice keeps one veterinarian on call until midnight has a subject and a finite verb, and so does the four hours after that belong to an emergency clinic. Two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, or a comma before a coordinating conjunction, and the only one of those three on offer here is the comma before but. However names the same contrast and cannot do the joining, which is the whole difference between a conjunction and an adverb.

Question

  • Standard

The inspection found the leak in ten feet of pipe under the ____ in the tower's riveted seams, which the town had been repainting every spring on the strength of a guess.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The period strands Not in the tower's riveted seams as a sentence. It has no subject and no finite verb of its own; had been repainting belongs to the which clause hanging off it.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and a contrast built out of a preposition and a noun cannot supply one.
A contrasting not phrase is supplementary, and a supplement attaches to the clause with a comma rather than with no mark at all. Run straight on, the correction reads as part of what it corrects: the leak in ten feet of pipe under the sidewalk not in the tower's riveted seams.
Correct. The chunk corrects the phrase in front of it and carries no verb of its own, so it attaches with a comma, which is also the mark that separates a correction from the thing corrected.

Why this answer

The chunk after the blank is built from a preposition and a noun, not in the tower's riveted seams, with its only finite verb locked inside the which clause that follows. Fragment, so both full stops strand it. It is also set against the phrase in front of it rather than continuing it, and a contrast of that kind is marked off, which is what rules out running the two together.

Question

  • Standard

The swim coach has run the same warm-up set since the first week of ____ decision the sprinters on the team have been arguing with all winter.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The chunk renames what the whole clause just reported, the coach's keeping to one set, and a rename carries no verb of its own, so it attaches with a comma.
A rename is supplementary, and a supplement attaches to the clause with a comma, never with no mark. With none, since the first week of November a decision the sprinters on the team have been arguing with runs together as one stretch of time and thing.
The period strands A decision the sprinters on the team have been arguing with all winter as a sentence. Its head is the bare noun decision; have been arguing belongs to the relative clause hanging off it.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side and gets a noun phrase, which is the same failure the period has and not a milder one.

Why this answer

The chunk after the blank renames nothing in the sentence: there is no noun in front of it that is a decision. What it renames is the whole clause, the fact that the coach has kept to one set, and that is a shape worth recognizing, because a reader hunting for the noun it belongs to will not find one. It is still a noun phrase with no finite verb, so both full stops strand it, and what it is doing is commenting on the whole clause rather than picking out any noun in it, which is one of the shapes the comma is for.

Question

  • Standard

Every feed wire under the club's layout gets back to the transformer by way ____ terminal strip, a rotary switch, and a fuse no bigger than a thumbnail.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A colon has one demand, a complete sentence on its left, and gets back to the transformer by way of is not one: of is left holding nothing. Cover the list and the support collapses.
Correct. The three items are the object of by way of, which makes them part of the clause rather than an elaboration of it, and nothing stands between a preposition and its object.
A comma here separates of from the only thing that finishes it. A list is punctuated inside itself, not fenced off from the word that governs it.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side and gets a stranded preposition on one and a list on the other.

Why this answer

The test for a colon is mechanical: cover everything after the mark and read what is left. Every feed wire under the club's layout gets back to the transformer by way of is not a sentence, because of is still waiting for its object. English does leave prepositions at the end of a clause, but only where something has been moved out from under them or where the word has a use of its own without an object, as through and past do; by way of has neither here. So no colon may follow it, and neither may anything else: the list is not an elaboration of the clause but the clause's own object, and the sentence runs straight on to it.

Question

  • Standard

The volunteers open the ticket window an hour before the ____ the projectionist has not called by then, someone walks up to the booth to see whether the reel is threaded.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A splice whichever way it is read. Attach the if clause to the first sentence and the second is left joined by a comma alone; attach it to the second and the first two are.
With no mark, an hour before the show if the projectionist has not called by then reads as one condition on the opening, and then someone walks up to the booth arrives with no join at all.
Correct. The if clause belongs to the sentence that follows it, which the comma after by then already shows, so the boundary in front of it falls between two complete sentences and takes a full stop.
Then is an adverb, not a conjunction, so the comma is still joining two complete sentences on its own.

Why this answer

Three chunks, and the middle one decides the boundary. If the projectionist has not called by then is a dependent clause, and the comma after it hands it forward to someone walks up to the booth, so the sentence is two complete statements with a condition fronted onto the second. Two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, and only the period is offered.

Question

  • Standard

The city patches the ramps at the skate park twice a ____ the plywood transitions on the north side, where water stands under the deck all winter.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The chunk is a noun phrase, the plywood transitions on the north side, with a relative clause hanging off it. A noun phrase anchors no clause, so it attaches to the clause in front of it, and the comma marks where the claim stops and the narrowing begins.
The period strands a noun phrase as a sentence. Stands is inside the where clause and belongs to it, so nothing in the chunk asserts anything.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side. What stands to its right is a phrase, however far it runs.
The especially phrase narrows what came before and is supplementary, so it attaches to the clause with a comma and not with no mark. Without one, twice a year especially the plywood transitions reads as one stretch of the sentence.

Why this answer

Especially can introduce a phrase or a whole clause, so it settles nothing on its own. Read what follows it: the plywood transitions on the north side, where water stands under the deck all winter is a noun phrase with a relative clause attached, and the only finite verb in it, stands, belongs to that relative clause. A noun phrase anchors no clause of its own, and this one narrows a claim the sentence has already made in full, which makes it supplementary. A supplement cannot stand across a period or a semicolon, so the comma is the only mark left that fits.

Question

  • Advanced

The marina replaced every cleat on the outer dock last ____ of the old ones frozen to bolts that had not turned in ten winters.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The chunk has a subject, half of the old ones, and it still cannot stand alone: frozen is a participle describing them rather than a finite verb asserting anything about them.
A semicolon makes a period's demand. Read the chunk alone and a listener waits, because naming a state the cleats were in is not the same as claiming it.
With no mark the sentence reads replaced every cleat on the outer dock last spring half of the old ones, and the reader has to find the boundary without help.
Correct. The chunk carries a subject of its own and no finite verb, which makes it a fragment however complete it looks, and this one is supplementary, so it attaches with a comma. Which mark a fragment takes depends on what it is doing; a supplement takes this one.

Why this answer

The chunk after the blank has a subject, which is usually the thing that makes a chunk a clause, and it is a fragment anyway: frozen is a participle, not a finite verb, so nothing in the chunk is asserted. A because or until clause carries a subject too, but it announces itself with the word in front of it; this one carries a subject with nothing to explain it away, which is what makes it the hardest fragment to catch. It cannot sit across a full stop, and it comments on the clause as a whole rather than completing any part of it, so the comma attaches it.

Question

  • Advanced

Bring a flashlight with a red lens to the club's star ____ light wrecks a dark-adapted eye for a good twenty minutes.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A comma joining two complete sentences is a splice, and an instruction followed by its reason is where the splice sounds most natural of all.
Correct. An imperative shows no subject because English leaves the you understood, and it is a complete sentence anyway, so the boundary falls between two independent clauses.
With no mark, star party white light wrecks a dark-adapted eye runs two sentences into one string.
Then is an adverb, not a conjunction. It orders the two clauses and does nothing to license the comma joining them.

Why this answer

The left side shows no subject on the page, which is what makes it look like a fragment. An imperative is a complete sentence anyway: the subject is understood rather than missing, and the test is that the chunk read alone leaves a listener waiting for nothing. Both sides stand alone, so the boundary takes a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, and the period is the only one offered.

Question

  • Advanced

The volunteers counted the washed-out steps on the lower ____ trail crew leader photographed each one before the detour was staked.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The trap, and a good one: unmarked, the washed-out steps on the lower switchback the trail crew leader photographed reads as one long noun phrase, the steps that somebody photographed, so the sentence seems to be still assembling its object. It collapses two words later, at each one, because that reading has already given photographed its object and there is no room for a second.
Once the boundary is found, a bare comma is a splice: two complete sentences with nothing but a comma between them.
Correct. Counted anchors one clause and photographed anchors another, each with a subject of its own, and a semicolon is a legal join for two clauses reporting two halves of one survey.
Meanwhile is an adverb, not a conjunction, so it cannot license the comma, and two complete sentences are still joined by a comma alone.

Why this answer

The boundary is disguised rather than absent. With no mark, a reader building the sentence left to right takes the trail crew leader photographed as a relative clause hanging on steps and never notices that a clause has ended, until each one arrives with nowhere to sit. Classify by counting finite verbs with subjects instead of reading forward: counted has one and photographed has another, so there are two independent clauses here, and the semicolon is the only legal join on offer.

Question

  • Advanced

The crew mows the firebreak in June, and a contractor burns it in strips in ____ two ponds at the south end are left alone all year for the sake of the marsh birds nesting in the sedge along their edges.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The sentence has already joined two clauses correctly with and, which is what makes a second comma feel like more of the same. It is not: a comma joins clauses only with a coordinating conjunction beside it, and the second one never comes.
With no mark, burns it in strips in October the two ponds at the south end runs the third clause into the second.
Then is an adverb, not a conjunction, so the comma is still joining two complete sentences on its own.
Correct. Three chunks, three independent clauses. The first two are already joined by a comma plus and, and the third needs a join of its own; the semicolon is the one on offer.

Why this answer

Count the clauses before choosing a mark. The crew mows the firebreak in June, a contractor burns it in strips in October, and the two ponds at the south end are left alone all year each have a subject and a finite verb. The first join is already made and made legally, which is exactly what stops a reader classifying any further. The second boundary needs a legal join of its own, and of the four on offer only the semicolon is one.

Question

  • Advanced

The last pickup at the blue box on Center Street is at ____ a letter into it before then means leaving the office while the mail room is still sorting.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Getting a letter into it before then is the subject of means rather than a participle hanging off the first clause, so the chunk is a complete sentence and the boundary takes a full stop.
The comma invites the reading the chunk is disguised as, a trailing -ing phrase describing the clause in front of it. Read as far as means and the chunk turns out to have a finite verb of its own, which makes the comma a splice.
With no mark the sentence reads is at four getting a letter into it, one clause, until means arrives with no subject left over for it.
Then is an adverb, not a conjunction. The comma is still joining two complete sentences by itself.

Why this answer

An -ing form at the head of a chunk can be a participle describing something, or it can be the subject; the sentence decides, not the form. Look for the finite verb before deciding. Means is one, and what it takes as its subject is the whole -ing phrase in front of it, so the chunk is a clause. Two independent clauses, and the period is the only full stop offered.

Question

  • Advanced

The park bolted the overlook's new guardrail into bare rock rather than setting it in concrete for one ____ wall behind it sheds a foot of sandstone in a wet decade, and a concrete footing would go over the edge with it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A comma cannot join two complete sentences on its own, and the fact that the second one delivers exactly what the first promised does not change what a comma is.
With no mark, for one reason the wall behind it sheds a foot of sandstone reads as one clause until it collapses.
Correct. The clause on the left stands alone and promises something, one reason, and the clause on the right is that reason. What follows a colon may be a list, a phrase, or a complete sentence of its own.
Then is an adverb, not a conjunction, so the comma is still splicing two complete sentences.

Why this answer

Cover everything after the blank: The park bolted the overlook's new guardrail into bare rock rather than setting it in concrete for one reason is a complete sentence, and one reason is a promise it has not paid off. That is the colon's contract, and the contract says nothing about the shape of the payoff, so a whole clause qualifies as readily as a list does. The two comma choices splice, and no mark at all fuses.

Question

  • Advanced

Not once in forty years had the orchard's frost fans failed on a night they were ____ crew still walked the rows with a thermometer every clear night in April.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A comma joining two complete sentences is a splice, and the inverted opening helps it along by making the first clause feel like a setup that has not finished.
Correct. Both sides are independent clauses, and a semicolon is a legal join for two clauses this closely related.
With no mark the two sentences run together and the reader is left to find the boundary.
Even so is an adverb phrase. It marks the contrast the sentence is drawing and has no power to join clauses, so the comma is still splicing on its own.

Why this answer

The left side puts its auxiliary in front of its subject, had the orchard's frost fans failed, which is what English does after a fronted negative and which reads like the opening of a question or the first half of something longer. It is neither: the clause is an ordinary main clause in an unusual order, and it stands alone. Two independent clauses, and the semicolon is the only legal join offered.
