Possessives and Contractions: practice session

20 questions on Possessives and Contractions, in random order. Unlike the lesson, the difficulty of each question stays hidden until you open the solution, so you meet them the way test day hands them to you: with no label. There is no timer and no score. Answer a question, then press Show solution whenever you want to see how you did and why.

Question

  • Introductory

Switch the safelight on before you open the box of paper, and set ____ enlarger timer to the exposure you worked out on the test strip.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The timer belongs to the person being addressed, and this is the possessive determiner that sits in front of the thing owned, with no apostrophe anywhere.
Expand it: set you are enlarger timer is wreckage, so the contraction cannot be doing the work of a possessive.
The same expansion written out, which is what that collapse looks like on the page. If the two words fail, the contraction fails with them.
The standalone possessive. It can close a clause, as in the timer is yours, but it never stands directly in front of the noun it owns.

Why this answer

Four forms that differ by an apostrophe or a space, which is the axis: what would an apostrophe be doing here? Expanding the contraction gives set you are enlarger timer, which collapses, and the written-out version fails the same way. Ownership survives, and because a noun follows the blank the sentence wants the determiner rather than the standalone form.

Question

  • Introductory

The yew maze at the end of the garden is older than the house behind it, and ____ never once been clipped back to the ground.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A possessive determiner has to be followed by the thing owned, and what follows the blank is a verb phrase rather than a noun.
Not a word under any reading. Its, hers, theirs, ours, and yours are spelled without an apostrophe, so there is no plural-owner version of this one to write.
Correct. Expand it and the sentence survives: it has never once been clipped back to the ground, with the apostrophe standing where the ha of has was dropped.
The other expansion, and the one the sentence rules out: is cannot take been, so the clause never reaches a verb it can use.

Why this answer

Run the expansion test, but run it on both words this apostrophe can hide. It is never once been clipped back fails, which is what makes the written-out version wrong as well; it has never once been clipped back is exactly what the sentence means. Ownership never comes into it: no noun follows the blank for a possessive to own, and no noun before it that one could stand in for.

Question

  • Introductory

The volunteer who runs the toy library keeps two jigsaw boxes of her own on the shelf by the door, and ____ are the ones that go home with her each week.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The apostrophe is the whole error. Hers, like his, its, theirs, ours, and yours, is possessive before anything is added to it.
Correct. The standalone possessive, already possessive without any marking, and the only one of the four that can head the subject of this clause.
Either the determiner, which must be followed by the noun it owns (her puzzles), or the object form, which cannot be the subject of a finite clause. Neither can stand where this blank does.
Expand it both ways: she is are the ones and she has are the ones both collapse, so a contraction is standing where a possessive is wanted.

Why this answer

Name the axis first: one pronoun in four forms, so the question is whether an apostrophe belongs and, if it does, what it is contracting. Neither she is nor she has leaves a sentence, which settles the contraction. That leaves ownership, and the blank heads a subject with no noun after it, which only the standalone possessive can do. It carries no apostrophe at all.

Question

  • Introductory

A folding bicycle was left on the late bus, and the station's lost and found office has tagged it with the ____ registration number.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A plain plural, which marks no ownership at all, and the sentence names one bus rather than several.
Hands a single registration number to several owners, where only one bus has been mentioned.
Doubles a letter inside the owner. The owner is spelled bus, and the marking is added after the complete owner rather than built into it.
Correct. One bus owns the number, and a singular owner takes apostrophe plus s; a word already ending in -s is marked the same way, which is what the major style guides give for a common noun like this one.

Why this answer

No expansion fits, since the bus is registration number and the bus has registration number are both nonsense, so the apostrophe is marking ownership. Find the owner and count it: one bus, named in the first half of the sentence. A singular owner takes apostrophe plus s, and a word that already ends in -s is no exception, because the marking goes after the complete owner rather than inside it. That is the difference from a plural: buses already ends in the -s that made it plural, so it takes the apostrophe alone.

Question

  • Introductory

At the harvest fair the ____ are lifted onto the scales one after another, and the heaviest of them wins the red ribbon.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Expand it first: the pumpkin is are lifted and the pumpkin has are lifted both collapse, so no contraction is hiding here. As a possessive it marks one owner, where the sentence lifts them one after another and gives them nothing to own.
Correct. Nothing in the sentence belongs to them, so a plain plural is all that is wanted, and a plain plural takes no apostrophe.
Gets the number right and still marks an ownership the sentence does not have: no noun anywhere in it belongs to the pumpkins, so there is nothing for a possessive to stand in front of or to stand in for.
Singular, so it disagrees with are lifted and with one after another.

Why this answer

The four spellings differ in number and in whether an apostrophe appears, so ask what would be owned. The answer is nothing: the pumpkins are the things being weighed, and no noun in the sentence belongs to them. With no ownership to mark, the apostrophe has no work to do, and the plural verb settles the rest.

Question

  • Introductory

Two bales of straw are still standing in the ice house, and the carters who packed the blocks say the bales are ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Expand it both ways: the bales are there is and the bales are there has both collapse. And there is not a possessive form at all, so it cannot say the bales belong to anybody.
The right pronoun carrying a mark it can never take. Theirs, like his, its, hers, ours, and yours, is possessive before anything is added to it.
Correct. The standalone possessive, which is what a clause needs once the owners are named and the thing owned has already been said.
The determiner form, which must be followed by the noun it owns: their bales, never the bales are their.

Why this answer

Two of these are homophones of the answer rather than spellings of it, so start with the expansion: there is leaves the clause with two verbs and no sense. Ownership survives, and the owners are the carters. Nothing follows the blank, which rules out the determiner, and the standalone possessive is spelled without an apostrophe, which rules out the marked form.

Question

  • Standard

The two rangers call the station at six each evening to report where they have camped and whether ____ still within radio range.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Expand it and the clause is complete: whether they are still within radio range, with the apostrophe standing where the a of are was dropped.
A determiner with no noun after it, so the clause after whether never reaches a subject or a verb.
The place word, and the clause is still short of a verb: whether there still within radio range says nothing.
The standalone possessive, which would have to stand in for a noun phrase, and the clause has no verb either way.

Why this answer

The clause after whether has no verb in it yet, which is the giveaway: only one of these spellings brings one. The expansion fits exactly, whether they are still within radio range. The other three are a determiner, a place word, and a standalone possessive, and none of them can supply the subject and verb the clause is missing.

Question

  • Standard

Every week the card on each table says the same thing. ____ keep the scoring sheets face down until the last team has handed one in.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

An imperative let still needs an object: let the room keep them face down. With a bare verb straight after it, nothing says who is being allowed to do anything.
The third-person form, which needs a subject standing in front of it, and at the head of the sentence there is none.
An apostrophe after the s marks a plural owner, and nothing here is owned by anything. Whatever that spelling could mean elsewhere, this sentence gives it nothing to own.
Correct. Expand it: let us keep the scoring sheets face down, with the apostrophe standing where the u of us was dropped.

Why this answer

An apostrophe hides more than a form of be. Expanded, this one gives let us keep the scoring sheets face down, an instruction that takes in the person writing it as well, so the apostrophe is doing contraction work with a dropped u. Ownership is not in play at all, which rules out the marked plural, and the two unmarked verb forms leave the sentence with no object to let and no subject to do the letting.

Question

  • Standard

The city sweeps the fallen leaves from the sidewalks of Rowan Avenue every week in November, but the driveway on the far side of the gate is ____, and it stays buried until we fetch a rake.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The driveway belongs to the we the sentence names at the end of it, and the standalone possessive is what says so when no noun follows. It takes no apostrophe.
The apostrophe again, on a word that is possessive before anything is added to it.
A different word that happens to sound the same: a plain plural noun with no possessive marking of any kind, so it cannot say that the driveway belongs to anybody.
The determiner, which has to be followed by the thing owned: our driveway, never the driveway is our.

Why this answer

Sort the four by what they are before choosing between them: a standalone possessive, the same word carrying a mark it never takes, a plain plural that is merely a homophone, and a determiner. The sentence names the owners, we, and the blank closes its clause with no noun after it, so what is wanted is the possessive that can stand alone, spelled without an apostrophe.

Question

  • Standard

Nadia and Mattie fire the same brick oven on alternate mornings, but nothing else about the two shops is shared: ____ ledgers are kept in separate rooms, and each buys flour from a different supplier.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Separate ownership is marked separately, so each name carries its own apostrophe and the two sets of ledgers stay apart.
The joint form, which is how careful writing marks a pair holding one thing between them. This sentence rules that out on both sides of the blank: the oven is the only shared thing, and the ledgers sit in separate rooms.
Two bare names make a subject, not a possessor, so nothing here says the ledgers belong to anybody.
Marks the first owner and abandons the second, so Mattie ends up owning nothing at all.

Why this answer

Two owners sharing one thing take a single marking at the end of the pair; two owners with one thing each take a marking apiece. The marking cannot carry that on its own, so the sentence has to say which it is, and this one says it twice: the oven is the only shared thing, and the ledgers sit in separate rooms. Two sets, so both names are marked.

Question

  • Standard

Only one club fills cylinders on this stretch of coast, and it keeps a log of every bottle it fills, so each entry in the ____ log can be checked against the test date stamped on the neck.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Marks the owner and gets the count wrong: the sentence opens by saying only one club fills cylinders here.
Correct. The sentence says the club keeps the log, so the club is the owner and takes the marking; safety is telling you what kind of log it is.
Hangs the marking on the end of dive club safety, which makes that whole phrase the owner. The sentence names the club as the keeper of the log, and the club is where the marking belongs.
Marks both, which reads as a chain: the club's safety, and that safety's log. The sentence gives the log one owner, so one link is all there is to mark.

Why this answer

Two nouns stand in front of the head noun and the sentence has already said which of them owns it: the club keeps the log. So the club takes the marking, and safety stays bare, because it is naming the kind of log rather than a second owner. Note that the bar on the others is the sentence, not the words: a purpose can own things in English (the project's log), and one club is one owner, so what settles both is what this sentence says.

Question

  • Advanced

Daylight reaches the fish aisle through a glazed lantern set at the ridge of the ____, a building the borough has reglazed twice since 1889.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Of has already named the owner, so nothing is left for an apostrophe to do. The page's rule says the noun after of stays bare, and this phrase names one definite ridge.
Plural, where the phrase that follows names a single building.
Plural and marked as well, so it is wrong on both counts at once.
Correct. The of-phrase is already doing the possessive work, so the noun after of stays bare.

Why this answer

English has two ways of saying one ownership, the hall's ridge and the ridge of the hall, and a sentence picks one. This one has picked of, and the thing owned is a single definite ridge rather than one of several, so nothing is left for a second marking to add. The plural spellings fail earlier still, on the single building named after the comma.

Question

  • Standard

Nobody dips a wick until the ____ up to temperature, because wax that has not cleared sets cloudy in the finished candle.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A plural, and it brings no verb, so the clause after until never says anything about the tanks at all.
The bare noun, which leaves the clause after until holding a subject and no verb.
Correct. Expand it: until the tank is up to temperature, with the apostrophe standing where the i of is was dropped.
A possessive with nothing after it to own, no noun earlier for it to stand in for, and no verb either.

Why this answer

The clause after until has a subject and needs a verb, and only one of these spellings brings one. Read the apostrophe as the word it abbreviates and the clause completes itself: until the tank is up to temperature. The lesson worth keeping is that an apostrophe on an ordinary noun is not automatically possessive.

Question

  • Advanced

Every page passes one reader before it goes to the printer, and the proof that travels with it is always the ____ desk copy, marked in pencil and initialed at the foot.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Marks the noun at the front and then carries on with the rest of the title, which puts the apostrophe in the middle of the owner.
No marking at all, and a plural besides, where the sentence names one reader.
Correct. The complete owner is the whole title, so the marking waits until the end of it.
Marks the title twice. One owner takes one marking, and it goes after the owner is finished.

Why this answer

The marking goes after the complete owner, and the complete owner here is a title of three words rather than the noun at the front of it. Write the owner out, editor in chief, then hang the apostrophe and s on the end. Splitting the title or marking it twice both treat one owner as though it finished early.

Question

  • Advanced

Only one of the pottery's twelve apprentices threw anything at all that winter, so the long shelf by the door still holds a dozen of that ____ from that season alone.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

That is singular and cannot stand in front of a plural owner, so that apprentices' fails before the ownership is even weighed. A dozen bowls do not make a dozen potters either.
Correct. One owner takes apostrophe plus s, and the number of bowls is a separate fact that changes nothing about the marking.
No marking at all, which leaves two nouns sitting side by side with nothing saying that the bowls are anyone's.
Marks the thing owned as well as the owner. Bowls here is a plain plural; it owns nothing that follows it.

Why this answer

Two numbers are in play and only one of them decides the apostrophe. The bowls run to a dozen; the owner is one apprentice, and that in front of the blank settles it before any reading of the first clause is needed, since a singular demonstrative cannot head a plural owner. The marking records the owner's number, so a dozen things owned by one person still take apprentice's. Then place the mark once, after the whole of the owner and nowhere else, which is why the unmarked plural and the doubly marked form both fail.

Question

  • Advanced

Teodora and Elias bought the corner bakery together and have never divided anything about it, so the ovens, the mixer and the delivery bike are all listed on the insurance as ____ equipment.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Marking each name separately says each of them owns some of the equipment on their own, and the sentence says they have never divided anything.
Correct. They own it together, so the pair is one owner and the marking goes after the whole of it, on the last name only. Three things are listed, and that changes nothing.
No marking at all, which leaves the names sitting in front of equipment with nothing saying it is theirs.
Half a marking. Whichever way the ownership runs, a pair marked on the first name and not the second is not a form English uses.

Why this answer

Count the owners and ask how they hold what they hold; do not count the things. The sentence says the two bought the bakery together and have never divided anything, so they own the equipment jointly, which makes the pair a single owner and puts one marking after the whole of it. Three items are listed, and a joint marking covers any number of them. Had the sentence said each of them brought their own machines, the separate marking would be right and the list would look exactly the same.

Question

  • Standard

A tenant leaving the housing co-op gives the committee ____ notice in writing, and the room is advertised on the board in the hall the same week.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. A stretch of time placed in front of a noun this way takes the possessive marking, and months is a regular plural, so the apostrophe follows its s.
Leaves the measure unmarked. English writes a week's wait and three months' notice with the marking, however little the phrase feels like ownership.
Marks a single month, and the sentence counts three.
Stacks a second s on a plural that already ends in one.

Why this answer

A stretch of time standing in front of the noun it measures takes the possessive marking even though nothing changes hands: the notice runs for three months, and English marks a measure used that way with an apostrophe. Note the shape rather than the words: three months ago and a three-month period are different constructions and take none. Then the ordinary owner test does the spelling, and a plural already ending in -s takes the apostrophe alone.

Question

  • Advanced

The gardener's shears have not touched the greenhouse vine since December, and ____ borne more fruit this summer than in the three years before it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Plural, where the sentence names one vine, and borne is a participle that cannot anchor a clause on its own.
Plural, marked as an owner, and owning nothing: what follows the blank is a participle rather than a noun.
Correct. Expand it and the clause completes: the vine has borne more fruit this summer, with the apostrophe standing where the ha of has was dropped.
The bare noun, which leaves borne with no auxiliary to hang on and the clause with no finite verb.

Why this answer

There are two apostrophes to account for here and they are doing different jobs. The one on gardener's sits in front of the thing owned and marks a possessor. In the blank, borne is a past participle and needs an auxiliary in front of it, so what the apostrophe hides is a verb: the vine has borne.

Question

  • Advanced

The packing list asks for your own towel and your own flashlight, because ____ in a tent with three other people from the first night.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The determiner, which is right twice earlier in this sentence and wrong here: a determiner must be followed by the noun it owns, and the clause after because has none for it.
The determiner carrying a mark it can never take. Yours, like its, hers, theirs, and ours, is spelled without an apostrophe.
The standalone possessive, which stands in for a noun phrase and still leaves the clause without a verb.
Correct. Expand it and the clause completes: you are in a tent with three other people, with the apostrophe standing where the a of are was dropped.

Why this answer

The same word has already appeared twice in this sentence, correctly and without an apostrophe, which is exactly why the blank has to be tested rather than matched to it. The clause after because has no verb, and only the expansion brings one: you are in a tent with three other people. A determiner would need a noun after it, and there is none.

Question

  • Advanced

The three-day forecast is chalked up beside the ____ board at the ferry office, and the crews read both before deciding whether to sail.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Marks the plural as an owner, and the board is not theirs and not for them: it lists them. A plural does take the marking in a girls' school, which is a school for girls, and takes none here.
Marks one owner, and the same objection stands, with a second on top: the board lists every sailing rather than one.
Stacks a second s after the apostrophe on a plural that already ends in one.
Correct. A plural standing in front of another noun and only saying what kind of thing it is takes no apostrophe at all.

Why this answer

Ask the ownership question: is the board theirs, or for them? Neither, since it lists them, so the word is telling you which board this is, in the way sports tells you which hall. A plural doing that job is a modifier and takes no marking however plural it looks. The contrast to hold on to is a girls' school, where the plural does take the marking because the school is for the girls.
