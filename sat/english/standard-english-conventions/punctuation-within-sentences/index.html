Punctuation Within Sentences

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

Each item asks what punctuation belongs inside one sentence. Decide what a mark would set off, whether the sentence can spare it, and whether the structure allows that mark.

Key takeaways

  • Removable material in the middle of a sentence takes a matched pair: one mark opening, the same mark closing.
  • Removable material at the end takes one opening mark; the sentence's own end closes it.
  • Material that says which one the sentence means takes no mark at all.
  • A lone comma never stands between a subject and its verb.
  • In a series, commas go between the items. None follows the last, unless it is closing a fence or doing some other job of its own.
  • The swap and and tests tell you whether two adjectives are coordinate. They are tests, not the rule.
  • A colon needs a complete sentence on its left.

What the SAT may ask

  • Close a fence the sentence has already opened.
  • Decide whether a clause is essential or removable.
  • Decide whether a comma belongs between a subject and its verb.
  • Punctuate a series, or two adjectives in a row.
  • Decide whether a colon may follow the words before it.

The rule

Ask one thing of any stretch of words: does it say which one the sentence means, or does it add? Lift it out. If the sentence still works and still points at the same thing, the material is supplementary. A matched pair of marks around it is a fence.

Where the material sits What it takes
Mid-sentence, and supplementary A matched pair, same mark both sides
At the end, and supplementary One opening mark; the sentence's end closes it
Saying which one No mark
A state after its town, a year inside a full date A pair, by convention

So The [subject], [supplementary], [verb] ... takes two marks and The [subject] [verb] ..., [supplementary] takes one. A pair of dashes fences exactly as a pair of commas does.

An opener before the subject usually takes a comma after it, and a short one often takes none.

A lone comma may never break the sentence's core: not between a subject and its verb, not between a verb and its object, not before a that clause completing a noun. So a phrase that is the subject takes no mark before the verb, however long it runs.

With a name, ask the same question and let the sentence's own count answer it. My only cousin, Devi is fenced because the sentence has already picked her out; my cousin Devi is not, because the name is what picks her out.

Two items joined by and are not a series and take no comma between them. Two whole clauses joined by and are a different structure, and Sentence Boundaries covers it.

In a series of three or more, commas go between the items. Whether one goes before the final and is a style choice, and no item here turns on it. Nothing follows the last item unless that mark has a job of its own, such as closing a fence.

When the items themselves contain commas, semicolons separate them. The tour stops in Reno, Nevada; Boise, Idaho; and Bend, Oregon. These semicolons join no clauses, so they need no complete sentence on either side.

Two adjectives in a row take a comma only when they are coordinate, meaning each describes the noun by itself. Swapping them or writing and between them tests for that; it is not the rule itself. A cold, steady rain, but a faded plastic chair. An -ly adverb is not an adjective (a freshly painted door), and no comma stands between the last modifier and its noun.

A colon needs a complete sentence on its left. The Sentence Boundaries lesson carries the full colon rule.

Why the rule exists

A fence is a promise: everything inside it could be lifted out and the sentence would close up behind it. One mark alone, mid-sentence, breaks that promise.

Worked examples

1. Identifying clause beside supplementary clause

The stockroom door that faces the alley stays bolted. The main door, which swells in wet weather, gets planed every spring.

Task: Decide which clause takes marks.

Evidence: That faces the alley answers which door?; lift it out and the first sentence points at no door in particular. The main door has already named its door, so the second clause answers no such question and lifts out cleanly.

Answer: The first clause takes no mark. The second takes a comma pair, the closing comma landing where the clause ends, at weather.

Closest trap: fencing the essential. Commas around an identifying clause invite the reader to drop the words that say which one.

2. When no mark is the answer

Everyone who signed up to carry a hamper to the far end of the meadow ____ arrived before the gate opened.

Task: Decide whether a comma belongs in front of the verb.

Evidence: Everyone who signed up to carry a hamper to the far end of the meadow is one long subject, and arrived is its verb. Nothing has been set off, so there is no fence to close.

Answer: No mark. On this question type the right choice is often the one with no punctuation in it.

Closest trap: subject-verb comma. A long subject invites a comma at the point a reader would draw breath, and length is not a reason.

Common traps

Half fences, mismatched pairs, and stray commas

Trap Correction
half-fenced interrupter An interrupter mid-sentence needs a mark on each side. One mark opens a fence it never closes
mismatched fence The mark that opens must be the mark that closes
semicolon as fancy comma A semicolon joins whole sentences, or separates list items that carry commas of their own. It does neither job as a stronger comma
fencing the essential If the material says which one, take the marks out
subject-verb comma No single comma inside the sentence's core

A choice can also put a colon after something that is not a sentence, or use a mark that cannot introduce a list.

The method: Identify or add, then mark both ends

  1. Decide whether the words identify the noun or add to it. Lift the stretch out and read the rest. If the sentence still works and still points at the same thing, the material is supplementary; if it stops saying which one, it is identifying and takes no mark.
  2. Check where the added material begins and ends. Mid-sentence it takes a matched pair, closing where the material ends: there is no half fence and no pair built of two different marks. At the end of the sentence, one opening mark is the whole job.
  3. Test any colon or semicolon. Cover what follows a colon; the rest must stand alone as a sentence. A semicolon joins whole sentences, or separates list items that carry commas of their own.
  4. Leave the sentence's own connections unbroken, then reread. No single comma stands between subject and verb, or verb and object. Count a series, and swap adjacent adjectives to test them.

At the back counter of the hardware store, the key cutter, a bench-mounted machine from the ____ cuts a clean copy of every blank in the drawer.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: half-fenced interrupter. The comma after cutter opened a fence around the appositive, and nothing closes it, so the aside runs straight into still cuts.
Trap: semicolon as fancy comma. A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, and still cuts a clean copy of every blank has no subject.
Trap: colon after a fragment. Everything to the colon's left is a subject and its appositive, with no verb, so nothing has been said for a colon to deliver.
Correct. The comma after cutter opened a fence around a bench-mounted machine from the fifties, and this comma closes it. Lift the material out and the sentence is complete without it.

The method, applied

Step 1: lift a bench-mounted machine from the fifties out and the key cutter cuts a clean copy of every blank still stands. Step 2: the comma after cutter opened a fence, so the blank must close it. Step 3: no colon or semicolon can follow a subject with no verb. Step 4: reread with fifties, still in place.

Practice 2

The painted turtles crowding the sunny end of the basking ____ off into the pond at the first footstep on the dock.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: subject-verb comma. The subject runs long, and the length is the bait: a single comma never separates a subject from its verb, however far the subject runs.
Correct. The whole stretch before the blank is one subject and slide is its verb, so no mark belongs between them.
Trap: semicolon as fancy comma. A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, and here the left side is a bare subject and the right a bare predicate.
Trap: colon after a fragment. A colon follows only words that could end with a period, and this subject has no verb yet.

The method, applied

Step 1: crowding the sunny end of the basking log cannot be lifted out, because it says which turtles slide. Step 2: identifying material takes no mark. Step 3: neither heavy mark can follow a subject with no verb. Step 4: log, slide would park a lone comma between the subject and its verb, so the blank takes nothing.

Practice 3

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Standard

The sap ____ first on cold nights, so the crew checks that stretch of the sugarbush before any other.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. That cross the open hollow answers which lines?, and identifying material takes no fence at all.
Trap: fencing the essential. The comma pair invites the reader to lift the clause out, but without it the sentence no longer says which lines freeze first.
Trap: half-fenced interrupter. The comma after lines opens a fence that nothing closes, and it opens it around material the sentence cannot spare.
Trap: subject-verb comma. The clause is identifying, so there is no interrupter to fence, and this lone comma lands between the subject and its verb, freeze.

The method, applied

Step 1: lift that cross the open hollow out and the sentence stops saying which lines freeze. Step 2: identifying material takes no fence, so lines that cross the open hollow freeze is right as written. A full comma pair fences the essential, and either single comma builds half a fence. Step 3: no colon or semicolon is on offer. Step 4: hollow, freeze also parks a lone comma between the subject and its verb.

Practice 4

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Boundaries
  • Standard

Deep under the farmhouse, the cheese cave's cedar ____ bloomy round, a washed-rind square, and a hard wheel that will age for a year.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: wrong mark before a list. A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, and the list that follows is not one. Introducing a list is a colon's job.
Trap: subject-verb comma. The colon at the end is placed correctly, but the first comma splits racks from its verb hold.
Correct. The cheese cave's cedar racks hold three styles could stand alone as a sentence, so a colon may follow it and deliver the list it promised.
Trap: colon after a fragment. This early, the colon follows the cheese cave's cedar racks, a subject with no verb, so nothing has been claimed yet.

The method, applied

Step 1: nothing here lifts out, so this is not a fencing decision. Step 2: with nothing removable, no comma pair applies. Step 3: the colon decides it. The cheese cave's cedar racks hold three styles could end with a period, so a colon may follow and hand over the list. Moved earlier, the colon follows a subject with no verb, and a semicolon cannot introduce a list. Step 4: racks, hold three styles: a splits the subject from its verb.

Practice 5

  • Standard English Conventions

  • Advanced

The gatehouse ____ needs two sets of hands on the crank before the portcullis will clear the arch.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: half-fenced interrupter. The fence opens after winch and never closes, so the clause runs headlong into still needs with nothing to mark its end.
Correct. Which the castle's last steward greased every winter is removable, and this choice fences it with a matched pair: one comma after winch, one after winter.
Trap: half-fenced interrupter. Here the fence closes but was never opened, so the comma after winter stands alone between a long subject and its verb.
Trap: semicolon as fancy comma. A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, and a clause beginning with which is not one.

The method, applied

Step 1: lift which the castle's last steward greased every winter out and the gatehouse winch still needs two sets of hands still stands, and still points at the same winch. Step 2: removable material takes a matched pair, and only winch, which the castle's last steward greased every winter, still both opens and closes the fence. The clause's length is the camouflage, and the comma after winch is easy to forget by the time the eye reaches winter. Step 3: a clause opening with which is not a complete sentence, so the semicolon fails. Step 4: the matched commas are a fence, not a lone subject-verb comma.

Practice 6

The planetarium's star projector—bought secondhand in 1961, rebuilt twice, and kept running on scavenged parts since 2004 ____ a sharper sky than the machine that replaced it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: mismatched fence. A dash opened this fence and a comma cannot close it, because two different marks are not a pair.
Trap: half-fenced interrupter. The dash after projector opened a fence that nothing closes, so the aside runs into the main clause's verb.
Trap: semicolon as fancy comma. A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, and still throws a sharper sky has no subject. It also leaves the dash unclosed.
Correct. The dash after projector opened the fence and this dash closes it, one mark on both sides. Lift the fenced material out and the sentence is complete.

The method, applied

Step 1: the dash after projector opened a fence around bought secondhand in 1961, rebuilt twice, and kept running on scavenged parts since 2004. Lift it out and the planetarium's star projector still throws a sharper sky still stands. Step 2: a fence closes with the mark that opened it, and only —still throws does. Step 3: the semicolon fails its own test, since still throws a sharper sky has no subject. Step 4: , still throws leaves a lone comma where the subject meets its verb, and still throws leaves the fence open.
