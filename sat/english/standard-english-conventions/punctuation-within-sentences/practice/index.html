Punctuation Within Sentences: practice session

20 questions on Punctuation Within Sentences, in random order. Question

  • Introductory

Before the pool opens each June, a county inspector ____ bolt that holds the high diving board to its tower and its ladder to the deck.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A lone comma cuts the verb off from the only thing that finishes it. Length is what makes the pause feel overdue, and length is not a reason: a verb and its object are two bones of one skeleton.
This comma lands between every and the noun it counts, which is a tighter join still. A counting word and its noun are one phrase.
Correct. Nothing stands between a verb and its object, however far the object runs on after it.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and what stands to its right is a noun phrase: the object the verb is still waiting for.

Why this answer

Find the skeleton before you weigh the pause. A county inspector is the subject, checks is the verb, and every bolt that holds the high diving board to its tower and its ladder to the deck is the object, all of it. A single comma may never break that frame, and the object's length is the whole of the disguise. The semicolon fails one step earlier still, since it needs a sentence on its right and gets a noun phrase.

Question

  • Introductory

The middle school's kitchen classroom keeps ____ oven mitts in a drawer beside every stove.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Two things joined by and are not a list, so nothing separates them.
The three-item pattern applied to two items. With only two things joined by and, nothing stands between them, and the comma here separates a subject's two objects for no reason.
The comma has landed on the far side of the conjunction, where it separates and from the item it was joining.
A semicolon takes over inside a list when its items are long or carry commas of their own, so that a reader can still tell one from the next. Two bare noun phrases give it nothing to do, and it is not a stronger comma.

Why this answer

Count the items. Spring timers and oven mitts are two things joined by and, and two joined that way take nothing between them: and is already doing all the separating there is to do. Commas between items belong to a list of three or more. The stronger marks are further out of reach still, since nothing on either side of the blank is a clause.

Question

  • Introductory

The lambing barn's ____ roof drums so loudly in a hailstorm that the ewes will not settle.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The comma says the two words are coordinate, a pair of adjectives each describing the roof. Run the tests: galvanized, low roof and low and galvanized roof both collapse, so they are not a pair.
Correct. Galvanized roof is the thing, and low describes that whole thing rather than sitting beside galvanized, so the two stack and take no comma.
The second comma stands between the last modifier and the noun it modifies, which no comma ever does.
A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, or list items long or tangled enough that commas can no longer keep them apart. Two adjectives are neither.

Why this answer

Two adjectives take a comma between them only when they are coordinate, and the two tests for that are mechanical: swap them, or write and between them. Galvanized, low roof and low and galvanized roof both fail, because galvanized has bonded to roof and low is describing the galvanized roof as a whole. Adjectives that stack that way take no mark at all, and none of them ever takes one in front of the noun.

Question

  • Introductory

Three of the county's churches still own handbells from the same Ohio foundry, and the ____ comes out only at Christmas.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Fencing a locating phrase offers it up as droppable. Drop in the Mercer chapel and nothing is left to tell one church's handbells from another's.
An opening mark with no closing one leaves the phrase neither fenced nor plain. This phrase wants no marks, and one mark is the worse of the two errors.
One comma sits between the whole subject, the set in the Mercer chapel, and its verb comes, which is inside the sentence's core where a lone comma cannot go.
Correct. In the Mercer chapel is what narrows three churches' handbells to one set, and a phrase doing that work is part of what the sentence names, so it takes no marks.

Why this answer

Three churches own handbells from the same foundry, so the set by itself does not say which one comes out at Christmas. In the Mercer chapel is the phrase that picks one out, and a phrase doing that is part of the thing being named rather than an aside about it, so no mark belongs at either end. A comma pair would offer it up as droppable; either comma on its own would leave a mark standing between the subject and comes.

Question

  • Introductory

Entries mailed to the quilt show's office in ____ after the first of June are returned unopened.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. A state named after its town is fenced by a pair, one comma opening and one closing, and the convention is what settles it rather than any judgment about what the sentence could spare.
The fence opens and never closes. The closing comma is the one writers drop, and dropping it runs the state straight into whatever follows.
This comma closes a fence that was never opened, leaving the town and the state jammed together in front of it.
With no mark at all the state reads as part of the town's name rather than as the place it sits in.

Why this answer

A state named after its town is fenced by convention, not by anything you have to work out and not by whether the sentence could spare it. American practice sets the state off with commas, and what the convention settles is that the fence is a pair. What hides the second comma here is that Bell Harbor, Wisconsin looks like one name: the comma inside it reads as part of how the place is written, so the eye stops counting marks and the fence never gets closed.

Question

  • Introductory

The town has recruited ____ biologist who has cleared duckweed from four other ponds, to spend one summer on the millpond.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The sentence already carries a closing comma after ponds, and with no opening mark that comma closes a fence nobody built. The name and the description it renames are left jammed together in front of it.
A semicolon cannot open a fence, and it demands a complete sentence on each side besides. What follows it here is a noun phrase.
A period demands a complete sentence on each side, and what follows is a noun phrase with a comma and an infinitive hanging off it. The town has recruited somebody and the sentence never says what for.
Correct. The comma after ponds is the closing half of a fence, and this is the half that opens it.

Why this answer

The sentence hands you one mark and asks for its partner. A biologist who has cleared duckweed from four other ponds renames the person just recruited, the name has already said which person that is, and the comma after ponds closes a fence around the rename. A fence is one mark on both sides, so the opening mark has to be the comma too: not a heavier mark, and not nothing.

Question

  • Standard

The farm raises its oyster mushrooms in a ____ room kept at fifty-five degrees all year.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The comma treats dimly as an adjective sitting beside lit. It is an adverb, and it is describing lit rather than the room: lit, dimly room and dimly and lit room both collapse.
Two commas, and neither has anything to separate. The second also stands between the last modifier and its noun, which no comma does.
Correct. Dimly modifies lit, and dimly lit together modifies room, so there is nothing here for a comma to stand between.
The pair of words is right and the comma is in the one place a comma never goes, between the modifier and the noun it modifies.

Why this answer

The comma between modifiers belongs only to a coordinate pair, meaning two words that each describe the noun and could swap places or take and between them. Dimly does not describe the room at all; it describes how the room is lit, and a word modifying the word after it is never separated from it. The room is a dimly lit one, and the modifier sits against its noun with nothing in between.

Question

  • Standard

The circle runs its yarn swap on the strict ____ nobody leaves with more skeins than they brought.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The comma fences off the very words that say what the understanding is. Strike the clause and the strict understanding names nothing, so the clause is not an aside and takes no mark.
Correct. The clause is the content of the noun in front of it, not a remark about it, so nothing stands between them.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and a clause opening with that is not one: it is doing a noun's work inside this sentence.
Which opens a relative clause, which needs a slot inside itself for the noun to fill. This clause has no such gap: it says what the understanding is rather than something about it.

Why this answer

Try the removal test and it fails at once: without nobody leaves with more skeins than they brought, the sentence has an understanding that is strict and no understanding of anything. A that clause completing a noun is that noun's content, and content is never fenced off from the word it completes. The relative which is a different word doing a different job, and this clause leaves it nothing to do.

Question

  • Standard

The charts in the archive's map drawer were signed by Emil Braun, the surveyor who ran the 1901 season ____ and Ada Poole, who inked the final plates.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Items that already carry commas of their own take semicolons between them, so an all-comma list gives one mark two jobs at two levels. Read straight through, five signers appear where three were meant.
Correct. Each item already carries a comma inside it, so semicolons separate the items and the commas keep the job they were doing.
One comma and one semicolon doing the same job. A list separates its items with one mark throughout, and the first boundary here still reads as a boundary inside an item.
The semicolons have gone between every pair of names rather than between the items, which cuts Nella Roux off from the words describing her and makes four signers out of three.

Why this answer

A semicolon has a second job, and it has nothing to do with joining clauses: when the items of a list carry commas of their own, semicolons separate the items so a reader can still tell one from the next. The three items here are Emil Braun, the surveyor who ran the 1901 season, Nella Roux, his assistant and Ada Poole, who inked the final plates, each holding a comma already. Commas throughout hide where one signer ends, and a semicolon inside an item cuts a name away from its description.

Question

  • Standard

The inverter under the library's solar panels gives off a ____ hum that carries down the stairwell on quiet afternoons.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

This is the pattern for adjectives that stack, and these two do not: uneven, low hum and low and uneven hum both read, which is the test for a coordinate pair.
The first comma is right and the second is not. Nothing stands between the last adjective and the noun it describes.
A semicolon needs a complete sentence on each side, or list items long or tangled enough that commas can no longer keep them apart. Two adjectives in front of a noun are neither.
Correct. Both words describe the hum itself, so they are coordinate and take a comma between them.

Why this answer

Run the two tests on low and uneven. They swap without damage, and and fits between them, so each is describing the hum on its own rather than one describing the other. Coordinate adjectives take a comma between them, and that is the only comma the phrase gets: the last one still sits directly against its noun.

Question

  • Standard

A practice keyboard, a rack of tuning weights, and two spare ____ in the room below the carillon's bells.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The list's own rhythm invites this comma, and it lands between the whole subject and its verb. Commas separate the items of a list from each other, and this one has nothing on its right to separate: the items have run out and the verb has arrived.
Correct. The three items together are the subject, and nothing stands between a subject and its verb.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side. To its right stands a bare verb with no subject of its own.
A colon may follow only words that could end with a period, and a list of three things is not a sentence. Nothing has been promised for the colon to deliver.

Why this answer

Read the list as one thing, because that is what it is: a practice keyboard, a rack of tuning weights, and two spare clappers is the sentence's subject, and sit is its verb. The commas inside a list separate its items from each other, so the last item takes none after it, and what follows the list here is not another item at all.

Question

  • Standard

The station released its first balloon on March ____ at six in the morning, and it has kept the hour ever since.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The fence opens after the day and never closes, so the year runs straight into the time of day with nothing to say the date has ended.
This comma closes a fence that was never opened, and it leaves the day and the year jammed together in front of it.
Correct. In a month-day-year date the year is fenced by a pair, one comma opening and one closing, and the sentence carries on after it.
With no marks at all the three parts of the date read as one long number, and nothing separates the year from the day it belongs to.

Why this answer

A month-day-year date fences its year by convention: nothing here has to be worked out, and it does not turn on whether the sentence could spare the year. What the convention settles is that the fence is a pair. What hides the second comma here is arithmetic rather than a name. March 4, 1962 is one date and the comma inside it looks like part of the number, so the eye treats the whole of it as a single figure and stops watching for a mark to close it. Half a fence is not a fence.

Question

  • Standard

Ana Reyes grew up with three brothers, and her ____ builds the sets for every show the community theater puts on.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A pair of commas says the name could be lifted out. Lift it out here and her brother stops naming anyone in particular, because the sentence has put three brothers in play.
The fence opens and never closes, so the reader is left inside an aside that the verb then runs into.
This comma closes a fence that was never opened, and it lands between the subject and its verb.
Correct. With three brothers in the sentence, the name is what says which one, and identifying material takes no fence.

Why this answer

Ask whether the name identifies the brother or adds a fact about a brother already identified. The sentence's own count answers it: three brothers are in play, so lift Mateo out and her brother points at no one in particular. The name is doing the identifying, and identifying material takes no marks. Had the sentence said her only brother, the name would add rather than identify, so the pair would be right and the unfenced form wrong; the two half fences stay wrong either way.

Question

  • Advanced

The label on every crate in the museum's storage wing, a strip of card ____ which way up the crate has to sit.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The fenced material runs to the end of the relative clause, so that is where the closing mark goes and the sentence's verb follows it.
The fence closes in the middle of the relative clause, cutting tie on off from the with wire that finishes it and leaving that phrase stranded in front of the verb.
The fence opened after wing and never closes, so the description of the card runs headlong into says with nothing to mark where it ended.
The fence is closed correctly and then a second comma is parked between says and the clause that is its object, which is inside the skeleton where no lone comma stands.

Why this answer

The question is not whether to close the fence but where. Lift the aside out and read what remains: the label on every crate in the museum's storage wing says which way up the crate has to sit. The material you lifted was a strip of card that the movers tie on with wire, all of it, so the closing comma goes at its far end. Closing inside the relative clause leaves part of the aside standing in the main clause, and not closing at all leaves the reader inside it.

Question

  • Advanced

The yard's own rules say that the operator has to ____ power cable for cuts, and the log has to show that it was done.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A dash opens this fence and a comma closes it, and two marks that are not the same mark are not a pair. Nothing is wrong with the dash itself; what is wrong is that nothing matches it further on.
The fence opens and never closes, so what was meant as an aside swallows the object as well.
Correct. This is the only choice whose two marks match and enclose the whole of what was dropped in, and a fence between a verb and its object has to be complete or it is a lone comma splitting the two.
One comma, and it lands where a lone comma can never stand: between the verb and the object it takes.

Why this answer

Lift the phrase out first: the operator has to check the magnet's power cable for cuts is the skeleton, so before every lift is by-the-way material that has been dropped into the middle of it. That changes nothing about the rule. Once a fence is opened it takes the same mark on both sides, and here the half-built versions are worse than usual, because the single comma each of them leaves behind stands between a verb and its object.

Question

  • Advanced

The club's Sunday water ____ the county dropped in 2011, when its own lab closed—is done by two retired chemists who live a mile upstream.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The sentence closes this fence with a dash, so a dash is what opens it: a pair is one mark on both sides.
A comma cannot open what a dash closes. Two marks that are not the same mark are not a pair, and the aside is left with no readable start.
With no opening mark, water testing a chore the county dropped reads as a phrase of its own until the closing dash arrives with nothing behind it to match.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and it cannot open a fence in any case. What follows it here is a noun phrase.

Why this answer

The sentence hands you the closing mark and asks for its partner. A pair of dashes fences an interrupter exactly as a pair of commas does, and why this writer preferred dashes is visible inside the aside, which carries a comma of its own before when its own lab closed: a comma pair outside that one would have left the reader three commas and no quick way to tell which belonged to the fence. That is a preference and not a rule: a comma pair there would be legal, only harder to read. What the sentence has already settled is the part that is fixed, and it is the part the blank asks about: whatever a pair is made of, both sides have to be made of the same thing.

Question

  • Advanced

The festival's only ____ has been keeping the same three-page cleanup checklist since 1994.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Left unmarked, the name reads as the thing that says which site manager is meant. Only has already settled that there is one, so the name has nothing left to identify.
Correct. With one site manager in the sentence, the name adds a fact rather than picking someone out, and supplementary material mid-sentence is marked at both ends.
Half a fence. The comma opens an aside that nothing closes, so the aside runs into the verb.
The other half. This comma closes a fence that was never opened, and it lands between the subject and its verb.

Why this answer

Ask whether the name identifies the manager or adds to a manager already identified, and one word answers it: only. It leaves exactly one, so lift Priya Raman out and the festival's only site manager still points at the same person. Material the sentence does not need in order to say which one is supplementary, and supplementary material mid-sentence is marked at both ends. Change only to three and the same sentence would take no marks at all.

Question

  • Advanced

Every dog that arrives at the shelter gets a collar tag, a photograph ____ card that follows it from run to run.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A comma on each side of the conjunction. The first separates the items as it should; the second separates and from the item it is joining, which leaves the conjunction stranded.
The comma has moved to the far side of the conjunction, so the mark that should be dividing the last two items is instead sitting between and and the item it introduces.
This puts a comma at one boundary and a semicolon at the next, and a list separates its items with one mark throughout. Semicolons take over only when the items carry commas of their own, and these three are bare noun phrases.
Correct. The series comma divides the last two items, so it stands in front of the word that joins them.

Why this answer

A series comma does one job: it stands between two items. That fixes where it goes when the conjunction arrives, because and belongs to the item after it, so the division comes first and the conjunction and its item stay together. A comma on the far side of and divides nothing, and semicolons are held back for lists whose items have grown long or tangled enough that commas can no longer keep them apart.

Question

  • Advanced

The depot's ____ bench has held four generations of passengers waiting for the morning train.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The second comma treats oak as a third adjective in the row. It is not: oak bench is the thing itself, and the other two words are describing that thing.
No comma at all, which would be right if all three words stacked. The first two do not: scarred, long bench and long and scarred bench both read.
Correct. Long and scarred are coordinate and take a comma between them; oak has bonded to bench and takes none in front of it.
The first comma is right and the last is not. Nothing stands between the final modifier and the noun it modifies.

Why this answer

Three modifiers, and the tests have to be run twice. Long and scarred swap without damage and take and between them, so they are coordinate and earn a comma. Scarred and oak fail both tests, because oak bench is one thing that scarred describes, so no comma comes between them and none ever comes between the last modifier and its noun.

Question

  • Advanced

The judging sheet, a single page the fair has used since ____ crust, filling, and appearance.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The fence opened after sheet and never closes, so what stands in front of the colon is a subject with an aside still hanging off it rather than a sentence.
Correct. The closing comma finishes the fence, and what is left in front of the mark is a complete sentence that has promised three scores.
A semicolon demands a complete sentence on each side, and three bare nouns are a list, not a sentence.
With no mark at all the list runs into the clause, and a reader meets three scores crust before working out that the naming has started.

Why this answer

Two decisions ride this blank and the first settles the second. Close the fence around a single page the fair has used since 1978 and what is left in front of the mark is the judging sheet asks for three scores, which could end with a period, so it has earned a colon and the colon pays off the promise three scores just made. Leave the fence open and the same words are a subject with an aside dangling from it, which no colon may follow.
