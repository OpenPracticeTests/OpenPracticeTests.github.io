Verb Forms and Tense: practice session

20 questions on Verb Forms and Tense, in random order. Unlike the lesson, the difficulty of each question stays hidden until you open the solution, so you meet them the way test day hands them to you: with no label. There is no timer and no score. Answer a question, then press Show solution whenever you want to see how you did and why.

Question

  • Introductory

The volunteers at the county park's meteor watch chalk every sighting onto a board by the gate, and this winter's column has already ____ the record they posted in 2011.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Broke is the plain past form, and the plain past cannot follow has. The perfect is built out of has plus the participle, and broke is not it.
Correct. Has takes the past participle, and the past participle of break is broken.
The bare form follows a modal or do, never has. Has break is not a shape English builds.
Two present-tense endings cannot stack. Has is already carrying the tense, so the word after it is a participle rather than a second finite verb.

Why this answer

The tense is settled before you reach the blank: has is already sitting there, so the only question is which form of the verb it takes. A perfect is have or has plus the past participle, and for an irregular verb that participle is a third form, not the plain past. Break runs break, broke, broken, so the one that can follow has is broken.

Question

  • Introductory

The route the bloodmobile follows on Saturdays was ____ by a dispatcher who has driven it herself in every kind of weather.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The bare form cannot follow was. Was choose is not a shape English builds, in the passive or anywhere else.
Chose is the plain past, and a passive is not built out of one. The form be reaches for is the participle, which for this verb is a third word.
Two tense-marked forms cannot stack. Was is already finite, so what follows it is a participle rather than a second verb carrying its own -s.
Correct. A passive is be plus the past participle, and the past participle of choose is chosen.

Why this answer

The by phrase names who did the choosing, which is the mark of a passive, and a passive is a form of be plus the past participle. Was is on the page already, so the blank holds chosen. Note which form loses out: chose is the plain past, and for an irregular verb that is a different word from the participle, which is exactly the confusion the item is built on. The other two cannot follow was at all, one because the bare form never does and one because was has already taken the tense.

Question

  • Introductory

The compressor under the curling club's floor quit an hour before the Saturday draw, so the ice crew did not ____ the sheet between the third and fourth games.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Did is carrying the tense, and what follows do is always the bare form.
Did not resurfaced marks the past twice. Once did has taken the tense, the main verb gives it up.
The -s ending is present-tense marking, and did has already put the sentence in the past. The two cannot sit next to each other.
Did cannot govern a second auxiliary. Did not had resurfaced stacks two tensed forms where the frame allows one.

Why this answer

English negates a plain past by handing the tense to do, and once do has it the main verb reverts to its bare form. So the blank takes resurface, however past the sentence feels. Every other choice leaves the tense marked twice, which is what did was brought in to prevent.

Question

  • Introductory

The allergy list taped inside the cafeteria door warns that the house dressing ____ ground peanuts under a layer of cream.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

An -ing form describes without asserting. That the house dressing hiding ground peanuts never gets around to saying anything, so the warning has no content.
A perfect participle sounds the most finished of the three and is still not a verb that can anchor a clause. It cannot be what the list warns.
Correct. The clause after warns that is a clause of its own, so it needs a verb that can serve as its predicate, and hides is the only one offered.
A bare past participle needs an auxiliary in front of it. The dressing hidden ground peanuts has no has or had to lean on.

Why this answer

Warns that opens a second clause, and a clause is not finished until it has a finite verb, one that can serve as its predicate. Read the four back with the house dressing in front of them and only hides completes a statement; the other three describe, name, or wait for an auxiliary that never comes. Nothing here turns on which tense is right, because only one choice is a tense at all.

Question

  • Introductory

At the first meet in the new pool, the referee asked that each swimmer ____ behind the blocks until the heat in the water had finished.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A verb of requesting does not take an ordinary indicative in its that clause at all. Asked that each swimmer stays reads as a report of what swimmers do, which is not what a referee was doing.
This reports what a swimmer happens to be doing, in the present, under a request made in the past. Both halves of that are wrong.
Correct. A verb of requesting takes the bare form in the clause it governs, whatever the subject and whatever tense the requesting verb itself carries.
Will states what is going to happen. A request is not a prediction, and asked that ... will stay mixes the two.

Why this answer

Some verbs govern a form that has nothing to do with time. Ask, require and demand, used to lay something down, take the bare form in their that clause, so it is asked that each swimmer stay, with no -s and no backshift, exactly as it would be asks that each swimmer stay. The three rejected forms are all indicatives, and an indicative reports what is so rather than asking for it.

Question

  • Introductory

The volunteers who feed the orphaned fawns wish the outdoor pen ____ twice its size, but the county will not release the field behind it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Wish takes were for a state that is not so, whether the subject is singular or plural.
Is asserts that the pen really is twice its size, which the rest of the sentence denies. Wish does not take a straight statement of fact.
A wish is not a forecast. Will says the pen is going to grow, and the sentence's but clause says the opposite.
A progressive reports something in the middle of changing, and a size is not an activity. Is being twice its size is not a shape English builds.

Why this answer

Wish is a mood trigger, and for a state that is contrary to fact right now edited English reaches for were, whatever the subject. The test is not what time the pen's size belongs to but whether the sentence is describing the world or wishing it different, and the but clause says which. Is asserts what the sentence goes on to deny, will be predicts a change the county has refused, and the progressive is not a form a size can take. (Informal American usage writes was here too, and it is deliberately not among the choices: an item may not be decided by which of two accepted forms you were taught.)

Question

  • Standard

A wet spring slide finally flattened the ridge's oldest avalanche fence in 2011; by then the ski patrol ____ four fences on that one slope in thirty-one winters.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A present progressive reads the count off the moment of speaking, and by then has pointed at 2011 instead. The sentence is asking what the tally stood at back there, not what is happening now.
Correct. By then names a vantage point in the past and looks back over what had accumulated before it, which is what the past perfect is built to do.
The present perfect counts up to now. This total is read off at a moment in 2011, and the sentence gives no reason to think the count stopped there.
Would lose looks forward from its reference point or makes a repeating habit of the losing. Either way it is not reading a completed tally off the moment by then names.

Why this answer

An accumulation is not enough by itself; what settles the form is the vantage point the sentence reads it off from, and all three rejected forms read the total off the wrong one. By then points back at 2011, so the count is being taken at a moment that is itself in the past: not now (has lost), not in front of us (is losing), and not over and over (would lose). The form that looks back from a past point is had lost. (Two other forms a careful writer might use here, the plain past and the future perfect in its guessing sense, are deliberately not among the choices: this item is about which vantage a form points at, not about whether the past perfect is compulsory, and it is not.)

Question

  • Advanced

The food bank's walk-in cooler has held steady at thirty-four degrees ever since the morning a rebuilt compressor ____ the failing one.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The perfect belongs in the main clause here, where the holding steady reaches the present. What follows since is one dated morning, and a single event at a named moment is stated in the plain past.
The swap is what opened the span, so it is behind us. A present-tense verb after ever since leaves the span with no beginning.
A progressive says the swap is still under way, but the main clause has been running on the new compressor ever since it finished.
Correct. Ever since takes a plain past verb for the moment the span opens, while the clause it modifies carries the perfect.

Why this answer

Since frames two halves and gives them different forms. The clause whose span reaches the present takes the perfect, and it already has one: has held steady. The half naming where the span starts is a single event pinned to one morning, and a punctual event at a named moment takes the plain past, so the blank is replaced. Putting a perfect on both halves is the common miss: it tries to make the moment the span opens into a stretch of its own.

Question

  • Standard

Every fall before the timing chips arrived, a volunteer at each checkpoint ____ a runner's card with a differently shaped punch.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Would is how English states a habit that belongs to a finished stretch of the past, which is where before the timing chips arrived puts this one.
Every fall looks like a license for the present, and it is not: this asserts a habit the volunteers still keep, while the sentence has said the practice belonged to the years before the chips arrived, and the chips have arrived.
Will points forward from now, and the punches were retired before now. A repeating habit does not become a forecast because it repeated.
The present perfect runs a span up to the present moment, and this one closed when the chips arrived. The frame will not let it reach.

Why this answer

A repeating adverbial such as every fall tells you the action was a habit; it does not tell you when the habit lived. That is the frame's job, and before the timing chips arrived seals the habit inside the past, which rules out both the present and the future. Would mark is the form English keeps for a past habit. (The plain past would also be correct here, which is exactly why it is not on offer.)

Question

  • Standard

The company will restring every marionette in the workshop as soon as the summer tour ____ in the first week of September.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Would is the past's way of pointing forward, and nothing here is being reported from the past. The main clause is a plain will.
The tour has not run yet. A plain past inside a clause about September contradicts the will it is attached to.
English does not mark the future twice in this frame. The main clause carries will, and a time clause under as soon as is barred from carrying a second one.
Correct. A time clause about the future is written in the present, and the main clause's will supplies the future for both halves.

Why this answer

As soon as, when, before and until introduce time clauses, and English writes a future time clause in the present: the future is marked once, in the main clause, and the subordinate clause is barred from repeating it. So will end is not merely redundant, it is ungrammatical here, and the blank takes ends.

Question

  • Standard

The observatory's banding shed sits close enough to the river that its plank floor buckles most springs. If it stood ten yards farther up the bank, the spring floods ____ it every year.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A plain present verb reports what actually happens, and the first sentence has already said what actually happens: the floor buckles.
Will belongs with a real condition (if the shed is moved, the floods will spare it). Once the if clause has gone into the past form, the result clause cannot go forward.
Correct. An if clause in the past form describes a present situation that is not so, and its result clause takes would plus the bare verb.
What is missing is the modal. The remote result this sentence has set up is built on a past modal, and a perfect can ride behind one (would have spared); standing alone, this form states a real run of dry springs instead.

Why this answer

The first sentence is there to close off the other reading: the shed is near the river and the floor does buckle, so if it stood ten yards farther up is a past form doing present work, describing a situation that is not so. Once that half is set the other half is fixed: the result clause takes a past modal plus the bare verb, and would is the only one offered. The indicative asserts a sparing the sentence has denied, will belongs to the version where the shed might really be moved, and the bare perfect states as fact the very sparing the first sentence denies.

Question

  • Standard

The hatchery, ____ its fingerlings at dusk for thirty years, has watched the herons learn the schedule anyway.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A finite verb, and the sentence already has one: has watched. Two finite verbs with one subject and no conjunction between them make two sentences run together.
Correct. The main clause is complete without this stretch, so what belongs inside the commas is a phrase rather than a clause, and an -ing form is what heads one.
Finite again, and the same collision follows. Changing the tense does nothing about the fact that a second tensed verb has nowhere to sit.
Read as a past tense this is the same collision. Read as a past participle it says the hatchery was released, which is not what the sentence is about.

Why this answer

Most verb items ask which finite form belongs in the blank. This one asks whether a finite form belongs there at all. Find the main clause first: the hatchery has watched the herons learn the schedule is complete on its own, so the comma-fenced stretch in the middle is a phrase describing the hatchery, not a second clause. A phrase like that is headed by a nonfinite form, and the -ing form is the one that fits a subject doing the releasing.

Question

  • Standard

Because the aerial ladder could not clear the new hotel's roofline from the street out front, the company ____ it up the alley behind the building for every drill that summer.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

An infinitive names an action without performing it, so the main clause never says anything happened and the whole sentence is left dangling off because.
A perfect participle is the most finished-sounding of the nonfinite forms and still cannot anchor a clause. It describes the company rather than reporting what it did.
A bare past participle needs an auxiliary. Without a had in front of it, the company driven it up the alley has no verb at all.
Correct. The fronted because clause is subordinate, so the main clause still owes the sentence a tense-marked verb of its own, and this is the only one offered.

Why this answer

A fronted because clause has a finite verb, could, and that verb belongs to the subordinate clause; it cannot do the main clause's work as well. So the blank has to hold a finite verb, and only drove is one. The trap is that the sentence already sounds long and busy by the time you reach the blank, which is what makes a participle there easy to accept.

Question

  • Advanced

Only after the custard in the aging vat has chilled overnight ____ it into the freezer barrel.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. A fronted only after phrase pulls the auxiliary in front of the subject, and where there is no auxiliary to move, do is brought in to be moved.
Ordinary word order is what the fronting rules out. Only has taken the front of the sentence and left the main clause standing as though nothing had been moved.
The do is here but has not moved. Adding it for emphasis in normal order does not satisfy a frame that is asking for the subject and the auxiliary to trade places.
Switching to a progressive changes the aspect and leaves the order alone, so the fronted only after is still stranded in front of an uninverted clause.

Why this answer

Nothing here turns on when the pumping happens: all four choices are finite and all four sit in the present, and the one that shifts to a progressive shifts only its aspect. What the sentence is testing is word order. A fronted restrictive phrase such as only after, like a fronted hardly or not until, forces the auxiliary in front of the subject, and when the clause has no auxiliary English supplies does. Three of the four leave the subject first, which is the one thing the frame forbids.

Question

  • Advanced

So badly warped ____ that the park roped off the north loop for a season and pried up every board on it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

This is the sentence with nothing moved, and so badly warped has been moved. A fronted degree phrase cannot sit in front of an untouched clause.
Correct. Fronting so plus an adjective pulls the verb in front of the subject, and the that clause hangs off the whole inverted frame.
The order is still ordinary. Changing the tense does nothing about the fronted so badly warped, which is what the sentence is asking you to answer.
Reaching for an earlier past does not answer the question the frame is asking, which is about position rather than time, and the subject is still standing in front of the verb.

Why this answer

So plus an adjective at the front of a sentence is a fronting like any other, and it carries the same consequence: the verb comes before the subject. The far that clause is a useful confirmation that the fronting is real, since so ... that is one frame rather than two. Only one choice actually moves anything: the other three leave the subject sitting in front of the verb, and two of them change the tense as well, which is beside the point the frame is making.

Question

  • Advanced

The crew resealed the dish's outer panels the week before the ice storm. If they ____ them, the array would have lost a full season of observations.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A plain past would make this an open question about what the crew did, and the sentence before it has already answered that: they resealed the panels.
This says no resealing has happened up to now, which the sentence in front of it flatly contradicts. It is also not the form the remote past condition takes, which is what would have lost has already committed the sentence to.
Correct. The first sentence says the resealing happened, so the if half is about a past that did not occur, and that takes had plus the participle.
Would have is the form the result half wears, and this sentence has already used it there. The if half takes the condition's form, not the result's.

Why this answer

The first sentence is doing the work: it states that the crew did reseal the panels, so the if clause can only be imagining a past that did not happen, and that half takes had plus the past participle. Without that sentence the plain past would be available as an open question about what the crew actually did, which is why it is there. The common error is copying would have into the if half; the two halves never wear the same form.

Question

  • Advanced

In the week after the water fell, the lock-keeper ____ the flood in four dense pages of the journal that the canal trust digitized only last winter.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. In the week after the water fell fixes a finished occasion in the past, and a finished occasion takes the plain past.
The present perfect reaches from the past up to now and takes no adverbial that pins it to a particular past occasion. Has described it in the week after the water fell is the shape English does not build.
The same bar, with an ongoing reading on top of it. The writing filled one week and was over, and the sentence says so.
The main clause has no other verb, so an -ing form leaves the sentence saying nothing about the lock-keeper at all.

Why this answer

The late relative clause is the distraction: digitized only last winter is recent, and it belongs to the trust rather than to the lock-keeper. Read the frame the main clause sets instead. In the week after the water fell names one finished stretch of past time, and an adverbial like that shuts the present perfect out: the perfect measures a span up to now and cannot be pinned to a dated occasion. That leaves the plain past.

Question

  • Advanced

The creamery's oldest delivery route, which a driver penciled onto a county map in 1958 and which the dairy still follows every weekday, ____ eleven farms between the plant and the state line.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

An -ing phrase describes the route without saying anything about it, so the sentence's subject is left with a pile of modifiers and no statement.
An infinitive would make the route something aimed at taking in eleven farms rather than something that does, and it still leaves the main clause without a verb.
Correct. Penciled and follows both belong to relative clauses, so the main clause has not had its verb yet, and this is the only choice that supplies one.
A bare past participle needs an auxiliary in front of it, and there is none. Nothing in the sentence can lend one across two relative clauses.

Why this answer

Two finite verbs have gone by before the blank, penciled and follows, and both are spoken for: each belongs to a which clause describing the route. A relative clause's verb cannot double as the main clause's, so the subject route is still waiting for one. Only takes in is a finite verb, and the length of the sentence is the whole of the disguise.

Question

  • Standard

The first team ran the phone line down to the second chamber, paying the reel out behind them, and then a second crew ____ it into the handset at the cave mouth an hour later.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

It matches paying the reel out, which is the trap: that phrase describes the first team while their clause's verb is ran. After and then a second crew, a describing form leaves the second clause with nothing to report.
An infinitive would state a purpose rather than an event, and the sentence is a record of what the two teams actually did.
A perfect participle can head a phrase that describes, but it cannot be the verb a clause is built on. After and then a second crew, that is the job the sentence needs filled.
Correct. A second clause with a subject of its own needs a finite verb of its own; the first clause's ran is spoken for.

Why this answer

And then a second crew opens a whole clause, not a second half of the first one, so it needs its own finite verb. The participial in the first clause is the disguise: paying the reel out is doing legitimate work there, describing the team whose verb is ran, and matching it in the blank puts a describing form where the sentence needs a reporting one.

Question

  • Advanced

The scoreboard stayed dark through the last three heats, and the cable behind the photo-finish camera turned out to have been ____ loose by a season of vibration in the timing shed.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Shook is the plain past, and the slot after passive been belongs to the past participle, which for this verb is a third word.
Correct. The chain runs have plus been plus the past participle, and the past participle of shake is shaken.
The bare form follows a modal or do, and neither is here. Been shake is not a shape English builds.
An -s ending marks a present-tense verb, and nothing after to have been can be tensed at all. Its own clause has already been given a tense, back on turned out.

Why this answer

The blank sits at the end of a perfect-passive sequence, to have been ____, and the word that decides it is the one right in front: passive been takes the past participle. The by phrase confirms the voice, since it names what did the shaking. So the only question is which form of shake the participle is, and for an irregular verb that is a third word rather than the plain past. Note that nothing in the sequence itself is tensed: the clause's tense sits back on turned out, several words away, which is what makes the shape hard to read and easy to fill in by ear.
