Verb Forms and Tense

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

The blank stands where a verb belongs, and the choices are forms of one verb. Decide what fixes the form: a clause with no verb yet, an auxiliary before the blank, the sentence's time frame, or a fronted phrase.

Key takeaways

  • A clause that makes a statement needs a finite verb, a form that carries tense.
  • An -ing form, a to form or a participle alone cannot be a clause's only verb.
  • After have or passive be the verb is a past participle; after do or a modal, the bare form.
  • Read the time frame from the whole sentence, not from the words beside the blank.
  • A sentence does not switch time frames partway through without a reason it supplies itself.
  • When a restrictive or degree phrase is fronted (hardly, only after, not until, so ... that), the auxiliary comes before the subject.

What the SAT may ask

  • Choose the verb form that completes a clause, or the nonfinite form that does not.
  • Choose the tense the sentence's time words set.
  • Choose the form that follows has, was or did.
  • Choose the tense that reads an event from the past point the sentence names.
  • Choose the word order after a fronted hardly or only after.

The rule

A clause that makes a statement needs a finite verb: a form that carries tense, such as boils, boiled or has boiled. An -ing form, a to form or a participle on its own carries no tense, so the kettle boiling states nothing. In a verb group of two or three words, only the first word carries the tense.

Every clause that makes a statement needs its own finite verb. A verb inside a which clause, a fronted because clause or the first half of an and pair belongs to that clause and cannot serve a second one.

Not every verb-like word is a clause waiting for a verb. Two verbs can share one subject (the press [inks] and [trims] the sheet) or one auxiliary (has been [washed] and [folded]).

A nonfinite phrase is also a legitimate part of a sentence whose main clause already has its verb. In the press, [running] since dawn, [needs] oil, only needs is finite and the sentence is complete. The question is never how many verbs the sentence holds. It is whether the clause the blank sits in already has one.

A cue somewhere in the sentence selects the form.

Cue Form Example
has, have or had before the blank past participle has sung, not has sang
passive be: the subject is acted on past participle was painted by students
progressive be: the subject is doing the action -ing form was painting
do or a modal (did, must, would) bare form did not sing
a dated past occasion: last March simple past the pipes burst
a span reaching the present: since 1970, for a decade now present perfect has lasted
the clause naming where that span opens simple past has lasted since the mill closed
the same span read from a past point: by then, by 1990 past perfect had lasted
a time clause about the future present will sing once the doors open
a fronted restrictive or degree phrase (hardly, only after, not until, so ... that) auxiliary before the subject Not until noon did the store open

A repeating phrase such as every June says the action is a habit; the frame around it says when the habit lived. A past habit takes the plain past or would.

A sentence keeps one time frame unless it gives a reason to change. A standing fact is a reason: the guide explained that the lake thaws each April. Without such a reason, a sentence does not switch frames partway through.

Why the rule exists

English marks time once per verb group, on its first word. So when an auxiliary carries the tense, the main verb is left as a participle or a bare form.

Worked examples

1. Read the time from the opening phrase, not the verb

As things stand now, the deli ____ its chalkboard menu each Friday. Last Friday, the deli ____ its chalkboard menu. (changes / changed)

Task: choose changes or changed in each sentence.

Evidence: As things stand now frames the first sentence as current practice; Last Friday names one finished occasion.

Answer: changes, then changed. The repeating phrase says the action is a habit; the frame around it says when the habit lived.

Closest trap: tense drift, taking each Friday as a license for the present wherever it appears.

2. A span that reaches the present takes the present perfect

The lighthouse ____ ships past the shoals since 1898. (guided / has guided)

Task: choose guided or has guided.

Evidence: since 1898 opens a span that runs to the present, and no past point is named to measure from.

Answer: has guided. The simple past would close a span the sentence holds open.

Closest trap: tense drift in the other half of a since frame. The clause that names the starting point takes the plain past: has guided ships since the county built the jetty, not since the county has built.

3. The past perfect marks a point before a past point

By the time the conductor raised the baton, the stagehands ____ the set. (have finished / had finished)

Task: choose have finished or had finished.

Evidence: by the time the conductor raised the baton names a moment in the past and asks what was already done as of that moment.

Answer: had finished. The past perfect looks back from a past point; the present perfect looks back from now, and now is not the vantage this sentence offers.

Note: the past perfect is not a rule about which of two events came first. By the time already carries the order, so the stagehands finished is grammatical too, which is why it is not among the choices.

Special cases

Requests, wishes, and if clauses that are not true

Ask that and require that take the bare form: asked that the form be signed. Wish takes the past form for a state that is not so, and for be that form is were with every subject.

A contrary-to-fact if takes the past form for a present unreality, had plus the participle for a past one. Its result clause takes would, could or might, with have added for the past. Read the form off the half already written. A fronted Had is that if dropped: Had the bridge been closed means if the bridge had been closed.

Common traps

Traps on verb-form items

Trap Correction
tense drift Read the time words in the whole sentence, then match the frame they set.
missing finite verb Check that the blank's own clause has a verb with tense; another clause's verb does not count.
inversion missed After a fronted restrictive or degree phrase, or a dropped if, put the auxiliary before the subject.
attractor noun Agree the auxiliary with the subject, not with the noun beside the blank.

A form that sounds complete beside the blank can still break the whole sentence.

The method: Find what fixes the form

  1. Read the choices first and name what changes: tense, finite against nonfinite forms, mood, or word order. A set that moves the subject and the auxiliary past each other is about position, not time.
  2. Find what fixes the form in the whole sentence. It will be a time phrase, an auxiliary before the blank, or will in the main clause. It can also be a request, a wish, a contrary-to-fact if, or a fronted hardly, only after, not until or Had.
  3. Ask three questions, in this order. Does this part of the sentence need a finite verb, or does its clause already have one? Does an auxiliary or a trigger fix the form? What time relationship does the sentence establish, read from the point it names?
  4. Pick the form that meets every demand, then reread the sentence. Check the subject too: an auxiliary agrees with it, not with the noun beside the blank.

Last January, in the hardest week of the freeze, the wooden water tanks on the building's rooftop ____ solid for the first time in years.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: tense drift. Freeze is a present habit, but Last January places this freezing at one finished moment in the past.
Correct. Last January names a finished past occasion, and a finished past occasion takes the simple past: the tanks froze.
Trap: missing finite verb. Freezing carries no tense, so the tanks freezing solid describes the tanks and never states that anything happened.
Trap: tense drift. Have frozen ties the event to the present, but Last January seals it inside a finished past.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices are one verb in finite and nonfinite forms. Step 2: the only time phrase is Last January, at the front of the sentence. Step 3: it names one finished past occasion, not a habit and not a span reaching now. Step 4: froze; reread with it in place. Freezing leaves the clause no finite verb, and both present forms break the frame.

Practice 2

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Introductory

The bell over the schoolhouse door, which ____ a full minute behind the classroom clocks until its wiring was replaced, now keeps better time than the clocks do.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: missing finite verb. Ringing carries no tense, so which ringing a full minute behind never states anything about the bell.
Trap: missing finite verb. An infinitive names an action without stating it, so which to ring leaves the clause with no verb.
Correct. A which clause is a clause, so it needs a finite verb of its own, and rang is the only finite form offered.
Trap: missing finite verb. Rung is the past participle, the form that follows has or had, and no auxiliary is here to carry it.

The method, applied

Step 1: one finite form and three nonfinite ones, so the axis is finiteness, not tense. Step 2: what fixes the form is the which that opens the blank's clause. Step 3: the sentence's other finite verb, keeps, belongs to the main clause, so the which clause still needs one of its own. Step 4: rang is the only finite choice; reread with it in place.

Practice 3

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Standard

For more than a century now, the mint that supplies the island's banks ____ its coins from dies cut entirely by hand.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. For more than a century now is a span still open at the present, which takes the present perfect, and the singular mint takes has.
Trap: tense drift. Struck closes the span, but the anchor's own now holds it open to the present.
Trap: tense drift. Strikes reports only the present and drops the century the sentence is measuring.
Trap: attractor noun. Have agrees with banks, but banks sits inside the that clause; the subject is the singular mint.

The method, applied

Step 1: one verb in three tenses plus a number error, so tense is the main axis and agreement the second. Step 2: the time phrase is for more than a century now, and the present supplies confirms that the arrangement still holds. Step 3: a span open at the present takes the present perfect. Step 4: the subject is the singular mint, so has struck; have answers to banks, the noun beside the blank. Reread with it in place.

Practice 4

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Standard

When the labeling line jammed halfway through the night shift, the fruit cannery's crew ____ the conveyors before unlabeled cans could pile up at the packing tables.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: tense drift. Stops is present inside a story told in the past: the line jammed, one night, once.
Trap: tense drift. Has stopped reaches toward the present, but the jam, the shift and the crew's response all sit inside one past night.
Trap: missing finite verb. Stopping leaves the main clause with no finite verb, so the whole sentence becomes a fragment.
Correct. The opening clause's jammed sets a finished past frame, and the crew's response belongs to the same night, so it takes the simple past: stopped.

The method, applied

Step 1: one verb in present, present perfect, nonfinite and simple past forms, so the axis is tense plus finish. Step 2: there is no date and no span; the frame is the opening clause's jammed, a full clause from the blank. Step 3: the stopping was the response to the jam, inside the same night, so it sits at the same finished past moment. Step 4: stopped; reread with it in place. The present forms switch the frame with no reason, and stopping leaves the main clause with no verb.

Practice 5

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Advanced

Hardly ____ the season's first cars at the grain elevator when word came down the line that the junction would close for good in November.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: inversion missed. A fronted hardly moves the auxiliary in front of the subject, so hardly the crew had loaded leaves the fronted word stranded.
Correct. Hardly ... when stacks two past events, so the earlier one takes the past perfect, and the fronted hardly puts had before the subject: hardly had the crew loaded.
Trap: tense drift. Did obeys the inversion but drops the sequence: hardly ... when puts the loading before the news, and the earlier event takes the past perfect.
Trap: tense drift. Loads is present beside the past came, and it also leaves the fronted hardly without the inversion it requires.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices move one verb through two word orders and three forms, so the axis is double: position, and which past. Step 2: the fixes are the fronted hardly, its paired when, and the past came. Step 3: a fronted hardly puts the auxiliary before the subject, and hardly ... when puts the loading just before the news, so the earlier event takes the past perfect. Step 4: had the crew loaded meets both demands; reread with it in place. Normal order strands hardly, did drops the sequence, and loads breaks the past frame. Two demands in one blank is what makes this advanced.

Practice 6

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Advanced

Had the miller not replaced the windmill's rotted canvas sails before the autumn gales, the first hard storm ____ them from their frames within the hour.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: tense drift. Had the miller not replaced is a dropped-if clause, so the past perfect belongs to that half. Repeating it in the result half puts the if clause's form where the consequence goes, and the consequence takes would have.
Trap: tense drift. The opening clause is a conditional, so the result clause states what would have followed, not what did. A plain past turns the second half into a report of something that happened.
Correct. Had the miller not replaced sets up a past that did not happen, and the result clause of a past counterfactual takes would have plus the participle: would have torn.
Trap: missing finite verb. Tearing leaves the result clause with no finite verb, so the sentence never states the outcome.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices run from the plain past to a would have form, so the axis is the shape of the conditional, plus finiteness. Step 2: the fix is the opening inversion. Had the miller not replaced is if the miller had not replaced with the if dropped, which is the past unreal conditional. Step 3: that structure settles the form: the if half takes had plus the participle, the result half a past modal plus have. Step 4: would have torn is the only such form offered. Tore would make the second half a report rather than a consequence, a second had puts the if half's form in the result half, and tearing leaves no verb.
