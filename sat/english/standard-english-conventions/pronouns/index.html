Pronouns

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

Identify what the pronoun refers to, then choose a form compatible with it. Number comes from the referent; case comes from the pronoun's own job in its clause.

Key takeaways

  • Find what the pronoun refers to and match its number. A referent can be one noun, a noun phrase, or two nouns joined by and.
  • Number comes from the referent. Case comes from the pronoun's job in its own clause.
  • Answer the clause with he or him: a he answer takes who, a him answer takes whom.
  • Each of, every one of, neither of and either of are singular. Both of is plural.
  • Its owns the noun after it. It's expands to it is or it has.
  • Singular they is standard English. One person takes they are and they have, never they is or they has.

What the SAT may ask

  • Choose it or they for a distant antecedent.
  • Choose who, whom, or whose inside a relative clause.
  • Choose its against it's, or their against they're.
  • Choose the case of a pronoun beside a noun in one slot.
  • Choose a pronoun after a counting word such as neither of.

The rule

A pronoun takes its number from its referent, the thing it stands for. Usually that is a noun printed in the sentence, its antecedent. It can also be a noun phrase or two nouns joined by and, and in it is raining the it stands for nothing.

An antecedent can sit a clause away, and after an opening subordinate clause it can come after its own pronoun.

Number is a fact about the noun, not about the count: scissors names one tool and takes they.

Case is a separate decision from a separate source: the pronoun's own job in its own clause, subject, object, or possessor. A preposition standing to the left of a whole clause decides nothing.

Each of, every one of, neither of and either of are singular where they head the subject. Both of takes the two together and is plural.

In a pair joined by or or nor, the half nearer the pronoun controls it. An each that has slipped past the subject to sit beside the verb leaves a plural subject plural.

A reflexive needs the subject of its own clause to point back at. The subject of an infinitive takes the object form after expect, want, or ask.

A relative pronoun attaches to a noun already named. Who and whom take a person and which a thing; whose and that serve both, so the dam whose apron cracked is as ordinary as the engineer whose report it was.

Whoever brings its own referent: read it as anyone who, and take it where a whole clause fills a noun's slot. Who also opens a question inside a sentence (the clerk asked who had signed). So a missing noun before the blank does not by itself call for whoever.

In edited writing whom is the object form and the SAT keys it. An object who is ordinary in speech and is not an error there.

A supplementary clause added after a comma attaches with a relative pronoun. A personal pronoun there leaves two sentences spliced.

Why the rule exists

A pronoun is a short way of pointing back at something already said. Number and case are what let a reader point it at the right thing.

Worked examples

1. Match the pronoun to what the verb is true of

The dishwasher holds two racks of glasses and plates, and even on the longest cycle ____ can still wash a full load before nine. (it / they)

Task: choose it or they for the blank.

Evidence: something washes a full load of dishes. The dishwasher washes. The glasses and the plates are what gets washed.

Answer: it, singular to match the singular dishwasher.

Closest trap: attractor noun. Racks of glasses and plates sits next to the blank and is plural, and nothing in it washes a load.

2. Read the number off the counting word

Every one of the printers along the copy room's back wall keeps ____ own error log. (its / their)

Task: choose its or their after every one of.

Evidence: every one of counts the group one member at a time, so it is singular. The of-phrase names the group being counted, not the noun being counted. The verb keeps is singular too.

Answer: its.

Note: both of is the counting word that goes the other way. It takes the two together and is plural.

3. Strip the compound to hear the case

The landlord thanked my roommate and ____ for repainting the stairwell. (I / me)

Task: choose I or me.

Evidence: the pronoun is one of the two people the landlord thanked, so it is an object of thanked. Strip the other half: the landlord thanked me.

Answer: me.

Closest trap: pronoun case by ear. And I sounds more careful, and sharing a slot with a noun does not change a pronoun's case.

Special cases

Fractions and portion words

After a fraction or a portion word (half, most, some, the rest), the of-phrase decides the subject's number. Leave it standing when you strip.

Most of the ceiling has lost its plaster, but most of the tiles have lost their grout. A counting word takes its number from itself, not from the of-phrase.

Common traps

Four ways a pronoun choice goes wrong

Trap Correction
attractor noun Match the noun the verb or the ownership is true of
pronoun case by ear Read the pronoun's job inside its own clause
possessive-contraction swap Expand the apostrophe: it's has to mean it is or it has
wrong category Ask what the form needs: a noun after it, or a clause to open

The method: Trace the antecedent

  1. Read the choices and name the axis. They vary on number (it against they), case (who against whom), or category (who against which, its against it's).
  2. Find the antecedent, if the pronoun has one. Ask what noun you would write into the blank if pronouns were banned. That noun controls the number. In an or or nor pair, the nearer half decides.
  3. Set aside what stands between them. Cross out prepositional phrases, appositives, and relative or subordinate clauses, but leave the of phrase after a fraction or a portion word: there it is what decides the number. Cover a reporting clause dropped inside a relative one (the muralist who the curator insists painted it). Then check that no second noun can claim the pronoun.
  4. Match number, then case, then reread. Number comes from the antecedent. Case comes from the pronoun's job in its own clause. Read the whole sentence once with the survivor in place.

Practice 1

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Introductory

The public aquarium's kelp tank keeps hundreds of amber fronds in constant motion, and once a month ____ must be drained so that divers can service the surge machine.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The pronoun stands for the tank, the singular thing that gets drained, so it takes the singular it.
Trap: attractor noun. They agrees with the fronds, the plural noun nearest the blank, but the fronds are not what gets drained. The tank is, and it is singular.
Trap: possessive-contraction swap. The apostrophe makes this the contraction of it is, so the sentence would read once a month it is must be drained.
Trap: wrong category. Its is a possessive and needs a noun after it, and this blank is a subject slot with a verb to carry.

The method, applied

Step 1: the axis is number, it against they, with a contraction and a bare possessive alongside. Step 2: ask what must be drained. The tank must; the fronds only sway inside it. Step 3: strip hundreds of amber fronds in constant motion. Step 4: tank is singular and the blank is a subject slot, so it.

Practice 2

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Introductory

The rowing club's new coach, ____ racks every shell in the boathouse before locking up each night, expects the whole team to do the same.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: wrong category. Which refers to things, and a coach is a person. A supplementary clause about a person opens with who or whom, not which.
Trap: pronoun case by ear. Whom sounds more formal, but case comes from the pronoun's job, and this pronoun is the subject of racks.
Trap: wrong category. Whose is a possessive and must own the noun after it, which would leave the clause with no verb.
Correct. The pronoun stands for the coach, a person, and inside its clause it is the subject performing racks, so it takes the subject form.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices are four relative pronouns, so the axis is person against thing, then case. Step 2: the antecedent is the coach, a person, which rules out the thing-pronoun. Step 3: nothing stands between the antecedent and the blank. Step 4: inside its clause the pronoun performs racks, and she racks every shell is a subject answer, so who.

Practice 3

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Standard

Neither of the two buoys marking the harbor channel has drifted from ____ mooring since the spring survey.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: attractor noun. Their agrees with buoys, inside the of-phrase, but the referent is neither, which is singular, and has drifted has already said so.
Correct. The referent is neither, singular however many buoys follow it, so the possessive is the singular its.
Trap: possessive-contraction swap. This is the contraction of it is, and drifted from it is mooring collapses on the reread. The possessive of it has no apostrophe.
Trap: wrong category. Theirs replaces an owned noun (the mooring is theirs) and cannot sit in front of one, and a plural would answer to the wrong referent.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices vary a possessive on number and form, so the question is whose mooring. Step 2: the referent is the counting word neither, not buoys. Step 3: strip of the two buoys marking the harbor channel, which leaves neither has drifted from ____ mooring. Step 4: neither is singular, so the singular possessive its, and the singular verb has drifted confirms the number. Reread the whole sentence with its in place.

Practice 4

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Standard

At the state debate tournament, the timekeeper, ____ the judges brief before every round, signals the last thirty seconds with a raised red card.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: pronoun case by ear. The judges do the briefing and the pronoun receives it: they brief him, so edited writing takes whom here. An object who is ordinary in speech, and this question asks for the edited form.
Trap: wrong category. Which points at things, and a timekeeper is a person, so this clause cannot open with it.
Correct. Inside its clause the pronoun is the direct object of brief: the judges brief him, and an object answer takes the object form.
Trap: wrong category. Whose must own the noun after it, and the next words are the judges, who belong to no one.

The method, applied

Step 1: four relative pronouns again, so the axis is person against thing, then case. Step 2: the antecedent is the timekeeper, a person, so the thing-pronoun is out. Step 3: the pronoun opens the interrupting clause, and its case has to be read inside that clause rather than in the main sentence. Step 4: answer the clause with he or him. The judges brief him, an object answer, so whom.

Practice 5

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Advanced

The dam was finished in 1936, and although the sluice screens were replaced after last spring's flood and the new pivots and hinges are greased every autumn, ____ concrete apron has never been resurfaced.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: attractor noun. Their agrees with screens, pivots or hinges, the plural nouns in the interrupting clause. The only thing in the sentence the apron can belong to is the dam, and the dam is singular.
Trap: wrong category. Whose is a relative possessive and opens a new clause, and this slot needs a plain possessive determiner inside a finished one.
Trap: possessive-contraction swap. Expand the apostrophe and the sentence reads it is concrete apron has never been resurfaced. The possessive of it is its.
Correct. The apron belongs to the dam, the sentence's only singular candidate and the thing it opens by naming, so the possessive is singular.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices vary a possessive on number and form, with a relative pronoun as a fourth decoy, so the question is whose apron. Step 2: ask what has a concrete apron. The dam does, and it is named in the first four words. The singular nouns inside the although clause name a flood and two seasons, none of which has an apron poured against it. Step 3: strip that clause, running from although to every autumn, and the three plural nouns leave with it. Step 4: dam is singular and a noun follows the blank, so the singular possessive determiner its.

Practice 6

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Advanced

Before the season opener, the head groundskeeper walked the outfield with Renata Vaz, an assistant the club had hired midseason, and then set a timed drill requiring the new hires and ____ to strip the infield tarp in under five minutes.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Strip the compound and the frame is a drill requiring ____ to strip the tarp, so the pronoun is an object of requiring and takes the object form.
Trap: pronoun case by ear. The subject form sounds like the polite pick in a compound, but requiring she to strip the tarp fails as soon as the other half is stripped.
Trap: wrong category. Hers replaces a noun that is owned (the clipboard is hers), and the slot needs a plain object pronoun naming one of the people the drill requires.
Trap: wrong category. A reflexive needs a subject in its own clause to point back at, and the requiring is done by the drill rather than by Renata Vaz.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices run one pronoun through four cases, so the axis is case. Step 2: the antecedent is Renata Vaz, named a clause earlier and separated from the blank by her own appositive. Step 3: cross out the appositive, then cross out the new hires and, and the frame is a drill requiring ____ to strip the infield tarp. Step 4: the pronoun is an object of requiring, and the drill required her, never required she. Reread with her in place.
