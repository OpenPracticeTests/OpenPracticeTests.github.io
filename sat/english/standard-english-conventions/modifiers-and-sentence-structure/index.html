Modifiers and Sentence Structure

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

An opening description needs the main clause to give it the right subject. The members of a pair or a series each need to work in the slot they share.

Key takeaways

  • An opening phrase that describes somebody or something, set off by a comma, describes the main clause's subject, unless it carries its own subject. A time or place phrase that names no doer describes the event instead.
  • A phrase at the end of a sentence is a separate question: it may describe the subject, or rename the noun beside it.
  • Owning the doer is not being the doer: read to the subject's head noun.
  • The subject must perform what the phrase names, undergo it, or fit its description.
  • Members of a pair or a series must each work in the slot the shared words give them.
  • A word written once has to reach every member of the list.
  • Both halves of a correlative pair take the same kind of member.

What the SAT may ask

  • Choose the subject an opening modifier can describe.
  • Choose the modifier that fits the subject already given.
  • Complete a series so every member matches.
  • Complete a correlative such as not only ... but also.
  • Choose the form a shared auxiliary or preposition can reach.

The rule

A modifier describes what its position points at. An opening phrase that describes somebody or something, set off by a comma, describes the main clause's subject. The subject has to be the thing the phrase names, so a subject that cannot be it is the error.

Two kinds of opener ask nothing of the subject. One carries its own subject, which makes it an absolute phrase: in the kettle still hissing, she lifted the lid, the kettle hisses and she need not. The other describes the whole event, such as a time or place phrase that names no doer (The night before the fair, the twins split the work).

Opening modifier It names So the subject must be
Sanding between coats, the one who sands the person sanding
Once sanded smooth, the thing sanded the sanded thing
A lifelong sailor, the one described the person described
After trimming the wick, the one who trims the person trimming
Bright red and still warm, the thing described the thing described

A phrase at the other end of the sentence is a separate question. A trailing appositive renames the noun it follows: in We visited Kyoto, an ancient capital, the capital is Kyoto, not us. A trailing participial usually does describe the subject (She left early, hoping to beat the rain).

So read what a trailing phrase sits next to, and what it could sensibly be about. Punctuation Within Sentences covers the marks these take.

A phrase carrying no comma is a third case. I found the letter in the attic has two grammatical readings, so no question can turn on one.

Items joined in a pair or a series must be able to fill the same slot. That usually shows up as one grammatical form, all gerunds or all infinitives or all clauses. But the endings are the symptom, not the rule. What matters is that each member works where the shared words put it.

A word written once and shared across the members has to reach every one of them. It may sit in front (a subject, an auxiliary, a to, a governing verb, a correlative's first half) or behind, as an object they all point at.

Series Members Verdict
to file, to sort, and to bind infinitive, infinitive, infinitive matched
to file, to sort, and binding infinitive, infinitive, gerund broken
can be filed and bound can be reaches both members matched
can be filed and binding can be cannot reach binding broken

Why the rule exists

English marks who does what mostly by position, so a reader hands a describing phrase to the subject automatically.

Worked examples

1. Match the opener to the subject

Hurrying to catch the last bus, my duffel bag split at the seam.

Task: check whether the subject can be what the opener names.

Evidence: Hurrying to catch the last bus names a person who hurries. The subject is my duffel bag, and a bag does not hurry.

Answer: the sentence is wrong. The fix: Hurrying to catch the last bus, I felt the duffel bag split at the seam.

Closest trap: dangling modifier, which the guessable meaning hides.

2. A possessive is not the doer

Studying for the midterm all weekend, Maria's notes spread across every table in the apartment.

Task: find the head noun of the subject.

Evidence: Studying for the midterm names the person who studied, and that is Maria. The subject is Maria's notes, whose head noun is notes. Notes get studied; they do not study.

Answer: the sentence is wrong. Put the studier in the subject slot: Studying for the midterm all weekend, Maria spread her notes across every table.

Closest trap: dangling modifier, hidden by the right name sitting one apostrophe away.

3. Match the third member to the first two

The campground's new water heater promises to light instantly, to hold its temperature, and running longer than the old one.

Task: give the series one grammatical form.

Evidence: The first two members are infinitives, to light and to hold. The word written once is promises, and promises running longer does not hold.

Answer: to run longer than the old one, which promises reaches like the other two.

Closest trap: broken parallelism, which reads smoothly because the meaning is clear either way.

Special cases

Three that look wrong and are not, and two that look right and are not

An imperative supplies an unwritten you: before starting the mower, close the fuel valve. That you is the doer, so no noun follows the comma.

A passive is right where the opener names what was acted on: sealed in wax, the jars were stacked in the cellar.

An absolute phrase brings its own subject, so it is not looking for one in the main clause.

The other two go the other way. An infinitive of purpose still needs the doer in the subject slot: To qualify for the discount, the form must be postmarked leaves the person who qualifies unstated, and the fix is applicants must postmark the form.

A dummy subject cannot be the doer. There and an empty it name nothing, so Having checked the gauges, there was no reason to wait has no one who checked.

Common traps

Three ways a modifier or a series goes wrong

Trap Correction
dangling modifier Name what the phrase is about, then demand that noun as the subject
broken parallelism Read each member alone with the word written once
missing finite verb Check that the main clause reaches a tensed verb

The method: Match the modifier to what it describes

  1. Read the choices and name the axis. If each choice gives the main clause a different subject, the question is what the opening description describes. If one action appears in several shapes, the question is how the list members fit.
  2. Identify what the opening description describes. Ask who performs its action, undergoes it, or fits it, and answer with a noun. A modifier carrying its own subject asks nothing, and one at the end may be renaming the noun beside it instead.
  3. Find the subject immediately after it, and check that they match. Read to the subject's head noun. Owning the thing is not being it, and there or an empty it is nothing at all.
  4. For a list, read each member alone with the words it shares, then reread the sentence. Put each member behind the word written once. A member that cannot work in that slot is the broken one.

Carrying the storm windows down from the attic each fall, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: dangling modifier. The windows are the cargo rather than the carrier, and windows do not carry themselves down a stairwell.
Trap: dangling modifier. The right person is present only as an owner, and the head noun of the subject is ladder, which carried nothing.
Correct. Only a person can carry storm windows down from an attic, and the caretaker is the subject the opener describes.
Trap: missing finite verb. Carried here is a participle, so the main clause never reaches a tensed verb and says nothing.

The method, applied

Step 1: every choice gives the main clause a different subject. Step 2: carrying the storm windows down from the attic names a person who carries. Step 3: the subject has to be that person. The windows are the cargo, the ladder is only owned by the right person, and the fourth choice never reaches a tensed verb. Step 4: no list here.

Practice 2

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Introductory

The night before the science fair, the twins split the work on their model volcano into two jobs: mixing the plaster and ____ the cone so it could dry overnight.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The pair reads mixing the plaster and shaping the cone: two gerunds in matching form, naming the two jobs the sentence promised.
Trap: broken parallelism. An infinitive can name a job (to mix and to shape), but not opposite a gerund: the pair opened on mixing, and and joins members that fill the slot alike.
Trap: broken parallelism. This is a whole clause with its own subject and tense, and the slot after the colon is filled by names for the jobs, not by statements about them.
Trap: broken parallelism. A bare verb form fills no slot here at all: nothing in the sentence gives shape a subject, so the second job is never named.

The method, applied

Step 1: one action in four shapes, so the axis is how the two members fit. Steps 2 and 3 belong to a modifier question, and this is a list. Step 4: read each member behind two jobs. The pair opened on the gerund mixing the plaster, so its partner has to fill that slot the same way, and only shaping does.

Practice 3

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Standard

Once retired from the climbing wall for a worn core, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: dangling modifier. The setters do the cutting rather than undergo the retirement, and the rope itself appears only as it.
Trap: dangling modifier. The head noun is inspection, which is an event, and an event is not the thing taken off the wall.
Trap: dangling modifier. The tag is added after the retirement rather than undergoing it, and the opener cannot reach past that subject.
Correct. Only a rope can be retired from the wall for a worn core, and a rope is the subject the opener describes.

The method, applied

Step 1: the choices vary in what follows the opener, so this is a placement item. Step 2: once retired from the climbing wall for a worn core names the thing that was retired, and a rope is retired for a worn core. Step 3: read to the head noun of each subject. Setters do the cutting, an inspection is an event, and a tag is added afterward, so none of the three came off the wall. Only a rope did. Step 4: no list here.

Practice 4

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Standard

Each new volunteer at the community radio station's record library learns three jobs in a single shift: to catalog the donation bin's arrivals, to sleeve them in fresh jackets, and ____ them alphabetically by label.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: broken parallelism. The series opened with the infinitives to catalog and to sleeve, and a gerund names the same job in a different form.
Correct. To catalog, to sleeve, and to shelve wear one form, so all three infinitives hang off learns three jobs.
Trap: broken parallelism. A subject and a finite verb burst into a list of phrases, and no comma pattern makes that a series.
Trap: broken parallelism. Having shelved points backward at a job already done, and it wears a participle where the series hands out infinitives.

The method, applied

Step 1: one action in four shapes, so the axis is how the list members fit. Steps 2 and 3 belong to a modifier question, and this is a list. Step 4: the series runs to catalog, to sleeve, and ____, and the words written once are learns three jobs. Read each member alone after them: learns three jobs, to shelve works, and only the infinitive does.

Practice 5

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Advanced

____, the ropemaker judged the line's evenness by feel alone.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Trap: dangling modifier. A bare participial phrase hands its action to the subject, and the ropemaker twists yarns rather than twisting into a strand.
Trap: dangling modifier. Changing the participle's form changes nothing about what it attaches to, and the ropemaker was not twisted into anything.
Correct. This opener carries its own subject, the tarred yarns, so it asks nothing of the main clause and the ropemaker may follow it.
Trap: dangling modifier. A perfect participle is still a participle, so it hands the becoming to the subject after the comma.

The method, applied

Step 1: the main clause is fixed and the four choices are four openers, so the question runs backward: which modifier can this subject carry? Step 2: ask what twists into an even strand. The yarns do, and the ropemaker twists them, which is a different claim. Three choices are bare participial phrases, so each hands that becoming to the subject. The tarred yarns twisting into an even strand carries its own subject instead, which makes it an absolute phrase. Step 3: the question is already answered, so there is nothing to check at the comma. Step 4: no list here.

Practice 6

  • Standard English Conventions
  • Form, Structure, and Sense
  • Advanced

Planted two centuries ago to divide two fields, ____ but also shades livestock through the summer.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. The hedgerow is the thing that was planted, and not only marks the boundary but also shades livestock hangs matching verb phrases on both halves.
Trap: broken parallelism. Not only the boundary promises a second noun, and the stem delivers the verb shades instead.
Trap: broken parallelism. The inverted not only does the hedgerow mark demands a full clause after but also, and the stem's shades has no subject.
Trap: dangling modifier. Nobody planted a boundary, and this choice buries the hedgerow in a by phrase where the opener cannot reach it.

The method, applied

Step 1: every choice rearranges not only, so the axis is double: which subject follows the opener, and what shape the correlative joins. Step 2: planted two centuries ago to divide two fields names the thing planted, which is the hedgerow. Step 3: the subject has to be the hedgerow, and the boundary was drawn rather than planted. Step 4: the stem's second half is but also shades livestock, a bare verb phrase, so not only has to carry a verb phrase too. Reread: one subject and two matched verbs.
