Question

  • Introductory

By planting beachgrass in rows along the crest each October, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Only people can plant beachgrass in rows, and the crew stands first after the comma, so the opener describes the one thing on offer that could have done the planting.
Storms are what the planting defends against, and no storm ever planted anything. The opener needs a planter in the subject slot and finds the weather.
The planters are in this choice, but only as owners. The noun the opener grabs is rows, and rows of grass do not plant themselves.
The subject is holding the sand in place, an activity rather than a planter, so the opener ends up describing something that cannot hold a trowel.

Why this answer

An opening phrase describes whatever noun follows its comma, and by planting beachgrass needs a planter. Ask who planted, then demand that name in the subject slot: a storm, rows of grass, and an activity are all things the planting was aimed at, made of, or consisted of rather than things that could do it. Only the crew is left.

Question

  • Introductory

A meadow kept for hay can either ____ or stand until the seed has dropped and the goldfinches have taken it.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A modal takes a bare verb after it, and can being cut is not a form English has.
Correct. Can governs both halves of the pair, and it takes the bare form on each: can either be cut ... or stand.
The to belongs to an infinitive, and can does not take one. Can to be cut collapses on its own.
A full clause with its own subject and verb cannot be the first half of the pair, because can is still waiting to govern something and this choice hands it a sentence.

Why this answer

Either and or promise two members that fit one slot, and here the slot is set by a word neither member contains: the modal can. What follows or is the bare stand, so what follows either has to be a bare verb too. Read the sentence with the modal in place and three of the four choices come apart at can.

Question

  • Introductory

Fluorescent orange and stiff with the reflective tape sewn along its hem, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The opener describes whatever follows the comma, and here that is drivers. A person has no hem, and nothing in the sentence is sewn to a driver.
Correct. The vest is the thing with the tape along its hem, and it stands directly after the comma, so the description lands on what it describes.
Fog is what the vest has to be seen through, and fog is neither orange nor stiff nor in possession of a hem.
A policy can be new and can take effect, and it has no hem to sew tape along, because it has no physical form. The opener describes something you could hold up to the light.

Why this answer

A fronted adjective phrase works exactly as a fronted participial does: it describes the noun after the comma, whatever the writer meant. So the question is which of the four subjects can be orange and stiff and have a hem. A person and a bank of fog cannot, and a policy is not the sort of thing that could be; the vest can be all three.

Question

  • Introductory

The cabinet by the community center's side door holds a defibrillator, a sealed pack of pads, and ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A participial phrase describes something rather than naming it, so it cannot be the third thing the cabinet holds. It is looking for a noun to attach to, and every noun in reach already has its own place in the list.
An infinitive names an action rather than an object, and the first two members are objects. A cabinet cannot hold to print.
Correct. Three noun phrases in a row, all of them things the cabinet holds.
A prepositional phrase is not a member of the list at all; it is a modifier looking for something to attach to, and holds ... and with the instructions leaves it nothing.

Why this answer

Every member of a series answers the same question about the same word, and here that word is holds: each item names something in the cabinet. Two noun phrases are already in place, so the third has to be a noun phrase too. A participle, an infinitive, and a prepositional phrase are three different ways of not being one, and none of them can be held.

Question

  • Introductory

A retired shop teacher, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

An appositive renames the noun beside it, and the noun beside this one is cart. A cart is not a retired shop teacher.
The wheels are no more a retired shop teacher than the cart is, and moving the opener's target one noun further along does not make it a person.
Correct. The opener names a person, and a person stands directly after the comma for it to rename.
The subject is welding the portage cart together, a job rather than the person who did it, and a job cannot have retired from teaching shop.

Why this answer

A noun phrase set off at the front of a sentence is an appositive: it renames whatever follows the comma. So the test is whether that noun could be called a retired shop teacher. A cart, a set of wheels, and a winter's work could not; only the person who did the welding can, and this is the one choice that puts a person first.

Question

  • Introductory

A diner's fryer baskets warp not from the oil's heat but from ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A clause with its own subject and verb cannot sit where the oil's heat sits. From they go has nothing to join the two halves on.
An infinitive names an action, and the half it has to match names a thing. From to be dropped is not a shape the pair can balance.
A bare past participle is a describing word looking for a noun, so this half leaves from holding a modifier with nothing to modify.
Correct. From the oil's heat and from the cold rinse sink fill both halves of the pair the same way: a preposition and the thing it points at.

Why this answer

A not ... but pair works like a scale: whatever shape you hang on one side has to hang on the other, and here the sentence makes that mechanical by writing from on both. The first half is the oil's heat, a noun phrase naming what does the warping, so the second half has to be a noun phrase naming the same kind of thing. Read each choice with the from in front of it and only one of the four balances the pair.

Question

  • Standard

Since the flood the youth theater's load-in has run neither ____ nor across the lobby carpet, and the crew now carries every flat up the fire stairs.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Across the lobby carpet is a prepositional phrase, so the half in front of nor has to be one too.
A clause with its own subject and verb cannot pair with a prepositional phrase, and the sentence already has a verb waiting to govern both halves.
A participial phrase is not a prepositional phrase, so neither using the alley nor across the carpet mismatches the pair however sensible each half is alone. A correlative is where the mismatch is loudest, because the two markers promise a matched set.
A passive clause is a clause, and it leaves has run with nothing to run through and neither with nothing to balance against nor.

Why this answer

The correlative promises matching parts, and the part that fixes the shape is the one that has not been written yet: across the lobby carpet, a preposition and its object, sitting on the far side of the blank. That is the whole of the difficulty here, since the model arrives after the slot it governs rather than before it. Give neither and its partner members of one kind, and the three rejected choices turn out to be three ways of making half a pair into a sentence.

Question

  • Standard

Before loading the autoclave's second rack, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

A rack is what gets loaded, not what does the loading. The opener needs the person with the tray, and this choice offers the shelf they are sliding it onto.
A checklist loads nothing, and the head noun of the lab's posted checklist is checklist. Naming the lab in a possessive does not put anybody in the subject slot.
The subject is checking the drain screen, a step rather than a person, so the opener describes something with no hands.
Correct. An imperative addresses a reader, so its unwritten subject is you, and you are the one loading the second rack.

Why this answer

An opening phrase still needs a doer, but a command supplies one without printing it: the understood subject of check the drain screen is you, and you are the person loading the rack. So the modifier lands. The three rejected choices all print a subject, and none of them, a rack, a checklist, or a step, can load anything.

Question

  • Standard

By the end of the week every string in the hop yard has been stripped of its bine and ____ for the winter.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Has been coiling points the wrong way: it would say the string did the coiling, and the string is what got coiled.
Has been can take an infinitive elsewhere (her goal has been to qualify), but not here: stripped has already made it the perfect passive, so the half joined to it has to be a past participle too.
Correct. Has been is written once and governs both halves, and what it takes is the past participle: has been stripped ... and coiled.
Has been coil is not a form English builds. Read instead as a second finite verb and it fails the other way, since every string takes coils.

Why this answer

Find the word the two halves share before you weigh either of them. Has been is written once and reaches both, so each half has to be a form it can govern, which for the passive is the past participle. Stripped already is. Push has been forward in front of each choice in turn and only one of the four survives the reading.

Question

  • Standard

____, sliding each viewing slot shut on the way past.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. A phrase set off at the end of a clause reaches back for that clause's subject first, and the volunteers are the only candidate here with hands to slide a slot with.
The subject is slots, so the trailing phrase has the slots sliding themselves shut one at a time as they walk past.
A blind is a building. The trailing phrase would have it closing its own slots and moving along the outside of itself.
The heron is what the slots are there to watch, and it has no hands. Nothing about a bird taking off closes a viewing slot behind it.

Why this answer

A participial phrase set off by a comma at the end of a sentence puts the clause's subject in reach, across any number of nouns standing closer, and that is where a reader starts. It is a default rather than a law, and here nothing competes with it: ask which of these subjects could be sliding the slots shut and moving on, and slots cannot, a building cannot, and a bird cannot. Only the volunteers can, and the choice that names them has to put them in the subject slot rather than anywhere else.

Question

  • Standard

The breed the urban farm keeps in its two goat pens is hardy, patient with visiting children, and ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Three descriptions of the same animal, all of them adjective phrases hanging off one is.
Which opens a relative clause, and a relative clause describes a noun rather than completing is. The series would run is hardy, patient, and which leads.
Leads easily is a real thing to say about an animal, and that is the trap. The shared is links hardy and patient to the subject, and leading would need it as a progressive auxiliary instead. One is cannot do both jobs at once.
The breed is with no trouble on a halter is not a sentence English builds. Some prepositional phrases do follow is as descriptions (the breed is in good health), but this one names how the animal behaves when led, and a manner phrase cannot fill the slot hardy and patient are filling.

Why this answer

The three members all answer the breed is what?, so each has to work in that slot as a description of the animal. Hardy and patient with visiting children do. Watch the second member: it ends in a prepositional phrase, and that makes a bare prepositional phrase look like a match, when what the series is actually built on is the adjective in front of it. The test is the slot, not the ending: in good health would sit there happily, and with no trouble on a halter answers how the breed behaves rather than what it is.

Question

  • Standard

The volunteer aurora alert network ____ every confirmed sighting within a minute of the first report.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Responds cannot take an object on its own: you respond to something. Push the shared object back behind each verb and the second one comes apart.
Correct. Logs takes the object directly and responds takes it through to, so each verb carries what it needs and the object serves both.
Both halves are wrong, and in opposite directions: logs to every sighting makes the sighting a destination rather than the thing recorded, and responds every sighting withholds the preposition from a verb that requires one.
Giving each verb its own preposition is the right instinct, and log to names where a record is written rather than what is recorded. A sighting is the thing logged, not the place it goes.

Why this answer

Two verbs sharing one object have to be able to reach it, and here they reach it differently: you log a sighting but respond to one. Test a coordination like this by reading each verb with the object alone. Logs every confirmed sighting stands, responds every confirmed sighting does not, so the second verb keeps its preposition and the first must not borrow it.

Question

  • Standard

Before the winter gales set in, the harbor board has recommended that the foghorn's compressor house be reroofed and ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Recommend does not take a to-infinitive at all: recommends to replace the frames is not English, whatever it is joined to.
A gerund is a shape recommend does accept on its own, which is what makes this the tempting wrong answer. What it cannot do is share one has recommended with a that clause: a verb takes one kind of complement across a coordination, not one of each.
A noun phrase names the work instead of stating what should happen. Recommend does take a noun phrase on its own, but not sharing one has recommended with a that clause.
Correct. Two that clauses, both hanging off has recommended, both in the bare subjunctive the verb calls for.

Why this answer

One verb, two complements, and the shape of the second is fixed by the first. Has recommended that the compressor house be reroofed is a that clause, so what and joins to it has to be a that clause as well. Recommend takes a that clause, a gerund, or a plain noun phrase, and it is strict about mixing them, which is what makes the gerund read so plausibly here; the infinitive is not on its list at all.

Question

  • Advanced

The club has not settled whether to resurface the strip its fencers have used since 1990 or ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Correct. Whether to resurface ... or to move ... hangs an infinitive on each half, which is what the pair promised eleven words earlier.
Whether does not take a bare gerund at all, whatever stands on its left, so this half is barred twice over: once by the pair and once by the word that opened it.
Whether can join two finite clauses perfectly well. What it cannot do is join one to an infinitive, and the first half is already to resurface.
Whether and that are rival ways of opening a complement, so a sentence cannot use one for each half of the same pair.

Why this answer

A relative clause and a date stand between the two halves of this pair, which is the whole of the difficulty: by the time the blank arrives, whether to resurface is eleven words back. Reach past the intervening clause, find the first half, and match it. Note that English would also allow or move, letting the second half borrow the first's to; that form is not among the choices, so the pair here is repeat-the-to or nothing.

Question

  • Advanced

Bagged in mesh and stowed under the transom between dives, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Nobody bags a survey team in mesh. The team does the stowing, and the opener describes what gets stowed.
The subject is checking the shackle, a job, and a job does not get bagged in mesh and stowed under a transom.
Correct. The buoy is the thing bagged and stowed, and the passive is what puts it in the subject slot where the opener can reach it.
The buoy is in the sentence, but only as something a line of a log is about. The noun the opener grabs is line.

Why this answer

The opener is a pair of past participles, so it names what was acted on rather than who acted: ask what gets bagged and stowed, and the answer is the buoy. Only one choice puts the buoy first, and it does that by being passive. On this page the passive is usually the trap, because it usually hides the doer an opener needs; here it is the repair, because what the opener needs is the thing the doing happens to.

Question

  • Advanced

After sitting unread in the town clerk's basement for thirty years, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

There is a placeholder holding the subject slot open, not a name for anything, so the opener has nothing to describe. The pages turn up later in the choice, but the subject slot is where an opener looks.
This it names nothing either; it is standing in for the that clause at the end. A dummy subject cannot host a modifier any more than a placeholder can.
A real subject at last, and the wrong one: the damp did not sit unread for thirty years, and the ledger it acted on is not in the sentence at all.
Correct. The ledger is the thing that sat unread, and it stands directly after the comma where the opener can reach it.

Why this answer

An opener needs a subject that names something, and two of these choices do not supply one: the there of there was no way and the it of it was obvious are placeholders holding a slot open, not words that point at anything that could have sat in a basement. A possessive subject at least names something and names the wrong thing; these two name nothing at all, which is the same failure one step further along, and it is worth meeting once, because a sentence beginning there was reads as though it has a subject.

Question

  • Advanced

For sixty years the loft's brass race clock ____ the club's final authority on a close arrival.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Push the complement back behind each auxiliary: will be the club's final authority stands, and has the club's final authority says the clock owns it. The first half needs a participle of its own.
Neither half is finished. Has is left owning something and will is left with no verb at all.
Correct. Each auxiliary carries the form it needs, has been and will be, so both halves reach the complement.
The first half is repaired and the second is not: will the club's final authority leaves the modal holding a noun phrase.

Why this answer

When two verb phrases share a complement, each has to be able to reach it by itself, and the test is to read each one alone with the words after the blank. Has been the club's final authority and will be the club's final authority both stand; has the club's final authority changes the meaning to ownership and will the club's final authority is not a sentence at all. What makes this hard is that the missing word is never printed anywhere, so there is nothing to compare the halves against except the reading itself.

Question

  • Advanced

Bought secondhand and rewired in the library's own workshop, ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

The head noun of the tool library's volunteers is volunteers, and nobody bought them secondhand. A possessive is not automatically the dodge; what matters is which noun it ends on.
Correct. This subject is a possessive too, and here the noun it ends on is drill, which is the thing bought and rewired. The opener lands on it.
You can buy a policy, and you cannot rewire one: a policy has no wiring to redo. The opener describes something with a circuit in it, and this subject is an abstraction.
The volunteer did the rewiring rather than undergoing it, and the thing that was bought appears only as it, at the far end of the clause.

Why this answer

Three of these four subjects are possessives, which is the point: the page's trap is a possessive that parks the right thing one noun too deep, and a student who has learned it as possessives are wrong has no way through this item. What an opener grabs is the head noun of the phrase after the comma, so the tool library's volunteers and the library's lending policy fail while the tool library's oldest drill passes. Ask what was bought and rewired, then check which choice ends its subject on that word.

Question

  • Advanced

Reseating the water bars each spring costs the trail crew far less than ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Than joins two things of one shape, and the shape on its left is the gerund reseating. An infinitive is a different one.
Correct. Two gerund phrases, one on each side of than, so the comparison is between two jobs.
A clause after than has to be one the sentence can compare a cost with, and costs less than they dig a ditch compares a cost with an act of digging.
The passive clause fails in the same place, and its subject makes the mismatch louder: the sentence is now comparing what reseating costs with a ditch being dug.

Why this answer

A comparison is a pair, so than obeys the same rule a series does, and the rule is about the slot rather than the ending: both sides have to name things of one kind. The left-hand term, reseating the water bars each spring, names a job. So the right-hand term has to name a job, which a gerund phrase does and a plain noun phrase would too, though none is offered here. An infinitive states a purpose and a clause states an event, and neither is a job the sentence can price.

Question

  • Advanced

Before the first train each morning the tower operator wipes down the lever frame, tests every route against the interlocking diagram, and ____.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Being pulled has no tense, so this half can never be the third thing the operator does; it is a description hanging off the sentence rather than a member of its series.
The -ing form leaves the third member with no tense, so it reads as a comment on the second rather than as the third thing in the list.
Wrapping the action in a noun makes the third member a thing where the first two are actions, and the operator is left performing it in no grammatical way at all.
Correct. Three present-tense verbs, all of them governed by the tower operator, which is what the first two set up.

Why this answer

The subject is written once and reaches all three members, so each has to be a verb it can govern: the operator wipes, the operator tests, the operator ____. Only one choice is a verb with a tense; the other three are a phrase with no tense at all, a gerund, and a noun, and none of them can be something the operator does before the first train. The distance is the rest of the difficulty: by the blank, the subject is fourteen words back.
