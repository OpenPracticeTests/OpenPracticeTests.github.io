Study guide

Reading and Writing

Everything this section tests, one page per topic. Each page teaches one rule or skill, shows it worked through, names the trap the test wraps it in, and drills it with original practice items tagged by difficulty tier.

16 of 16 topics are written. They are organized by the section's four content domains, in the order the domains appear in every module.

Craft and Structure

About 28% of the section.

Words in Context

Predict the missing word from the sentence's own logic before the choices can mislead you.

Text Structure and Purpose

Read for what a text is doing, then judge each choice's verb before its topic.

Cross-Text Connections

Pin each author's stance in three words and stop the test from inflating a mild caution into a rejection.

Information and Ideas

About 26% of the section.

Central Ideas and Details

The main idea must cover the whole text, not its loudest sentence.

Command of Evidence: Textual

Match findings to the claim, not the topic; on-topic is not on-claim.

Command of Evidence: Quantitative

Read the axes first, then find the one comparison the sentence actually needs.

Inferences

The right completion is forced by the text's premises, not merely allowed by them.

Standard English Conventions

About 26% of the section.

Subject-Verb Agreement

Find the true subject when the sentence parks a decoy noun next to the verb.

Verb Forms and Tense

Keep a sentence's clock consistent and its verbs finished.

Pronouns

Match every pronoun to one unambiguous antecedent in number and case.

Possessives and Contractions

Its and it's, whose and who's, plural and possessive: the apostrophe rules that decide them.

Sentence Boundaries

Periods, semicolons, and the comma splice your ear insists is fine.

Punctuation Within Sentences

Colons, dashes, and the commas that fence off nonessential material.

Modifiers and Sentence Structure

Put descriptions next to what they describe, and keep parallel ideas in parallel form.

Expression of Ideas

About 20% of the section.

Transitions

Name the relationship between two sentences before you look at a single choice.

Rhetorical Synthesis

Read the goal first; every choice is faithful to the notes, and only one serves the goal.
