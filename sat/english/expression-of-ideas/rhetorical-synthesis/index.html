Rhetorical Synthesis

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessons

Each item gives you bulleted notes, a goal sentence naming what the student wants, and four sentences built from those notes. Decide which one does the job the goal sets, for whatever reader it names.

Key takeaways

  • Read the goal sentence before the notes, and name every constraint it sets.
  • A two-part goal is two checks, and meeting one of them is not enough.
  • Check every choice against the notes, then decide on the goal, which accuracy cannot do.
  • A choice can use the notes correctly and still do a job the goal did not name.

What the SAT may ask

  • Introduce a subject to a reader new to it.
  • Emphasize a difference or a similarity between two things.
  • Explain the purpose of a change or a practice.
  • Present what a study found, and by how much.
  • Emphasize a limitation the researchers themselves acknowledged.
  • Explain how a study tested what it tested.

The rule

The goal sentence is the question. Read it before the notes and write down every constraint it sets.

Constraint What it asks for What it rules out
Audience A reader, and what that reader already knows A choice that spends its length on what the reader has
Job Introduce, explain a purpose, present a finding, describe a method, and more A sentence doing a different job well
Target The difference, similarity, limitation, or generalization named A true sentence about something else
Emphasis What the sentence has to carry, not mention in passing A choice that mentions the required part and makes something else its point

Check every choice against the notes and the goal for a claim they cannot support: a noun no bullet supplies, an any or a must no bullet earns, or a fact moved onto the wrong thing. The table below names the first two.

A figure you work out from the bullets is not one of these, and neither is a careful conclusion drawn from them. The list is not closed, and more than one choice can fail the check.

A study's notes usually hold four different things. A team asks whether a hedge shelters a field: the research question. It counts yields along hedged and open edges: the method.

Hedged rows ran a tenth heavier: the finding. Those hedged fields were also the older ones: the limitation. A goal names one of the four, or something else, such as a plan for what comes next.

A choice that meets one constraint of two is wrong.

Why the rule exists

Writing is selection. The same facts support many true sentences, and the useful one is chosen for a particular reader and a particular job.

Choose the move

Worked examples

1. Mark the notes the goal needs

Goal: the student wants to emphasize how quickly the footbridge was replaced.

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • NEEDED A March flood took out the wooden footbridge on the mill path.
  • not needed: the town council approved repair money at its April meeting.
  • not needed: volunteers milled the decking from two storm-felled oaks.
  • NEEDED The new bridge opened forty days after the flood.

Task: read the goal first, then mark each bullet needed or not needed.

Evidence: speed needs a start and an end. The flood is the start, the opening the end, and forty days is the gap. The April vote and the milled decking are true and neither is needed here.

Answer: the two marked bullets, and nothing else: Forty days after a flood took out the mill path's footbridge, its replacement opened.

Closest trap: goal mismatch. A sentence about the volunteers and their storm-felled oaks uses the notes correctly and says nothing about speed.

2. A comparison needs both sides on one dimension

Goal: the student wants to emphasize how the two greenhouses differ.

  • A steel-framed greenhouse at Hollis Park holds 4 degrees Celsius above the outside air on a clear winter night.
  • Its frame is uninsulated and its glass is single-glazed.
  • The park's newer timber-framed house, glazed the same way, holds 7 degrees Celsius above the outside air on the same nights.
  • Both houses are heated only by the sun.

Task: a difference needs both subjects on one dimension. Find a dimension the notes give for each, and on which the two come out differently.

Evidence: the glazing and the sun are the same for both houses, so neither can carry a difference. The two temperature bullets name the frames as well, so one sentence can hold both differences at once.

Answer: On a clear winter night the timber-framed house holds 7 degrees Celsius above the outside air where the steel-framed one holds 4.

Closest trap: true-but-partial. The steel-framed house holds 4 degrees Celsius above the outside air is one side of a comparison, and one side compares nothing.

3. Check each constraint separately

Goal: the student wants to present the project's result to an audience already familiar with the project.

  • In 2021 the town of Brinmouth offered to coat any flat roof white, free of charge.
  • A white coating reflects most of the summer sunlight that dark asphalt absorbs.
  • Homeowners who signed up logged their indoor temperatures the following July.
  • The coated houses averaged three degrees Fahrenheit cooler than they had the July before.

Task: two constraints, so run two checks.

Evidence: Brinmouth offered to coat any flat roof white, and the coated houses ran cooler gives the result and re-explains the project. A white coating reflects most of the sunlight dark asphalt absorbs suits the reader and gives the mechanism, not the result.

Answer: The coated houses averaged three degrees Fahrenheit cooler that July than they had the July before. Keep the previous July: it is what the three degrees is measured against, and the notes give no other baseline.

Closest trap: goal mismatch. The mechanism sentence suits the reader exactly and reports no result at all.

Common traps

A correct sentence doing the wrong job

Trap Correction
goal mismatch Name the job the goal set, then name the job the sentence does
true-but-partial Count what the goal asks the sentence to carry; the answer carries all of it
accurate-but-irrelevant Ask what it bears on; goal mismatch does another job, this does none
unanchored noun Point at the noun and find the bullet that supplies it
overreach (must/all) Strike an any, every or must that no bullet earns

The first three survive an accuracy check, so only the goal can remove them. These are the commonest, not all.

The method: Read the goal, then match the choices

  1. Identify the goal before you read the bullets. Write down the job it names, what must be emphasized or compared, and what any reader it names already knows.
  2. Find the notes the goal needs. The bullets are an inventory, not a passage, so read each for what it holds and let the goal say which ones matter.
  3. Check every choice against the notes. Strike a claim the notes cannot support. Accuracy will not finish the job.
  4. Pick the choice that does everything the goal asks and nothing it forbids.

Practice

Work each item with the method, untimed, until the method is automatic. The Reading and Writing section averages about 71 seconds a question, but that is an average across the section and not a deadline for any one item; save the clock for mixed practice later. Practice 1

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Kintsugi is a Japanese craft for repairing broken pottery.
  • The broken pieces are rejoined with lacquer dusted with powdered gold.
  • The gold turns each mended crack into a bright, visible seam.
  • Where an ordinary repair hides the break, a kintsugi repair displays it.

The student wants to introduce kintsugi to a reader who has never heard of it. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Trap: goal mismatch. The contrast is true, and it assumes the reader already knows what a kintsugi repair is, which the goal rules out.
Trap: true-but-partial. The goal names kintsugi as the thing to introduce, and this sentence never names it. A reader who has never heard of it is handed a technique with nothing to attach it to.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at collectors: no bullet mentions buyers, selling, or what a mended bowl is worth.
Correct. It names the craft, its origin, the technique, and the visible seam, which is what an unfamiliar reader needs. Every word comes from the notes.

The method, applied

Step 1: the goal is to introduce kintsugi to a reader who has never heard of it, so the sentence must name it. Step 2: the notes hold a name, a technique, a seam, and a contrast. Step 3: the collectors sentence claims a worth no bullet mentions. Step 4: the contrast sentence needs a reader who knows the craft already, and the lacquer sentence never names it.

Practice 2

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Tennis is played on several court surfaces, including clay and grass.
  • A ball landing on clay digs into the loose granules, so it comes off the bounce slower and higher.
  • A ball landing on grass skids off the low blades, so it stays faster and lower.
  • The net stands at the same height on either kind of court.

The student wants to emphasize a difference between the two surfaces. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Trap: goal mismatch. This gathers what the two surfaces share, and emphasizing a similarity is the opposite of the difference the goal named.
Correct. It sets the two surfaces against each other on the same measures, speed and height off the bounce, so the difference does the sentence's work.
Trap: true-but-partial. One surface cannot carry a contrast, and grass never appears, so half of the required comparison is missing.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. Naming two surfaces is not comparing them, so nothing here bears on the difference the goal asked for.

The method, applied

Step 1: the goal is to emphasize a difference between the surfaces. Step 2: one bullet describes clay, one describes grass, and two separate nothing. Step 3: all four sentences report the bullets exactly, so nothing falls to the notes. Step 4: only the choice setting clay against grass on speed and height off the bounce carries a difference.

Practice 3

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Riders on the city's buses once boarded through the front door only, filing past a single fare box.
  • Planners call the time a bus spends stopped at the curb while riders board its dwell time.
  • At crowded stops, front-door boarding could hold a bus at the curb for more than a minute.
  • In 2022 the transit agency installed card readers at every door and let riders board through any of them, saying it wanted to get buses away from the curb faster.
  • Average dwell time at the busiest stops has since fallen by a third.

The student wants to explain the purpose of the agency's change. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Trap: goal mismatch. This reports what happened after the change, not what the agency meant to bring about, and a purpose looks forward from the decision.
Trap: goal mismatch. The goal asks for the agency's aim, and both halves of this sentence set up the problem instead.
Correct. So that joins the change to the aim, and the aim points forward from the change rather than back from the result.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Every and must turn one agency's outcome into a rule for all transit, which no bullet supports.

The method, applied

Step 1: the goal names purpose, and the notes give the agency's aim in its own words. Step 2: the notes hold the old system, a definition, the change with its stated aim, and the result. Step 3: the every-agency-must sentence turns one outcome into a rule for all transit, which no bullet earns. Step 4: the result sentence is the real rival, since a result follows from a purpose without stating it. Only the so that sentence joins the change to the aim.

Practice 4

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • In 2023 a research team led by Ines Marchetti counted how many of one gallery's 22 paintings each visitor stopped in front of.
  • For the study's first month the gallery had no seating; benches were then installed at its center.
  • The team wanted to test whether visitors were giving up on the room before they reached the end of it.
  • With the benches in place, the median visitor stopped at 9 of the 22 paintings, up from 4.

The student wants to present the study's finding, with the size of the change it measured. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct. It gives what the study observed and the size the goal asked for, 4 to 9 of 22, both straight from the notes.
Trap: goal mismatch. This reports what the team set out to test, which is the question rather than the finding the goal asked for.
Trap: overreach (must/all). The first half is the finding, and any gallery turns one room's result into a promise about every other gallery.
Trap: true-but-partial. The goal asked for the size of the change and this gives only its direction: more could mean one painting.

The method, applied

Step 1: two constraints, the finding, which is what the study observed rather than what it was for, and the size of the change. Step 2: the notes hold a setup, a motive, and one pair of numbers. Step 3: the any-gallery sentence turns one room's result into a law, which no bullet earns. Step 4: the in-order-to-test sentence gives the question rather than the answer, and the vague sentence meets the first constraint and drops the second. Only 9 of 22, up from 4 carries both.

Practice 5

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • The standard keyboard's letter arrangement was inherited from the mechanical typewriter.
  • On a typewriter, each key swung its own type bar, and bars swinging in quick succession from neighboring slots could jam.
  • A long-standing claim holds that the letters were arranged so that frequently paired letters would sit on widely separated bars.
  • In 2019 the historian Adele Kowalczyk counted how often consecutive letters in a large sample of English prose sat on neighboring bars.
  • Several of English's most frequent letter pairs turned out to sit on neighboring bars.

The student wants to explain how Kowalczyk tested the old claim, for an audience that already knows how a typewriter's type bars work. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Trap: goal mismatch. It reports what Kowalczyk found, and the goal asked how she went about finding it.
Correct. It is the only choice that says what she did: counted consecutive letter pairs in a prose sample against how far apart their bars sat. It names the bars without explaining them, which suits the reader the goal describes.
Trap: goal mismatch. It fails both halves: it gives the question rather than the method, and it explains type bars to a reader the goal says already knows them.
Trap: overreach (must/all). The notes never say what the arrangement was for, so must have been designed settles a question the bullets leave open.

The method, applied

Step 1: two constraints, explain how the claim was tested, and address a reader who already knows how type bars work. Step 2: the notes hold the machine, the old claim, the counting, and the outcome. Step 3: the must-have-been-designed sentence settles what the arrangement was for, which no bullet says. Step 4: a method is what somebody did. The found-that sentence gives the outcome; the type-bar sentence gives the question and then explains the machinery the reader already has, so it misses both constraints. Only one choice says what Kowalczyk counted and against what.

Practice 6

  • Expression of Ideas
  • Rhetorical Synthesis
  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Many basketball players repeat a fixed routine before every free throw: the same dribbles, the same pause, the same breath.
  • In 2024 a team led by the sports scientist Mateo Andrade timed the pre-shot routines on every free throw taken in one college league's season.
  • Players whose routine stayed within half a second of their usual length made 78 percent of their free throws; players who varied more made 69 percent.
  • Andrade's team cautioned that the steadiest routines may simply belong to the calmest players, so the routine itself may not be what improves accuracy.

The student wants to emphasize a limitation that the researchers themselves acknowledge. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Trap: goal mismatch. The attribution checks out, and nine points of shooting percentage is the case for the routine, where the goal asked for the doubt.
Trap: goal mismatch. Timing one league is a fair worry about how far the result reaches, and the goal asks for the limitation the researchers acknowledged. Their one caution is a different one.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Any player will turns one season's correlation into a guarantee, the reading the researchers' own caution warns against.
Correct. It states a limitation, that the routine may deserve none of the credit, and it is the researchers' own doubt rather than a reader's.

The method, applied

Step 1: two constraints, a limitation, and one the researchers themselves acknowledge. Step 2: the notes hold the routine, the study, the nine-point result, and one caution. Step 3: the any-player sentence turns one season's gap into a guarantee, which no bullet earns. Step 4: attribution cannot decide the rest, since two sentences are credited to Andrade's team. The 78-versus-69 sentence is theirs and reports a result, not a doubt. The one-league sentence is a sound limitation and nobody in the notes raises it. Only the sentence about calm shooters is both a limitation and the researchers' own.
