Rhetorical Synthesis: practice session

20 questions on Rhetorical Synthesis, in random order. Unlike the lesson, the difficulty of each question stays hidden until you open the solution, so you meet them the way test day hands them to you: with no label. There is no timer and no score. Answer a question, then press Show solution whenever you want to see how you did and why.

Question

  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • A ha-ha is a boundary built as a wall sunk into a ditch, its top level with the lawn on the garden side.
  • From the house the wall is below the line of sight, and the lawn appears to run on into the field beyond it.
  • Sheep and cattle grazing the field cannot climb the wall's outer face.
  • Ha-has were dug on many English country estates in the eighteenth century, and hundreds of them survive.

The student wants to tell a reader who has never met the word ha-ha what one is and what it looks like. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This turns one arrangement into a rule for every estate, which no bullet supports, and it lays down an instruction rather than saying what the word names.
This says when they were dug and how many are left. The goal asks the sentence to say what one IS and what it looks like, and neither is here.
This says what a ha-ha keeps out. It never says what one is, and the only part it names is the outer face, which is the side the house never sees, so a reader meeting the word here still cannot picture it.
Correct. A wall, sunk in a ditch, at a garden's edge, low enough that the lawn seems to carry on into the field: that is the word defined and the thing pictured, which is both halves of the goal.

Why this answer

The goal asks for two things in one sentence, what the word names and what the thing looks like, so read every choice for both. The any-estate-must sentence imports a rule the notes never state. Of the two left, one dates the practice and one says what it keeps out, and neither puts an object in front of the reader.

Question

  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Roasting drives water and gas out of a green coffee bean, and a bean taken far enough fractures audibly twice.
  • The first crack is a run of slow, sharp pops; the second is a faster, quieter crackle.
  • Steam builds the pressure that opens the bean at the first crack; by the second the bean has gone brittle and the pressure is carbon dioxide.
  • A roaster who wants a light roast pulls the batch before the second crack begins.
  • In a drum roast the two cracks usually fall two to four minutes apart.

The student wants to emphasize a difference between the sound of the two cracks. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This gives what causes each crack rather than what either one sounds like. The goal named the sound, and nothing in this sentence can be heard at all.
Correct. It holds both cracks in one sentence and separates them on the two things a listener actually hears, the rate and the loudness.
This gives the gap between the two cracks. The goal asked what they sound like, and a stretch of minutes has no sound of its own.
This says what a roaster does about the second crack rather than what either crack sounds like, so it answers a question the stem did not ask.

Why this answer

The goal names a job, emphasizing a difference, and narrows its object to the sound, so a difference of any other kind will not do. One choice separates the cracks by cause, one by timing, and one is about roasting practice; only one separates them by what a listener hears.

Question

  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • A barn owl hunts in near-total darkness and locates prey by sound alone.
  • Its two ear openings sit at different heights on its head, one above the level of the other.
  • A sound coming from below the owl is therefore louder in one ear than in the other, and the owl reads that difference as an angle.
  • The stiff feathers around the rim of the owl's flat face gather sound inward toward the openings.

The student wants to specify how a barn owl's two ear openings are placed. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct. The goal asks where the openings sit, and this is the only accurate sentence that says: two of them, at unequal heights.
This states what the arrangement does to an arriving sound, and it never says where either opening sits. The goal asked for the placement, not for what the placement buys.
The notes put the unequal heights on the ear openings, not on the face feathers, and say the feathers gather sound inward rather than steer it. It also gives what a different part of the head is for.
The notes say the owl locates prey by sound alone, so hunting by eye is exactly what they deny. It also introduces the bird, which is a job the stem did not assign.

Why this answer

The goal names one object, the two ear openings, and one job, saying how they are placed. TWO of the four sentences claim what no bullet carries: one moves the unequal heights onto the face feathers, the other has the owl hunting by eye where the notes say sound alone. Both also do the wrong job, giving what the feathers are for and an introduction to the bird. Of the two that are accurate, one says what the arrangement does to a sound, and only one puts the openings anywhere.

Question

  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • A wooden ship's frames were first drawn on paper at a scale where one pencil line stood for several inches of oak.
  • In the mould loft, shipwrights chalked the same curves at full size on a long smooth floor.
  • At full size a join that missed by an inch showed as a gap, where at the drawing's scale it had been hidden inside the thickness of the lines.
  • Thin wooden patterns were then laid on the chalk and cut to it.

The student wants to explain the purpose of chalking the lines at full size. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This is the situation the loft work answered, not the reason for it. A purpose statement needs the aim, and this sentence stops before the chalk is picked up.
This is how the work was done and where. It says what the shipwrights did and never once says what any of it was for.
Correct. It joins the step to what the step was meant to achieve, which is what a purpose is, and both halves come straight from the notes.
This inflates one shipyard's practice into a rule for every structure, which no bullet supports, and it lays down an instruction rather than explaining an aim.

Why this answer

A purpose is an act joined to what it was meant to bring about, so the sentence has to carry both. The any-structure-must sentence reaches past the notes. The other two are accurate, so the goal decides between them: one gives the situation beforehand and one gives the procedure, and neither says what the full-size chalking was for.

Question

  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Starlings feed by day alone or in small parties and gather again at dusk to sleep in one place.
  • The reedbed at Merrivale Moss holds a winter roost that counters put at about 340,000 birds on its largest nights.
  • Birds come in from as far as thirty miles away, the last of them after full dark.
  • Under the roost the reeds are bent flat by the weight the birds leave on them.

The student wants to emphasize how many birds the Merrivale Moss roost draws, and how far they come. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This gives the habit that produces a roost rather than anything about this one. It counts no birds and measures no distance, which is both halves of the goal.
Correct. The goal names two things, a number of birds and a distance, and this is the only sentence that carries either, let alone both.
This is what the roost does to the reedbed, which is a consequence of the numbers rather than the numbers. Neither a count nor a distance appears in it.
The notes say the birds gather and never say why, so the reason here is imported. It also answers what for rather than how many.

Why this answer

The goal names two things the sentence has to carry, a count of birds and a distance, so read the four choices for those and nothing else. The keep-one-another-warm sentence supplies a reason for the gathering that no bullet gives. Of the rest, one gives the daytime habit and one gives a flattened reedbed, and neither counts a bird or measures a mile.

Question

  • Introductory

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • A lime kiln burns limestone into quicklime, which builders slaked for mortar and farmers spread on sour ground.
  • The kiln is charged from the top in alternating layers of broken limestone and coal.
  • Finished quicklime is raked out through an opening at the base while the burn is still going.
  • Fresh layers go in at the top as fast as the lime comes out at the bottom.

The student wants to explain how a lime kiln is kept burning without a break. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This says what a kiln makes and who used it. That is an introduction to the thing, and it leaves the question of how the fire is kept going untouched.
One end of the cycle. Loading alone would fill the kiln and stop it, so this cannot account for a fire that never goes out.
The notes describe one kiln, and this makes a claim about furnaces in general. It also states a rule where the goal asked for an account of this kiln.
Correct. The continuity is the two ends of the cycle taken together, and this is the only sentence that puts them together.

Why this answer

The goal asks how the burn is sustained, so the sentence has to name the mechanism rather than a part of the machinery. The any-furnace sentence reaches well past four bullets about one kiln. Of the rest, one introduces the kiln and one gives the loading without the drawing, which is half a cycle and therefore no explanation at all.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Until 2021 the walk signal at each of the forty Thornby intersections gave a flashing raised hand and nothing else once the crossing interval began.
  • In 2021 the city added a counter beside each of those hands, showing the seconds a walker had left.
  • A team led by the traffic engineer Priya Raval watched 6,000 crossings at the same forty intersections, before the counters went in and after.
  • The share of walkers still in the roadway when the light changed fell from 9 percent to 3 percent.
  • Raval's team noted that traffic across the city ran lighter than usual in the summer the counters went in.

The student wants to state what the study found at the intersections it watched. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This is how the study was run. A method can be reported faithfully and still leave a reader with no idea what came of it.
This is the arrangement the study was measuring against, which is the situation before rather than anything the study found.
Correct. The goal asks what the study saw happen, and this reports it: which way the share moved and how far, both figures taken straight off the bullet.
This is the team's own caution, and it is about traffic across the whole city rather than anything seen at the forty intersections the goal names.

Why this answer

The goal names the finding, which is the one thing among the notes that the study produced rather than set up or qualified. The caution is the rival worth naming: it is the researchers' own and it is about the study, and it still reports nothing that happened at a crossing.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Seedlings of some conifers take root more often on fallen, rotting trunks than on the ground around them.
  • A rotting fallen trunk that carries seedlings this way is called a nurse log.
  • Over four years the ecologist Tomas Iversen tagged every hemlock seedling in a five-acre plot of old forest, recording whether each had rooted on a fallen trunk or on open ground.
  • Of the seedlings still alive after four years, 61 percent stood on nurse logs, which covered about 6 percent of the plot.

The student wants to describe how Iversen carried out the survey. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct. The goal asks for the method, and this is the only sentence that gives one: how long, how much ground, which plants, and what was written down about each.
This is what the survey found rather than how it was carried out, and the goal asked how.
This fixes the meaning of a term. A definition is a job the stem did not assign, and no part of the survey appears in it.
One plot cannot settle what happens in any forest, and the notes promise nothing about cleared ground. It is also a prediction where the goal asked for a method.

Why this answer

A method is what somebody did, so the sentence has to report an action of Iversen's. The any-forest sentence predicts what happens in forests the survey never reached. The other two are accurate and do other jobs: one reports what the survey found and one fixes the meaning of a term.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Every object the Rundell Museum acquires is held for six weeks in a sealed room before it goes to the stores.
  • Wood-boring beetles can travel into a collection inside a single carved chair and spread from it to whatever stands near it in the stores.
  • The shelves and racks in the sealed room are steel rather than wood.
  • No infestation has been found anywhere in the museum's stores in the four years since the room opened.

The student wants to explain the purpose of the museum's quarantine room. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This reports what has happened since the room opened. An aim is settled before the fact and an outcome is counted after it, and the goal asked for the first.
Correct. It gives the practice and the harm the practice is meant to head off, which is the pair a purpose has to carry.
A detail of the fittings. It is true of the room and says nothing about why the room exists.
This gives the hazard the room answers. The danger is not the aim, and neither the room nor the six weeks appears in the sentence.

Why this answer

A purpose joins an act to what it was meant to bring about, so the sentence needs the practice and the harm it heads off. The result sentence is the real interference, since it points at the same danger and it counts four years in which none arrived, where the goal asked what the room was set up to stop.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Each spring the water in Kerrow Pond reaches one temperature from top to bottom, and the first strong wind after that mixes the whole pond in a day.
  • The pond's shallows warm ahead of its deep water, and they warm fastest in those same weeks.
  • Anglers on Kerrow Pond have long said that its trout move into the shallows during the very week the pond mixes from top to bottom.
  • In 2023 a team led by the limnologist Aurel Sandu set out to find which of the two the fish were following, since the mixing and the warming arrive together.
  • Sandu's team tagged eighty trout and logged each fish's depth every ten minutes for eight weeks.
  • The tagged trout moved into the shallows three days before Kerrow Pond mixed.

The student wants to emphasize what Sandu's team set out to settle. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This reports the belief the study went after. The claim a study addresses is not the question it poses, and this sentence contains no question at all.
This is how the team went about it. A method can be complete and still say nothing about what the team was trying to decide.
This is the answer the study reached. The goal asked what it set out to settle, which the notes give in the bullet just before the method.
Correct. The notes say the two arrive in the same weeks and that the team went looking for which one the fish track, and this is the only sentence that states the question.

Why this answer

The goal asks for the question rather than its answer, and the question here is a choice between two explanations the notes say arrive together. The three rivals give the belief the anglers hold, the method the team used, and the result it reached, and none of them names the two candidates the team was separating.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Earthworms pull leaves down into their burrows and cast soil at the surface, so that whatever lies on the ground is slowly buried under it.
  • Archaeologists working on old pasture have long treated the depth of soil that has gathered over a buried object as a rough guide to how long it has lain there.
  • In 2022 the soil scientist Nadia Sarraf laid marked discs on the surface of twelve pastures and measured how deeply each had been buried three years later.
  • Burial ran from 2 millimeters a year in one pasture to 6 millimeters a year in another, tracking each pasture's worm population.
  • Two objects lying at the same depth in different pastures may therefore have lain there for very different lengths of time.

The student wants to emphasize what Sarraf's measurements mean for reading depth as a guide to how long an object has lain buried. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This is the measurement, set out with the comparison that gives it its force. The goal asks what it does to the depth rule, and this sentence never reaches the rule.
This is the rule the goal names, stated on its own. It gives the reader what was believed beforehand and nothing about what the new figures do to it.
Correct. It carries the range and what the range costs the depth rule, which is what the goal asked the sentence to join up.
This explains how burial happens at all. A mechanism is a different job, and it would be as true if every pasture buried things at the same rate.

Why this answer

The goal names both ends of a join: the measurements, and the depth rule they bear on. A sentence that carries only one end does not make it. The bare range is the rival worth naming, because it is the finding and reads like the point, but the goal asked what the finding costs the rule, and only one sentence puts the two together.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Walkers crossing Ellersike Moor step around the wet middle of the path and tread a new line beside it, so the crossing spreads sideways rather than wearing deeper.
  • One 400-yard stretch of the crossing was measured on the ground in 1946 and again from air photographs in 1975 and 2020.
  • The stretch was 3 feet across in 1946, 14 feet in 1975, and 61 feet in 2020.
  • The park is now laying a stone causeway over the wettest quarter of that stretch.

The student wants to emphasize how much wider the path has grown since 1946. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct. The goal asks how much, and this is the only sentence carrying a width at all, let alone the 1946 figure the question is measured from.
This explains why the path widens instead of deepening. It would read exactly the same whether the spread since 1946 had been one foot or fifty, so it answers no part of how much.
This is what happens next. The goal asked how far the widening has already gone, and the causeway is not a width.
Three dates and no width. The 400 yards is how long the stretch is, and this says when the record was made rather than what the record shows, which is a near miss and still a miss.

Why this answer

The goal asks how much, from a stated year, so the sentence has to give the size of the growth since 1946, and here only one choice carries a width at all. The survey sentence is the interference, since it carries the same three years and looks like a record of change, but a date is not a width.

Question

  • Standard

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Before radar, listeners on a chalk headland used a concrete dish twenty feet across to hear aircraft far out at sea.
  • Sound arriving from out to sea is turned back by the dish into a small pocket of air in front of it, where the listener stood.
  • The domed hall at Whitcombe has a gallery where a whisper against the wall is heard clearly a hundred feet away on the far side.
  • The whisper runs around the gallery's curved wall, each reflection keeping it against the stone instead of letting it spread into the room.
  • Neither surface adds any sound of its own; each works by gathering what would otherwise spread out and be lost.

The student wants to emphasize what the listening dish and the whispering gallery have in common. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This sets the two against each other, which is the opposite assignment. Both halves are accurate and the sentence emphasizes how the surfaces differ.
One of the two, described exactly. A shared property needs both in the frame, and the gallery never appears here.
This gives what one of the two was built for. A purpose is a different job, and the sentence never reaches the gallery at all.
Correct. It holds both objects in one sentence and names the one thing they do alike, and it rules out the thing a reader is likeliest to assume, that either is a source of sound.

Why this answer

A sentence about what two things have in common has to hold both of them and name one property they share. One choice states a genuine difference, one carries only the dish, and one gives what the dish was built for, so only one is doing the assignment at all.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • A Balinese gamelan is an ensemble of tuned bronze gongs and metallophones, played together with drums and cymbals.
  • Its metallophones are made in matched pairs, one of each pair set a few cycles a second above the other.
  • Struck together, a matched pair produces the slow beating pulse that players call ombak.
  • Two gamelans are rarely tuned alike, so an instrument is not easily moved from one set to another.

The student wants to introduce the paired tuning of a Balinese gamelan to an audience that does not know what a gamelan is. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This does the first half of the goal, saying what a gamelan is, and stops there. The paired tuning the stem asks it to introduce is nowhere in the sentence.
Correct. It says what a gamelan is for a reader who does not know, and gives the pairing the stem named, in that order. Both halves of the goal, in one sentence.
This does the second half, the pairing, and never says what a gamelan is. The stem says its reader does not know, so the sentence leaves them holding a detail of nothing.
This gives what the pairing produces rather than introducing the pairing, and it hands a term of art to a reader who has not yet been given the instrument.

Why this answer

Read the goal twice before the bullets, because it asks for two things at once: a reader with no idea what a gamelan is, and the pairing as the subject. One rival hands over the ensemble and never the pairing, another the pairing and never the ensemble, so a student who checks only one of the two demands cannot tell those two rivals apart.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • A conifer pushed off vertical by a landslip or a steady wind does not stay leaning.
  • On the underside of the lean it lays down denser, darker wood with shorter cells, called compression wood.
  • Compression wood pushes lengthwise as it forms, levering the trunk back toward upright over several seasons.
  • A trunk knocked twenty degrees off vertical may take a decade to come back within five.

The student wants to emphasize what pushes a leaning conifer back upright, for a reader who already knows that a conifer lays down new wood each year. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct. It names the mechanism, where the wood goes and what it then does, and it spends none of its length re-teaching the annual ring the stem says the reader has.
Half of this sentence explains the annual ring to a reader the stem says already knows it, and the other half stops at unevenly without saying what the unevenness does.
This is the fact to be explained. Stating that a tree straightens is not saying how it straightens, which is what the goal asked for.
This gives how slowly the straightening runs. A rate is not a mechanism, and a reader could take this sentence away without knowing what does the pushing.

Why this answer

Two constraints: the mechanism of the straightening, for a reader who already has the annual ring. The ring sentence is the rival built to look right, since it is about wood being added and points the right way, but it gives what the stem says the reader knows and never reaches the lever.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Over one weekend in June, volunteers checked all 412 drinking fountains listed in Calderwell's parks database.
  • 149 of the 412 gave no water at all, or gave it too slowly to drink from.
  • The database covers fountains in parks only; those in libraries, transit stations, and school yards are held on separate lists and were not visited.
  • The city shuts many park fountains from November through March, so a count taken in winter would find more of them dry.

The student wants to emphasize a limitation in the range of fountains the count reached. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

A real limitation, and of the wrong kind: it is about when the volunteers went, not about which fountains they could reach. The goal names the range.
This is what the count found. A finding is a different job, and this one is the number a limitation would be qualifying.
Correct. It names the boundary of the list the volunteers used and what falls outside it, which is exactly a limitation in how far the count reached.
Nothing outside the parks was checked, so the notes cannot rank the parks against anywhere else. It also states a conclusion where the goal asked for a limitation.

Why this answer

The goal narrows the object: not any limitation, but one in the range of fountains the count reached. The worst-served sentence rests on a comparison the notes cannot make, since the same bullet that gives the key says nothing outside the parks was visited. Of the two left, one is the finding and one is a limitation of the season rather than of the reach, and a limitation of the wrong kind is still the wrong answer.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Field names on the Wraycombe estate appear in leases from 1560 onward, and most were never printed on any map.
  • Over three winters, volunteers transcribed 2,100 field names out of 340 leases and matched each one to a boundary drawn on one of the estate's own plans.
  • Sixty-one of the names carry a word for a crop or a soil that has dropped out of local use.
  • The group now means to set those crop words against the estate's crop accounts, to see whether a field's name records what was grown in it.

The student wants to emphasize what the group intends to do next, for readers who already know what the transcription turned up. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This is what the transcription turned up, which the stem says the reader already has, and it is about work finished rather than work planned.
The boundaries came from the estate's own plans, and the notes say most of these names were never printed on any map. It also gives the method, which is a different job.
The notes call this an open question the group means to test, so asserting it reaches past them. It also announces a conclusion where the goal asked for a plan.
Correct. It names the next piece of work and what that work is meant to decide, and it takes the finding as read rather than re-reporting it.

Why this answer

Two constraints: what is planned, for a reader who has the finding already. TWO sentences claim what the notes do not: one settles the very question the last bullet says is still open, and the other puts the boundaries on a printed map the notes say most of these names never reached. Both also do the wrong job. The one accurate rival re-reports the finding the reader already has.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Four abbeys in the Ashen valley each dug a group of fishponds, and all four groups survive today as earthworks.
  • In all four, the ponds run down the slope in a line, each spilling into the one below it.
  • In all four, the smallest pond sits at the top of the line, nearest the abbey buildings.
  • The group at Wickmoor has seven ponds, more than twice as many as any of the other three.
  • At two of the four, a cut channel brings water down from a stream a mile up the valley rather than from the brook nearest the ponds themselves.

The student wants to make a generalization about the layout of all four groups of ponds. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

This counts the ponds at one abbey. A count is not a layout, and one abbey is not all four, so it misses both halves of the goal at once.
Correct. It reaches all four and it is about layout, which are the two things the goal asked for, and every part of it comes from a bullet that says all four.
This is about layout and reaches half the abbeys. Two of four is a pattern worth noting and it is not a generalization about all four.
This reaches all four and says nothing about how any of them is arranged. It introduces the subject rather than generalizing about it.

Why this answer

Two constraints sit inside a short goal: a claim covering all four groups, and a claim about layout. Each rival drops at least one constraint, one holding to two abbeys, one counting rather than arranging, and one covering four without describing any, so both checks have to be run on every choice.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Some machines carry one part built to give way first, so that an overload destroys the cheap piece instead of the costly one.
  • On an outboard motor a soft brass pin holds the propeller to its shaft; a rock strike shears the pin and the propeller spins free, leaving the gearbox unharmed.
  • A replacement pin costs about a dollar and can be fitted without leaving the boat.
  • Blocks of zinc bolted below a ship's waterline corrode away over months in place of the steel hull around them and are renewed whenever the ship is hauled out.

The student wants to give a specific example of a part built to give way under a sudden overload. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Correct. It is one named part, and it gives way in an instant under a sudden load, which is the claim the stem sent you to illustrate.
A sacrificial part, and it is spent slowly by corrosion rather than broken by a sudden load. The goal named the overload, and this example answers a neighboring claim.
This restates the claim in the terms it was already given in. The goal asked for a case that shows it, and a case has to name something in particular.
This is what the repair costs, which is a detail alongside the example rather than the example. Nothing here breaks and nothing is protected.

Why this answer

Two constraints again: a specific case, and one that fits the particular claim the goal names. The zinc blocks are the rival worth naming, because they are a real sacrificial part and the notes carry them, but the goal says sudden overload and a block of zinc gives way over months.

Question

  • Advanced

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • In dog agility a course is run against the clock over obstacles that include the A-frame, the seesaw, and the dog walk, each with a painted band at the foot of its down ramp.
  • The band is called a contact zone.
  • A dog must set at least one paw inside the painted band at the foot of the ramp on its way down, or the run is faulted.
  • Handlers walk the course on foot before any dog runs it, planning where to stand at each obstacle.
  • The bands are there because a dog that launches from high on a ramp lands hard on its shoulders.

The student wants to specify what a dog must do at a contact zone, for an audience that already knows an agility course's obstacles. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Most of this names obstacles the stem says the reader already knows, and the rest only says a painted band is there. After all of it the dog has still not been told to do anything.
This gives why the rule exists. A reason is a different job, and a reader could take it away without knowing what the rule requires.
Correct. It states the requirement, where it applies, and what happens if it is missed, and it assumes the obstacles the stem says the reader has.
This is what the handler does, and before the run rather than at the obstacle. Nothing in it is a thing a dog has to do at a contact zone.
