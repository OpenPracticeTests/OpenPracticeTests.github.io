Question

  • Introductory

A weather vane turns on a single spindle, and the arrow that ends up pointing into the wind is the smaller half of it. The broad tail behind the spindle catches far more air than the head does, so the moving air pushes the tail around until it sits directly downwind. The head is then left with nowhere to point but upwind.

According to the text, what brings a weather vane to rest with its head into the wind?

The text never gives the head's shape any work to do. It says the head is the smaller half, which is the opposite of leading.
Correct. The text states both the cause and the direction: more surface behind the spindle, so the tail ends up downwind and the head ends up facing the wind.
Point at the spindle. The text says the vane turns on one and says nothing about where it sits or what either half weighs.
There is no roof in this text and no mention of how high a vane is mounted. A true fact about where vanes go is not an answer to this question.

Why this answer

The stem asks what settles the vane, and the middle sentence answers it: the tail catches more air than the head, so the wind carries the tail downwind and leaves the head pointing the other way. The three rejected choices turn on the head's shape, the spindle's position, and the vane's mounting, none of which this text mentions at all.

Question

  • Introductory

Building rules ask that a handrail carry on for some distance past the last step, running level before it stops. The extension looks like a flourish, and it is not one. Somebody coming down without watching their feet meets that level stretch under the hand at the moment the stairs end, and the rail has reported the ending before the floor does.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The rules are in the text; this is not what they are said to require. The text is about the piece of rail past the last step, not about the rail's length.
Aimed at the right part of the stair and arguing from something the text never supplies: there is no fall here and no ranking of one part of a stair against another.
The text raises the decorative reading in order to reject it in the same sentence. This is the reading it rejects.
Correct. Both sentences after the first are working for this: the extension looks ornamental, and what it actually does is report the end of the stairs early.

Why this answer

The text spends one sentence on what the rules ask, one on the reading it wants to rule out, and one on what the extension does. Only the claim that the level stretch carries information rather than decoration accounts for all three. The others take the rules without the reason, supply a comparison the text never makes, or state the reading the second sentence exists to deny.

Question

  • Introductory

Growers in cold districts paint the trunks of young fruit trees white before winter. On a clear February afternoon the sun can lift the temperature of dark bark on the south side of a trunk well above the temperature of the air, and the sap beneath it thaws; after sunset it freezes again, and the bark splits along the line where the two met. A white trunk reflects that sun and never warms enough for any of it to begin.

According to the text, how does the white paint keep the bark from splitting?

Correct. The last sentence gives the mechanism, and the sentence before it supplies what that mechanism prevents.
The text has splitting bark in it, but the paint goes on before any split and works on temperature. Nothing here is sealed.
This runs the text backward. The trouble is bark that gets too warm by day, and the paint's job is to stop that rather than to hold heat in.
Find the machinery on the page. There is none, and no rows either, which is what separates a plausible answer from an answer to this text.

Why this answer

The stem asks how the paint works, and the text answers in its last sentence: white reflects the sun, so the warming that starts the whole sequence never happens. The other three treat the paint as a sealant, as insulation holding heat in, or as a marker, and the text supports none of the three.

Question

  • Introductory

Between one panel of a comic and the next there is a blank strip with nothing drawn in it. A cat sits on a windowsill in one panel and is on the floor in the next, and the reader supplies the jump without noticing that anybody has. Artists set the width of that strip on purpose: a wide one hands the reader more to cover, a narrow one almost nothing.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

A reason the text never gives. It is the reason a reader supplies from outside, and it makes the strip a printing convenience rather than a working part.
The text says the opposite: what happens between panels is exactly what is not shown, and the reader supplies it.
Correct. It is the only claim that gives all three sentences work, including the last one, which is about the artist choosing the size of the gap.
True of comics and absent from this text, which never mentions prose or compares the two. The nouns are all imported.

Why this answer

Walk each choice across the three sentences. Only the claim that the reader fills the gap and the artist sizes it reaches the last sentence, which is where the text puts the artist's decision. The others explain the gap away as a printing habit, reverse who supplies the missing action, or compare comics with prose, which this text never does.

Question

  • Introductory

The glues used to bond a dry surface will not hold on a wet one, because the water is already there and the glue never reaches the stone. A barnacle settling on a rock appears to deal with this before it starts. It puts out an oily fluid that spreads beneath itself, and the working proposal is that this fluid pushes the water off the rock, so that the cement following it meets stone the water has been cleared from.

According to the text, how does a barnacle's cement reach the rock at all?

There is no weight and no sinking anywhere in this text. Its answer is that the water is moved out of the way first, not driven through.
Correct. The third sentence gives the two steps in order, and it carries the hedge the text carries: this is the working proposal, not a settled finding.
Point at the grip. The barnacle does nothing mechanical in this text; what clears the water is a fluid, and the text names it.
The mechanism with the text's hedge taken off. The text says appears and calls it a working proposal; this reports it as settled. It overreaches again at the end: the text says only that dry-surface glues fail on a wet rock, not that nothing at all will stick to one.

Why this answer

The text sets a problem in its first sentence and offers an answer in its third: an oily fluid goes down first and is thought to clear the water, so the cement meets stone the water has left. Match the hedge as well as the mechanism: one of the rejected choices is this same account reported as settled fact, and the text calls it a working proposal twice over. The other two invent a mechanism the text does not have at all.

Question

  • Introductory

A speed hump runs the full width of the lane, so everything coming along has to ride over it. A speed cushion does not. It is a square pad set in the middle of the lane with a gap on either side of it, narrow enough that a car must put at least one wheel up on it, and wide enough that a fire engine, whose wheels are set further apart, passes with a wheel running in each gap.

According to the text, what lets a fire engine cross a speed cushion without riding over it?

No law appears in this text. What lets the engine through is a measurement, and the text gives it in the last line.
Nothing here weighs anything or gives way. The pad is fixed, and the difference between the two vehicles is the width of their axles.
This contradicts the text, which says a car has to put a wheel up on the pad. If nothing were slowed there would be no point in laying one.
Correct. The text gives the whole answer in its final clause, and the gaps exist for exactly this reason.

Why this answer

The text is built as a contrast: a hump catches everything, a cushion catches what is narrow. The reason is stated outright, that a fire engine's wheels are further apart than a car's and so fall in the gaps. The rejected choices reach for a legal exemption, a pad that flattens, and a device that slows nobody, and the text supplies none of them.

Question

  • Standard

A subtitle stays on screen only as long as the shot allows, and a reader gets through a limited number of characters in that time. Subtitling houses fix that number in advance and hold every line to it. So the work is not finding English for what was said; there is usually no room for what was said. It is deciding which of the things a line was doing, naming, joking, hedging, insulting, will survive into forty characters, and which of them will be let go.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The timing is where the limit comes from, and the text spends its second half on what the limit forces a subtitler to do instead.
Joking is one of four things the text lists, and the text never puts them in an order or says which goes first.
Correct. The constraint, the denial that this is translation, and the list of things that compete for the space all have work to do under it.
True, and it is the second sentence. It states the rule and stops before the consequence the rest of the text is about.

Why this answer

The text names a constraint, denies that the job is translation, and then says what the job is instead, which is choosing what to drop. Only one choice reaches that last sentence. The others promote the timing, the character limit, or one item from the list of things a line does, and each of them leaves the closing sentence with nothing to do.

Question

  • Standard

Farmers on stony ground clear a field one spring and find it stony again the next, and the usual explanation is that the plow is turning up what was always down there. Something else is happening as well. Water in the soil freezes first against the underside of a stone, because the stone chills faster than the earth around it, and the ice that forms lifts the stone a little way. When the ground thaws, soil runs in below, and the stone cannot come back down to where it was.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct. It is the smallest claim that reaches the last two sentences, which are the lifting and the closing that make the rise permanent.
The text grants this and then says something else is happening as well. It is the explanation the text is adding to, not the one it arrives at.
The third sentence, reported accurately: the text says water freezes FIRST against a stone's underside, which is the comparison this choice makes. It is the first step of a mechanism whose point is in the sentence after it.
There is no wet spring in this text and no claim about how hard clearing is. The nouns are borrowed and the claim is imported.

Why this answer

The text concedes the familiar explanation, says there is more, and then gives a two-part mechanism: ice lifts the stone, soil fills the space, the stone stays up. A main idea has to reach both parts. The rejected choices stop at the concession, stop at the first half of the mechanism, or import a claim about wet springs the text never makes.

Question

  • Standard

A copyist preparing the parts an orchestra plays from does not simply divide the music into pages. A player needs a hand free to turn, so a break has to fall where that player is resting; a break in the middle of a run leaves either a page unturned or a phrase unplayed. Where the music offers no rest at all, the copyist prints a fold-out sheet, or seats two players at one stand so that one keeps playing while the other turns.

According to the text, what decides where a page break falls in an orchestral part?

No paper size in this text, and no requirement that pages hold equal amounts. The criterion the text gives is about the player, not the sheet.
Correct. The second sentence states the criterion and then names both of the things that go wrong when it is not met.
The fold-out is in the text, and it is what the copyist reaches for when no rest exists. This makes the fallback into the rule.
Movements appear nowhere on this page. It is the kind of thing that would plausibly be true of orchestral parts, which is how it gets waved through.

Why this answer

The stem asks for the criterion, and the text states it in the second sentence: the break goes where the player is resting. The third sentence is what happens when that is impossible, so a choice built on the fold-out has taken the exception for the rule. The other two import a paper size and a movement structure the text never mentions.

Question

  • Standard

The rim of a pitcher plant is ridged, and every ridge ends in a small overhang. Dry, the surface gives an insect's foot as much purchase as any leaf, and ants walk across it and away again. Wet, the ridges hold a continuous film of water that a foot cannot get through, and the same ant slides in. Watch one plant for a week and it takes almost nothing for days together, and then, in the hour after a shower, a great deal.

According to the text, why does the plant catch almost nothing on a dry day?

There is no scent in this text, and no account of what brings an ant to the rim in the first place.
The overhang is on the page and this gives it the wrong job. What the text says fails on a wet rim is purchase, and what supplies the failure is the film of water.
An import from general knowledge. The text counts no insects and never suggests the traffic across the rim changes with the weather.
Correct. The second and third sentences are a matched pair, and the answer is the difference between them.

Why this answer

The text sets the dry rim and the wet rim side by side, and the only thing that changes between them is a film of water the foot cannot penetrate. The rejected choices explain the quiet days by scent, by the shape of the overhang, and by how many insects are about, and the text supports none of the three.

Question

  • Standard

An old bristlecone pine is mostly dead. Bare, weathered wood runs around three quarters of the trunk, and the branches that once stood above those quarters have gone with them. What is still alive is a strip of bark about a hand's width across, running from one root up to one crown of needles. Physiologists who work on these trees read that the other way round. A root that is losing ground can no longer supply the whole of a large plant, and what the strip looks like to them is not the residue of a defeat but a match: one root, one line of living bark, one crown, and nothing else drawing on any of them.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The dead wood is on the page and this gives it a job the text never gives it. Nothing here shelters anything.
This is the reading the fourth sentence turns around. It describes the same trunk as the loss the researchers in the text say it is not.
Correct. The description, the researchers' reversal of it, and the closing account of what supplies what all have work to do under it.
The third sentence, quoted accurately. It is the observation the last two sentences exist to explain, so it stops short of the point.

Why this answer

The text describes, then reports a reading that turns the description around, then explains it. Only the claim that the dieback matches the crown to what one root can supply reaches the last sentence, which is where the explanation lives. The others give the dead wood a protective job the text never mentions, restate the defeat the researchers reject, or stop at the description.

Question

  • Standard

When an allotment site wrote its first shed rule in 1978 it capped the height at seven feet and said nothing else. Sheds stopped going up and started going along: within a few years the long low shed was standard, and several ran the whole width of a plot. In 1991 the committee added a floor area. Sheds then multiplied instead, two small ones to a plot where the rule allowed one large, until a 2004 amendment counted structures rather than measuring them. The rule runs to three clauses now, and each of the two that followed the first was added after somebody had built to the edge of the one before it.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct. Both workarounds, both amendments and the closing sentence about how the three clauses arrived all have work to do under it.
The first workaround, reported exactly. It is one of two the text gives, and the 1991 and 2004 clauses have nothing to do under it.
The text never says a short rule could not have worked. It says how this one grew, which is a history rather than a claim about what rules can do.
The first half is the 2004 amendment, accurately. The second is a superlative the text never licenses: it says how this rule grew, not that any kind of rule is proof against a plotholder.

Why this answer

The text runs a chronology: a rule, a workaround, an amendment, another workaround, another amendment, and a closing sentence saying every clause arrived the same way. A claim that covers it has to hold the pattern rather than one turn of it. The others take the first workaround, the last amendment, or a claim about rules in general that this text never makes.

Question

  • Standard

Older theaters slope the stage itself, tilting it up away from the audience so that a performer standing at the back is not hidden by one standing at the front; the words upstage and downstage are left over from the practice. Sloping the stage puts the whole difficulty on the people working on it, since a dancer turns on a floor that is not level and scenery has to be built out of square. Newer houses leave the stage flat and slope the seating instead, which separates the sightlines in the same way and does it on the side of the footlights where nobody is working.

According to the text, why were stages once built with a slope?

An acoustic reason, and this text gives none. Every reason on the page is about what an audience can see.
Correct. The first sentence gives the reason directly, and the rest of the text is about what that reason cost.
Out of square is on the page as a cost of the slope, not a benefit of it, and nothing here is about how long a set lasts.
The obstruction here is a row of the audience, and the obstruction the text names is a performer at the front of the stage. Sloping the stage lifts the back of the stage into view; sloping the seating, which the newer houses do, is what lifts one row of the audience over the next.

Why this answer

The reason is in the first sentence: a raked stage lifts the performers at the back clear of those at the front, so the audience can see both. The closest wrong choice moves the obstruction onto the audience's own rows, which is what the newer houses answer by sloping the seating. The other two supply an acoustic reason and turn a stated cost into a stated benefit.

Question

  • Advanced

A marathon is the same distance wherever it is run, and a time set on one course is still not always comparable with a time set on another. Governing bodies therefore decline to ratify a record made on a course that falls more than a meter per kilometer between start and finish, or whose start and finish lie more than half the distance apart. Neither condition is about how hard the course is: a runner may take a hilly loop and be ratified, and a flat straight course may not be. What the two conditions exclude is assistance the runner did not supply, a net descent and a following wind that can push for the whole distance without ever once turning into a headwind.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The rule quoted correctly, and the text spends its second half saying what the rule is for. A claim that stops at the rule leaves that half with nothing to do.
The text denies this in as many words: neither condition is about how hard the course is, and a hilly loop qualifies.
Both halves of the example are on the page and the conclusion is not. Being ratified is not a reward, and difficulty is what the text says is not at issue.
Correct. The opening problem, the two conditions, the denial about difficulty, and the closing account of what is excluded all sit under it.

Why this answer

The text opens on comparability, states two conditions, denies that difficulty is what they measure, and closes by naming what they exclude. Only one choice carries the denial and the reason together. The others quote the rule without its purpose, or take the difficulty reading the third sentence exists to rule out.

Question

  • Advanced

Novels that first appeared in monthly parts had to earn the next month's shilling, and their authors ended installments accordingly: on a door opening, a letter arriving, a name spoken and not explained. When the parts were bound up as books those endings stayed where they were and became chapter endings, so a reader today meets a rhythm set by a payment schedule rather than by anything in the story. Critics have gone both ways on it. Some read the recurring cliff as a constraint the author worked against, and point to the novels whose best chapters end quietly. Others read it as a shape the author learned to use and would have kept without the shilling. The books cannot settle it, since a chapter that pulls and a chapter that is well made look the same on the page.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct. The origin of the breaks, the disagreement, and the reason the disagreement stands are all accounted for.
The first sentence, accurately, and the sentence the rest of the text is built on top of rather than the point the text arrives at.
The last sentence promoted to headline. It explains why the question stays open without saying what the open question is.
The text says the rhythm came from the schedule and then reports critics who think the author owned it. Nothing at all is a size the text does not license.

Why this answer

The text has three parts: where the chapter breaks came from, two readings of what the author was doing with them, and why the books cannot decide between the readings. A claim that covers it has to keep the question open. The rejected choices take the origin alone, the reason for the deadlock alone, or close the question in one direction.

Question

  • Advanced

Two patterns are pressed into the paving around a crossing and they do not say the same thing. Rows of flat-topped bars, run in the direction of travel, are a guide: follow these. A field of rounded domes is a warning: stop, something changes here. Somebody reading the ground through a cane or a shoe has learned the difference and acts on it without deliberating, which is the whole point of putting information into a surface. It is also why the two cannot be swapped when a contractor is laying them. Domes where bars belong bring a walker to a halt in the middle of a route that was safe; bars where domes belong walk somebody confidently into a road. A surface that says nothing leaves a walker to find out alone. A surface that says the wrong thing has already been believed.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The first three sentences, reported exactly. It is the setup, and the text spends its second half on what follows from it.
One clause of the fourth sentence, given a headline. The text uses that clause to explain why a wrong pattern is believed rather than to argue about simplicity.
Correct. It reaches the last two sentences, which are a comparison between saying nothing and saying the wrong thing.
The text counts no mistakes and recommends nothing. It says the surface is believed, which is the opposite of advice to stop believing it.

Why this answer

The text sets out two patterns, says a walker acts on them without deliberating, and closes by ranking a wrong surface below a silent one. The claim that covers it has to carry that ranking and the reason for it. The others stop at the description, promote a single clause, or convert the argument into advice the text never offers.

Question

  • Advanced

An estate planted forty acres of conifers in 1948 under an agreement that paid it by the acre put in the ground and settled nothing at all about what came after. A block like that wants thinning at twenty years and again at thirty, and thinning costs money before it returns any; the estate did neither. The trees are long past both those ages now and standing too close, drawn thin and tall with small crowns, which is what happens when nothing is taken out. What stands is worth a fraction of what the same forty acres would have carried thinned, and it is too tall now to thin without bringing the neighbors down with it. The agreement bought a wood, and the wood is still standing, and the work that would have made it a crop stopped being possible somewhere back in the middle of its life.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The condition of the wood, reported exactly. It is what the last two sentences draw a conclusion from, and this stops at the description.
Correct. The agreement, what it did and did not cover, the thinnings nobody did and the closing sentence about when the work stopped being possible all sit under it.
The missed thinnings are on the page and this stops at them: the agreement that explains why nobody did them is half the text, and it has nothing to do under this.
Worth less than nothing is a size the text will not carry: it says the stand is worth a fraction of a thinned one, which is a reduced value rather than a negative one.

Why this answer

The text gives an agreement, what it covered, the work it did not cover, the state of the wood long afterward, and a closing sentence about when the work stopped being possible. A claim that covers it has to reach the agreement at one end and that closing sentence at the other. The others stop at the condition of the trees, stop at the missed thinnings without the agreement that explains them, or take a stand worth a fraction for one worth less than nothing.

Question

  • Advanced

A district moved the start of its high school day from half past seven to half past eight and kept records for three years. Attendance rose, the number of first-period failures fell by about a third, and both changes held across all three years. The district reported them as an effect of the extra hour of sleep. The researchers who reviewed the records would not go that far. Moving the bell moved the buses, so students who had been driving themselves in the dark were now driving in daylight, and it moved the first period itself, so a lesson once taught at half past seven was now being taught to a room that was awake for reasons besides sleep. The records cannot separate those, and the review says so rather than choosing between them.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct. It keeps the findings, keeps the reviewers' refusal, and keeps the reason for it.
This is the district's reading, stated as settled. It is the claim the reviewers declined, and the text gives their reason for declining.
What the reviewers doubt is the cause, not the effect. The text reports both changes as holding for three years.
There is a disagreement and this names the wrong noun. Nobody in the text argues about size; they argue about what produced the change.

Why this answer

The text gives two solid findings, one interpretation, and a reviewer's refusal with the reason attached, and the claim that covers it has to hold the findings and the refusal at once. The rejected choices settle the cause, throw out the findings along with the cause, or move the disagreement onto a question nobody in the text is asking.

Question

  • Advanced

A photographer copying a collection of paintings shoots a card of neutral gray in the first frame of every session. Nothing on that frame is a painting. What is on it is a known value: whatever the lamps were doing that morning, the card is supposed to come out a particular gray, so the color of every frame shot under those lamps can afterward be pulled until it does. That does not make two frames identical, and nobody claims it does; what it removes is one reason a frame made in March and a frame made in July might not match, which is that the two sets of lamps were a different color. Skip the card and each photograph may still be a good photograph. What is lost is any ground for saying that a difference between two of them belongs to the paintings, and a set that cannot say that is a set of pictures rather than a record.

According to the text, what does photographing the gray card accomplish?

Exposure appears nowhere in this text. What the card supplies is a value the frames are pulled to afterward, not a setting fixed on the camera beforehand.
Nothing here is being judged strong or weak. The card is a value to match to, and the text never suggests a session might be called off.
Correct. The middle of the text gives it in those words, and the text is careful to claim only that this one difference is removed rather than that two frames become identical.
True of this text and offered to a question it does not answer. The closing sentence draws that contrast; the stem asks what the card does.

Why this answer

The stem asks what the card accomplishes, and the text answers in the middle: it is a known value in the frame, so the color of everything shot under those lamps can be pulled to match it and frames months apart stop differing on that. Two of the rejected choices give the card a job the text never gives it, and the third takes the closing contrast, which is true, and offers it as an answer to a different question.

Question

  • Advanced

When a country house library was broken up in 1919 it went in three hundred and forty lots, and the auctioneer's catalogue is the only account of what the room had held. Beside about half the lots a clerk penciled the buyer's name. Following those names has turned up about a fifth of the books, in twenty collections and two countries, and every one of them has matched its entry: the edition the catalogue gave, the binding it described, the shelfmark it noted. That is the whole of the argument for the four fifths nobody has found. Nothing can confirm those entries directly, and nothing needs to; a catalogue that has been right about every book anybody could check is treated as evidence about the books nobody can.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

The recovery, reported exactly. It is what the text spends its last two sentences drawing a conclusion from, and this stops at the count.
Lost against unlocated is a distinction the text never draws, and the four fifths it discusses are exactly the ones it declines to give up on.
The match is on the page; the comparison is not. Nobody's memory appears in this text, and no second kind of source is weighed against the catalogue.
Correct. The sale, the penciled names, the matching, the four fifths and the closing sentence about what can and cannot be confirmed all sit under it.

Why this answer

The text reports a dispersal, a partial recovery, a run of matches, and then says what that run licenses about the books still missing. A claim that covers it has to carry the licensing move, which is the last two sentences. The others stop at the recovery, import a lost-against-unlocated distinction, or rank the catalogue against a source the text never mentions.
