Central Ideas and Details

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

Decide which choice states the point the whole text makes, or which one answers the exact question asked. Recognizing the subject is not enough: sort the choices by what the text supports.

Key takeaways

  • The main idea is the point the whole text makes, not its most striking sentence.
  • Read to the end: a turn or hedge in the last sentence changes the idea.
  • On a details item, answer the question asked, not the nearest interesting fact.
  • A choice may paraphrase. It may not add a claim the text does not support.
  • Keep the text's strength and direction: suspect is not is.

What the SAT may ask

  • State the main idea of a whole text.
  • Answer a factual question from one stated line.
  • Summarize a text that turns near its end.
  • Report a claim the text hedges, hedge kept.

The rule

Two tasks share this skill. A main idea question asks for the point the whole text makes. A detail question asks for one stated fact.

Three words keep the tasks apart. The topic is what the text is about (a way of storing eggs). The central idea is the claim it makes about that topic (behavior that looked wasteful is storage). A supporting detail is one piece of evidence for the claim (foxes dug up buried eggs in November). Only the central idea answers a main-idea question.

Task What the answer must do What to reject
Main idea Carry the central claim, with any turn or hedge the text kept A claim about one part only, one the text does not support, or one pointed the wrong way
Detail Answer the exact question, from the line that states it A nearby but different detail, or a step the text never took

A choice is not wrong for leaving out a minor detail. Background, a concession and an example support the text without each supporting its central idea. What sinks a choice is missing the point, claiming more than the text supports, or reversing it.

Name how a wrong choice fails: coverage, anchoring, or size and direction.

Why the rule exists

Reading leaves you holding the vivid sentence. The idea is the point the vivid sentence was there to make.

Worked examples

1. Topic, central idea, detail

From The Long Field, an invented novel. Ellen has come back to her parents' farm after ten years away.

Everything had been kept. The kettle was the kettle. Her mother had not moved the tin where the string lived, and Ellen found it with her hand without looking, the way she had at eleven. She stood in the cold scullery holding it. The house had gone on being ready for her the whole ten years, and nobody in it had ever asked whether she wanted it to be.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Topic: a woman's first hour back in the house she grew up in.

Central idea: the house was kept ready for her return without anyone asking whether she wanted that, and coming back is what shows it to her.

Supporting detail: she finds the string tin with her hand, without looking.

Answer: the central idea. A main-idea question wants the claim, not the topic or a detail.

Closest trap: her mother kept the kitchen exactly as it was is the detail without the point.

2. A detail, and the line that answers it

From Signal Hill, an invented novel. Rafa keeps a lighthouse on a headland.

The inspector came up on the Tuesday boat. He walked the gallery, looked at the log, and asked Rafa nothing at all about the lamp. What he wanted to know was whether the generator had been run in April. Rafa said it had, twice, and the inspector wrote that down, looked out at the water a while, and said that would be the end of it.

According to the text, what did the inspector come to find out?

Task: answer the exact question, from the line that states it.

The line: What he wanted to know was whether the generator had been run in April. Two men are in the paragraph, so check the pronoun: he here is the inspector, the one who has been asking.

Answer: whether the generator had been used that April. The text says run and the answer says used; a faithful paraphrase is still the answer.

Closest trap: whether the lamp had been kept in order takes the nearest interesting fact. The gallery and the log are in the sentence before, and that sentence says he asked nothing about the lamp.

3. Keep the hedge

A hiccup is a sharp pull of the diaphragm and then, an instant later, the throat snapping shut. The second half makes the sound, and it makes the first half pointless: almost no air arrives. Nothing else the human body does works that way. A tadpole's gill stroke does, pulling water in and shutting the throat to keep it out of the lungs. Some physiologists suspect the hiccup circuit is old equipment, kept rather than designed and never switched off. The case rests on that resemblance and stops there for now.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Task: state the main idea.

Evidence: the words to carry out are suspect and rests on that resemblance and stops there for now.

Answer: the tadpole link is proposed and unproven, and the main idea has to say so. Keeping the hedge is not hedging your own answer; it is reporting the text.

Closest trap: the hiccup is left over from a gill stroke. That is the same claim with the hedge taken off.

Common traps

How a wrong choice fails here

Trap Correction
true-but-partial Ask whether it carries the point or only one part of the text
accurate-but-irrelevant Re-read the stem: this answers a question it did not ask
unanchored noun Point at what the choice names. The text neither states nor supports it
outside knowledge Find the step on the page. The text never supplies it
overreach (must/all) Match the text's strength: keep suspect, some, for now
wrong direction The text settled this, or left it open, and the choice reverses it

The method: Read the question, then match the claim to the text

  1. Read the question first. A main-idea question and a detail question want different things, and which one you have decides how much of the text you need.
  2. For a main idea, read to the end, then say the point in one sentence of your own before you look at the choices. Make it a claim with a verb in it, not a topic.
  3. For a detail, go to the lines that answer the question asked. You do not have to re-read the whole text for this. Strike a true choice that answers a question the stem did not ask.
  4. Take the choice that keeps the meaning and the scope. A paraphrase or a synonym is fine. An added claim, a dropped hedge and a reversed direction are not.

Practice 1

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
  • Introductory

A paper wasp nest holds several egg-laying females, and each one has a rank the others respect. Researchers noticed that no two of those females carry the same pattern of black and yellow on the face. When they painted new patterns onto a few faces, nestmates treated those wasps as strangers, crowding and biting them until the paint wore away.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct. Every sentence works for this claim: rank gives the wasps a reason to know who is who, patterns that repeat on no two faces give them something to know it by, and repainting a face changed who the nestmates saw.
Trap: true-but-partial. The text states this outright, and it is the setup. Take it for the idea and the painting experiment has nothing to attach to.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at scent. There is no smell in this text. The wasps were let back in when the paint wore away, which is when the pattern returned.
Trap: overreach (must/all). An altered wasp is treated as a stranger, but only until the paint wore away. For good makes a temporary result permanent.

The method, applied

Only the last sentence reports a result: change a face and the nestmates treat that wasp as a stranger. The closest wrong choice is the unrepeated patterns, which are the setup, and taking it leaves the experiment with nothing to attach to. Scent is supported nowhere, and for good outruns a result the text ends when the paint ends.

Practice 2

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
  • Introductory

A ship with no cargo rides too high to steer, so it pumps seawater into tanks below deck as ballast and releases it at the next port. Seeds and stems come aboard with the water and go over the side with it. A survey of one harbor turned up seedlings of plants that grow nowhere within a thousand miles of it.

According to the text, why does a ship take on ballast?

Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. Every word is on the page. The seeds are what ballast turned out to do, found later by a survey, and not a reason any ship fills a tank.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at fresh water. The text never sets two kinds of water side by side, and it gives its actual reason in the first sentence.
Correct. The text gives the reason and labels it: a ship with no cargo rides too high to steer, so it pumps water in.
Trap: unanchored noun. There are no inspectors in this text, no rule, and no clearance. It sounds like shipping, which is how a plausible noun gets waved through.

The method, applied

The stem asks why, and the first sentence answers it with so pointing at the reason: an empty ship rides too high to steer. The closest wrong choice is the seeds, which are accurate and are what ballast turned out to do, found later by a survey. They are not why anybody fills a tank. Fresh water and inspectors are supported nowhere.

Practice 3

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
  • Standard

A glass sponge grows no bone and no shell. What holds it upright is a cage of spicules, needles of the same silica that window glass is made from, which the animal secretes one at a time and cements into a lattice. Glass is brittle, and length makes it worse: a plain rod as thin as a spicule, run the whole height of the cage, would snap under its own weight. In the sponge's lattice, though, each needle is short, and every joint hands its load to several neighbors, so a stress that would break a single rod is shared out before it can travel.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Trap: true-but-partial. The silica is the problem the text poses, not what it concludes. A claim that stops at the material stops at the second sentence of four.
Correct. It is the point the whole text builds to: the material is named, then disqualified (a long plain rod that thin would snap under its own weight), then rescued by short needles and joints that share a load out.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at the water. It is never mentioned, calm or otherwise, and the last sentence describes a stress that would break a single rod.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. The text says this in a subordinate clause, on the way elsewhere. How the lattice is built is not why the lattice holds.

The method, applied

The turn is at Glass is brittle. Before it the material is named; after it the material is disqualified and then rescued by short needles and joints that share a load out, so the point is the arrangement. The closest wrong choice stops at the silica, which is the problem the text poses rather than what it concludes. The text never says material or arrangement, so the link from every joint hands its load to several neighbors to the answer is yours to make.

Practice 4

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
  • Standard

Ground madder root is a dull brown powder, and cloth boiled in it alone comes out dull brown. The red a dye works sells is not sitting in the root waiting to be poured out: it is bound to a sugar, and the long warm steeping in the vats is what breaks the two apart. Even freed, the pigment will rinse straight back out again unless the cloth has been soaked in alum first, which is work the dye works does days before any red is made at all.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Trap: true-but-partial. This is the second sentence, reported correctly, and it is half the process. The freed pigment still rinses out of cloth that was not soaked in alum.
Trap: unanchored noun. Find the field. The text opens at ground root, already dug, and everything it describes happens indoors. Madder really does stand for years, and that is not here.
Trap: overreach (must/all). The alum step is right for this red. Every red speaks for the whole trade, and this text follows one root through one set of vats.
Correct. All three sentences work for it: the powder that gives brown and not red, the steeping that frees the red, and the alum soak that keeps it on the cloth.

The method, applied

Count the operations. The raw root gives brown and not red, the steeping frees the red, and the alum applied days before makes it stay. The point is that the red is made rather than poured out. The closest wrong choice is the steeping, which is a whole half of this text rather than a stray clause, and it leaves the alum with nothing to do. The years in the ground are not here, and every red speaks for a trade the text never mentions.

Practice 5

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
  • Advanced

In the weeks when a goose colony is laying, an arctic fox takes far more eggs than it can eat, carrying them off one at a time and pressing each into the tundra in a separate spot, sometimes hundreds of paces apart. The behavior looks wasteful, and for a long while it was read that way: a glut of easy food, and an animal that could not stop taking it. Later work followed the foxes into winter, when lemmings are scarce, and found them digging up eggs they had buried in June and eating them in November. The cold ground had kept the eggs edible; the scattering had kept any one thief from finding the lot. What the fieldwork has not settled is how a fox finds a particular egg again after five months, whether by remembering each site or by searching the kind of ground it tends to bury in.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Correct. The first two sentences give the old reading, the third and fourth overturn it, and the fifth marks what stays open without taking the overturning back. The open question is how a fox relocates an egg, not why it buried one.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Researchers found them digging up eggs and eating them: a meal, observed. Get through the winter on is a diet, and nothing here measured that.
Trap: outside knowledge. Predators limiting prey is a step this text never takes. It counts no geese, follows no colony, and never looks up from the fox.
Trap: wrong direction. What is open is how a fox finds a particular egg again. Why it takes them was settled two sentences earlier: in November it eats them.

The method, applied

Read the five sentences as a sequence: an old reading, work that overturns it, then what stays open. Predict it looked like waste, it is storage, and one piece is open. The closest wrong choice is fieldwork has yet to explain why, which swaps the open noun: what is open is how a fox finds a particular egg again, and why was settled two sentences earlier. The colony's numbers are never counted, and get through the winter on turns one observed meal into a diet.

Practice 6

  • Information and Ideas
  • Central Ideas and Details
  • Advanced

Growers on hill estates have argued about the shade trees standing over the tea for as long as there have been hill estates. The trees are undeniably in the way. They take light the bushes could have used; their roots draw on the same water; the crews work around them. Where the argument has gone in circles is over yield. On a hill estate's cooler, cloud-shrouded upper terraces, thinning the shade has reliably brought in more leaf, and every trial says so. On its lower terraces, where the afternoon sun stands on the slope and the dry months run long, the same thinning has cost leaf, and every trial says that too. For years the two results were treated as a contradiction that further trials would resolve. The estates that picked one of them and worked the whole hillside by it lost leaf they did not have to lose.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Trap: true-but-partial. The text says this about the upper terraces. The next sentence, carrying the same backing, says the opposite about the lower ones.
Trap: true-but-partial. The text's other half, quoted just as accurately and just as locally. Both halves are backed by every trial, and neither is true of the whole hillside.
Correct. It is the only claim under which the text stops contradicting itself: two opposite results, both solid, differing in the ground they came from. The last sentence then prices the cost of forgetting it.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at the litter and the soil. Neither is in this text, which lists what the trees cost and measures what they add only in leaf.

The method, applied

Read the shape rather than the sentences: an old argument, what the trees cost, two findings that contradict, then a bill for taking either one home. Predict the trees pay on one slope and cost on the other, so the answer is the slope. The two closest wrong choices are those two findings, on offer at full strength with every trial says so attached to each, and each true of half a hillside: take either one whole and it collides with a sentence a few lines away. The fallen leaves and the soil they feed are supported nowhere.
