Command of Evidence: Quantitative

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

The stem gives you a claim from the passage and a table or a graph. Decide which choice reads the graphic correctly and supplies what that claim needs, which is not the same as being true.

Key takeaways

  • Read the title, axis labels, units and legend. Skipping them is what costs you.
  • A true statement is not evidence unless it carries what the claim needs.
  • A comparison is not evidenced by one side of it, however large that figure is.
  • A share needs a part and a total. A part on its own cannot settle one.
  • A claim about change needs the quantity read at two points, not one point twice.
  • A target, a limit or an average may sit in the text, not on the graphic.

What the SAT may ask

  • Use data from the table to support a claim.
  • Use data from the graph to complete an example.
  • Support a claim about a share or a percentage.
  • Support a claim comparing two groups or two years.
  • Support a claim that a figure clears a stated target.

The rule

The answer has to do two things. It must read the graphic correctly, and it must carry what the claim needs.

Accuracy alone settles little. A choice can read every figure correctly and still leave the sentence unfinished, so decide what the claim needs before you check whether a number is right.

Restate the claim in your own words. Fix three things: its subject, its direction (more or less, rose or fell), and whether it wants a count or a share. Then find the comparison in the graphic that settles it.

What the claim says What the evidence must carry
A high or a low value That figure, and the ones it beats
A change over time Two figures from one series
A difference between groups A figure from every group named
A share or a percentage A part and the total it comes out of
Where a change is concentrated The quantity that changed, read at two points
A figure against a stated target That figure and the target

Why the rule exists

A graphic holds many true statements and a claim needs one of them. A figure read correctly can still answer a different question, and accuracy is the check that cannot tell those apart. A bar means nothing until its axis says what it counts.

Choose the move

The question asks forFirst move
Two number columns, or a legendRead both headings; they may count different things
A stated target, limit or gradeSet every figure the claim names against it
A share word over a graphic of countsLook for the total; a count alone cannot settle a share
The scale or the units stated in wordsWrite down which direction is larger

Worked examples

1. Two series, and the legend says which

A student writing about a small cinema claims that its afternoon screenings are catching on.

Labels: the axis counts tickets sold, the groups are months, and the legend names the two series, matinee and evening. Nothing here is a share.

Claim: afternoon screenings are growing, so only the matinee series bears on it.

Answer: the matinee bars read 540, 610 and 880 from February to April.

Closest trap: the evening bars are the tallest on the chart, and they fall. Read correctly off the wrong series, that is an axis misread, and the legend is what separates the two.

2. The share is not in the table

A hostel reports how many beds it kept open each season and how many were slept in on an average night. A student claims the hostel is at its fullest in spring.

Season Beds kept open Beds filled
Spring 40 26
Summer 120 66

Labels: the two columns are a part and a total, not two rivals. Beds kept open is what beds filled is measured against.

Claim: fullest is a share, so it needs both columns on both rows.

Answer: 26 of 40 against 66 of 120, which is 65 percent against 55. Spring is the fuller season.

Closest trap: summer fills 66 beds against spring's 26. That is true of the hostel and answers whether it holds more people, which is on-topic-not-on-claim.

3. A level is not a change

A campsite counts the nights booked in each month of its season. A student claims that the monthly bookings climb steeply and then level off. The rise from April to June, the claim runs, is far larger than the rise from June to August.

Labels: the axis counts nights booked in that month, not nights booked so far. Each point is one month's own figure.

Claim: it names two stretches, April to June and June to August, and says the first rise is the larger.

Answer: the line runs 120 to 340 across the first stretch and 340 to 370 across the second, which is 220 nights against 30.

Closest trap: August is the highest point on the graph, at 370. That is a level, and a claim about where a rise happens is settled by the rises.

Common traps

The four wrong-choice types

Trap Correction
axis misread Check the figure against its heading, axis, legend or stated scale
on-topic-not-on-claim Ask what the claim needs, not what its subject is
true-but-partial Ask whether the choice reaches both sides of the comparison, counting what the passage already supplies
accurate-but-irrelevant Ask whether the sentence is finished, not whether it is true

A misread reads correctly off the wrong thing, so its numbers are on the graphic. Checking that a figure appears there rules nothing out; check that it is the figure the claim asks for. The other three traps are true of the graphic too.

The method: Identify the claim, read the labels, find the data

  1. Identify the claim. Say what the sentence needs the data to show: its subject, its direction, and whether it wants a count or a share.
  2. Read the graphic's title, axis labels, units and legend. Say what one bar, point or cell is. A backward scale, a column in thousands or a legend that names two series is stated here. Either order works; what does not work is skipping this.
  3. Find the data that bears on the claim. It is on the graphic or in the passage, and a target or baseline the claim leans on is often in the passage. Adding a graphic's own bars, or setting a part beside its total, is still reading it. A figure neither one can give you means a different question.
  4. Compare the choices for accuracy and for fit. Ask whether the sentence is finished, not only whether the number is right. An accurate number that completes nothing is still wrong.

Practice 1

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
  • Introductory

A cafe keeps beehives on its roof and weighs the honey taken off each hive at the end of every summer. A student writing about the roof claims that a single hive gave more than twice as much honey in 2024 as a single hive gave in 2021.

Year Hives on the roof Honey per hive (kilograms)
2021 5 11
2022 8 14
2023 9 19
2024 12 24

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

Trap: axis misread. 5 and 12 are hives on the roof, not kilograms of honey. The honey column reads 11 and 24. The wrong column happens to produce the comparison the claim wants.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Right column, and both cells read correctly, but 2023 against 2024 answers a different question. The claim names 2021 and 2024.
Correct. Both figures come from the honey column and from the two years the claim names. Twice 11 is 22, and 24 clears 22, which is what more than twice requires.
Trap: true-but-partial. More than twice is a comparison and this gives one side of it. Without the 2021 figure beside it, 24 kilograms settles nothing.

The method, applied

The claim fixes the column and the two rows: one hive's 2024 honey against its 2021 honey. The honey column reads 11 and 24, and twice 11 is 22. The closest wrong choice reads the hive column, where the counts also double, which is what makes the misread produce the comparison the claim wants.

Practice 2

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
  • Introductory

A harbor project replanted eelgrass at four beds and counted the shoots in one square meter of each bed the following August. The project treats a bed as established once it holds 200 shoots per square meter. A student writing about the project claims that the bed at Gullet has not taken hold yet.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

Correct. The claim names one bed and one threshold, so the evidence sets that bed's figure against 200. Gullet's bar reads 140, which is below the mark.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. The bar does read 320, and it is the tallest of the four. It is also a different bed, and nothing Longspit did settles whether Gullet reached 200.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Right bed, and true, but coming last is a rank and the project's test is a number. A bed could finish last and still clear 200.
Trap: axis misread. 210 is on the graph one bar to the right, on Skerry. Gullet's bar reads 140, so the neighbor's figure also reverses the claim.

The method, applied

The claim names one bed and one threshold, so the evidence is that bed's bar set against the dashed 200 mark: Gullet reads 140. The closest wrong choice also names Gullet and gives 210, which is the bar one place to the right, so the misread both changes the bed and reverses the claim.

Practice 3

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
  • Standard

The Tolbray Canal carried grain and coal between two market towns for forty years. A rail line running the same route opened in 1848. A student writing about the canal claims that the rail line did more than slow the canal's growth: the freight the canal lost in the two years after the line opened more than wiped out everything it had gained in the two years before.

Year Canal freight (thousands of tons) Rail freight (thousands of tons)
1846 210 none
1847 246 none
1848 258 40
1849 201 190
1850 158 305

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

Trap: axis misread. 190 and 305 are the rail line's tonnage. The canal's own cells for 1849 and 1850 read 201 and 158, which is a fall rather than a climb.
Trap: true-but-partial. The 1848 peak is the pivot both changes are measured from, which is why it feels central. Alone it names neither the gain nor the loss.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. True, and it answers whether the canal declined, which the student grants before asking something harder. Two falls of unstated size cannot compare them.
Correct. The claim sets one change against another, so the evidence carries both. 258 less 210 is a gain of 48; 258 less 158 is a loss of 100, more than double it.

The method, applied

The claim is not that the canal declined, which the table shows plainly. It is that the fall after 1848 was larger than the rise before it, so the evidence carries both changes from the canal column: a gain of 48 and a loss of 100. The closest wrong choice is the 1848 peak, which is the pivot both changes are measured from and names neither of them.

Practice 4

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
  • Standard

A city plants four species along its streets and checks each planting five years on. The city's arborist sorts the four into two that shrug off the salt spread on winter roads, ginkgo and London plane, and two that do not, hackberry and red maple. A student writing about the plantings claims that the arborist's split shows up in the survival figures: both of the species he calls salt-tolerant came through better than either of the other two. For example, ____

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the example?

Trap: true-but-partial. The claim sorts four species into two groups, so it needs all four rates. One species' rate leaves open how the other salt-tolerant species finished.
Correct. The claim compares two groups, so all four rates appear. The tightest pair decides it: ginkgo at 78 percent still sits above red maple at 60, so the groups do not overlap.
Trap: axis misread. The two figures are read off the bars and then read as the wrong thing. The axis is a survival percentage, and the graph never says how many trees were planted.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Both figures are right, and they compare the two salt-sensitive species with each other, which the claim never disputes. It says nothing about ginkgo or London plane.

The method, applied

The axis is the percentage of each planting alive after five years, and nothing here counts trees. The claim sorts the four species into two groups, which the passage supplies, so the evidence has to reach all four rates: the tightest pair is ginkgo's 78 percent against red maple's 60, and the groups do not overlap. The closest wrong choice gives London plane alone, which leaves ginkgo's finish open.

Practice 5

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
  • Advanced

For six years an upland farm has fenced sheep out of half of one meadow and let them graze the other half. Each June a surveyor counts the open flowers in a fixed square of each half. Last June the ungrazed square held 400 flowers in all and the grazed square held 100. Sheep crop most of the meadow's flowers but leave self-heal alone, since it flowers low and flat against the turf. A student writing about the trial claims that grazing did not simply thin the meadow, it changed what the meadow is made of: self-heal accounts for a far larger share of the flowers where the sheep graze than where they do not.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Both bars are read correctly and self-heal is the right subject, but a bare pair of counts answers whether self-heal itself got rarer. The claim is about its share.
Trap: true-but-partial. A share is a count out of a total, and this gives the total without the count. It shows the thinning that the claim sets itself against.
Correct. A share needs a count and a total, and this pairs both on each side of the fence: 30 of 100 is 30 percent, and 40 of 400 is 10 percent, so the share triples where the sheep graze.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. Every word is right, and it is the largest movement on the graph. It is the reason self-heal's share rose rather than evidence that it rose, and it never names self-heal.

The method, applied

The legend is what to read first here: the axis counts flowers, each species has an ungrazed bar and a grazed one, and there is no percentage on this graph. The claim is not that grazing thinned the meadow but that self-heal takes a larger share, and a share needs a part and a total. Self-heal's bars read 40 and 30 and the passage gives the totals, 400 and 100, so 10 percent against 30. The closest wrong choice is the bare pair of counts, which falls while the share triples.

Practice 6

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Quantitative)
  • Advanced

On one clear, moonless night an astronomy club drove the straight road east out of Harkness, stopping at five points to measure the sky overhead with a handheld meter. The meter reports sky brightness in magnitudes per square arcsecond, a scale that runs backward from ordinary speech: the larger the number, the darker the sky. A student writing about the outing claims that on this road nearly all of the darkening is bought in the first dozen kilometers, and driving on past that buys almost nothing.

Distance from Harkness (kilometers) Sky brightness (magnitudes per square arcsecond)
2 18.1
6 19.4
12 20.6
20 20.9
30 21.0

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

Correct. The claim sets one stretch of darkening against another, so the evidence carries both. 18.1 to 20.6 is a climb of 2.5, and 20.6 to 21.0 is 0.4. On this scale a climb is a darkening.
Trap: true-but-partial. It is true, and reading a backward scale correctly makes it feel earned. The claim is about where the darkening happens, and one endpoint is half of one comparison.
Trap: axis misread. Both figures are copied correctly and then the scale is read forward. The passage says a larger number is a darker sky, so 21.0 is the darkest reading, not the brightest.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. True: the readings never turn back. But the claim is about where the darkening is concentrated, and a run that never reverses says nothing about that.

The method, applied

The passage states the scale, and it runs backward: a bigger number is a darker sky. The claim is not that the sky darkens as you leave town but that the darkening is concentrated near it, and a claim about where a change happens is settled by two changes: 2.5 magnitudes across the first ten kilometers against 0.4 across the next eighteen. The closest wrong choice reads the two end figures correctly and then reads the scale forward, calling the darkest stop the brightest.
