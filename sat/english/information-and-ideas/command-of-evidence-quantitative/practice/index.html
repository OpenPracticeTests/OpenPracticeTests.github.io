Question

  • Introductory

A laundromat keeps a counter on each of its four washing machines. The owner, deciding which machine to have serviced next, claims that Machine 1 is doing markedly less work than Machine 2: over one week it ran 24 fewer cycles.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the owner's claim?

This sets Machine 2 against all three of the others. The claim sets it against Machine 1 alone, and being the busiest machine in the shop says nothing about the size of that one gap.
Correct. The claim names two machines and one figure, so the evidence is those two bars and the gap between them: 63 less 39 is 24.
One of the two figures the claim needs, beside a total the claim never asks for. A gap between two machines cannot be read off one machine's bar and the shop's week put together.
Right graph, right units, both bars read correctly, and neither machine is one the claim names. Whatever separates Machines 3 and 4 leaves the gap between 1 and 2 exactly where it was.

Why this answer

The claim fixes two machines and one quantity, so the evidence has to carry both readings and the gap between them. Machine 1's bar stands at 39 and Machine 2's at 63, and 63 less 39 is the 24 the sentence needs. Of the rest, one names the busiest machine in the shop, one sets Machine 1's figure beside the shop's whole week, and one reads a different pair of bars correctly.

Question

  • Introductory

The merchant an oyster farm sells to takes only the grade whose shells run between 70 and 100 millimeters long. Each August the farm measures a sample from every trestle and records the mean. A student writing about the farm claims that all five trestles averaged inside the merchant's grade this August.

Trestle Mean shell length (millimeters)
Bight 74
Craik 96
Doles 88
Ellin 81
Fother 79

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

One limit of the two, and a claim about a range needs both of them. That the longest trestle mean sits under 100 millimeters leaves open whether the shortest reached 70.
This measures the trestles against each other. The claim measures them against the merchant's two limits, and a spread of 22 millimeters would read the same if both trestles sat outside the grade.
Three of the five rows, and the three the claim never had to worry about. A claim about every trestle turns on the two means nearest the limits, and neither of those is here.
Correct. If the smallest mean clears the lower limit and the largest clears the upper one, every mean between them does too, so two readings settle all five rows.

Why this answer

A claim about a range is settled at its two ends: nothing between the smallest and the largest reading can fall outside limits that both of those clear. Bight's 74 is above 70 and Craik's 96 is below 100, which finishes the sentence in two readings. The rest give one end without the other, measure the trestles against each other rather than against the grade, or report the three rows that were never in doubt.

Question

  • Introductory

A town's local paper came out once a week for most of its life and went twice weekly in 2019. It counts the copies it sells in an average week and publishes the figure each year. A student writing about the paper claims that going twice weekly added 1,240 copies a week in its first year.

Year Copies sold in an average week
2016 3,700
2017 3,950
2018 3,880
2019 5,120
2020 5,260
2021 5,340

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

Correct. The claim names one year's gain, so the evidence is the two rows either side of the change and the gap between them: 5,120 less 3,880 is 1,240.
True, and it answers whether the paper kept growing rather than how much it gained in the first year. Three rises of unstated size cannot make any one of them 1,240 copies.
One of the two figures the claim needs, and the one the gain is measured from. A level in the year before says nothing about the size of the jump that followed it.
Right column, correct arithmetic, and the wrong two rows. The claim is about the single year the paper changed, not about the six years the table covers.

Why this answer

A claim about one year's gain needs the readings either side of that year, and nothing else on the table can supply it. The 2018 row reads 3,880 and the 2019 row 5,120, which is the 1,240 the sentence names. The others report that the paper kept growing, name the last weekly year without the jump, and measure the whole table end to end.

Question

  • Introductory

A coastal group runs a beach clean on the first Saturday of each summer month and counts every item it collects. Before the season the group set itself a target of 3,000 items for the four mornings together. A student writing about the season claims that the group met the target it had set.

Beach clean Items collected
May 612
June 845
July 1,104
August 738
Four cleans together 3,299

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

The largest figure in the table, and one morning of four. A target set for the season is met by the season, so the other three mornings have to be in the sum.
True, and it measures each morning against a figure the group never set. The target is a season total, and knowing that three of the four mornings passed 700 items leaves both the fourth and the sum unstated.
Correct. The target is a figure for the season, so the evidence is the season's figure: the table's last row reads 3,299, which clears 3,000.
This orders the four mornings against each other. The claim orders the season against a number, and which morning came first or last adds nothing to a total.

Why this answer

The target is a figure for four mornings together, so the row that answers it is the one for four mornings together: 3,299 items against a target of 3,000. Every other choice reports part of the table, the best morning, a floor the group never set, or the order the mornings finished in, and none of them reaches the season.

Question

  • Introductory

A community center lets four rooms by the evening. The Long Room is available every evening of the term; the other three are let only on the nights their regular groups do not need them. The booking clerk records how many evening slots each room was offered and how many were taken. A student writing about the center claims that fewer than half of the Long Room's evening slots were taken.

Room Evening slots offered Slots taken
Long Room 96 42
Studio 32 22
Annex 24 11
Loft 36 21

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

The Long Room really was booked on more evenings than any other room, and that is a count. The claim is about the share of its own slots that went, and a room with far more slots on offer can lead on the count and trail on the share.
Correct. A share needs the part and the whole, and the Long Room's row carries both: 42 of 96 is under half, which is what the claim says.
The whole without the part. How many slots a room had on offer cannot say how many of them went, and a room can be offered the most and still fill most of them.
Both figures are on the table and both sit on the Studio's row. The Long Room was offered 96 slots and took 42, so reading the row beneath it turns the claim on its head.

Why this answer

A share is a part over a whole, and both are on the row the claim names: 42 slots taken out of 96 offered is under half. The rest give the count without the share, the whole without the part, and a pair of figures lifted off the Studio's row, which is the one wrong reading here and the one the row labels settle.

Question

  • Introductory

A hill farm put an array on its barn roof at the start of the year and meters what it generates each month. The farm uses about 380 kilowatt hours a month itself. A student writing about the farm claims that the array first covered the farm's own use in April.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

Correct. A FIRST crossing needs every month before it, not only the month before: all three earlier months fall short of the line and April clears it, so April is where the array first covers the farm.
April on its own, and it does clear the line. The claim is that April is the FIRST month to clear it, and one month above the rule is equally consistent with an earlier month having cleared it too.
380 is on the graph and it is the dashed rule rather than a plotted point. April's point stands at 430, so reading the level line as a reading puts the array level with the farm when it is above it.
A spread, read correctly off the two ends of the line. How far the half year ranged cannot say in which month the line passed a particular level.

Why this answer

A claim about when a line FIRST passes a level needs every earlier reading, not only the one before the crossing: a single month above the line is consistent with an earlier month having cleared it too. January, February and March read 140, 210 and 320, all under the farm's 380, and April reads 430. The others give April on its own, read the dashed level as a plotted point, and measure the half year's spread instead of its crossing.

Question

  • Standard

An adult education center publishes, for each of its four evening courses, how many students enrolled in September and how many were still on the register at the final class. A student writing about the center claims that upholstery held on to a larger share of the students it enrolled than Greek did.

Course Enrolled Finished Share who finished
Upholstery 24 18 75%
Greek 40 22 55%
Life drawing 30 21 70%
First aid 16 8 50%

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

A count, and the largest one in its column. Which course finished with the most people says nothing about which kept the largest share of the people it started with, and Greek enrolled far more than upholstery did to begin with.
A share read correctly off a course the claim never mentions. Its 70 percent falls between the two shares in question and settles neither of them.
Correct. The claim compares two shares, and the last column gives both of them: 75 percent against 55.
The second half is right and the first is read off the wrong row: 70 percent is life drawing's share, two rows further down. Upholstery's row reads 75 percent.

Why this answer

The claim is about shares and no count will stand in for one: Greek finished with more people than upholstery only because it started with far more. The share column reads 75 percent against 55. The rest report a count, give a third course's share, and take life drawing's 70 percent for upholstery's, two rows above it.

Question

  • Standard

A castle runs guided tours of its keep and counts the visitors on each one. A student writing about the castle claims that a February tour carries more visitors on average than an August tour does, though August is by far the busier month.

Month Tours run Visitors carried Visitors per tour
August 96 2,880 30
February 24 960 40

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

A difference between two totals, and the claim concedes it in the same sentence. A month that runs four times the tours carries more visitors whether or not its tours are fuller.
The tours column, which is what makes August the busier month and is the half of the claim it concedes. How many tours ran is as consistent with a fuller February tour as with an emptier one.
Both numbers are on the table and both count tours rather than visitors. The last column is the one that counts visitors a tour, and it reads 40 and 30.
Correct. The claim is about how full a tour is on average, which the last column gives directly on both rows: 40 in February against 30 in August.

Why this answer

The claim is about an average tour and the table has a column for it: 40 visitors a tour in February against 30 in August. The first three columns are all about the month rather than the tour, which is the whole difficulty. The others give a gap between totals the claim already concedes, the tour counts that make August the busier month, and a pair of figures lifted from the tours column.

Question

  • Standard

A school canteen weighs what comes back on the trays. For one week it recorded how many portions of each main dish it served and how many portions came back untouched. A student writing about the canteen claims that the dish served most often is not the dish that returns the fewest portions.

Dish Portions served Portions returned
Bean stew 210 34
Fish pie 186 12
Pasta bake 264 41
Curry 198 22

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

A share, read correctly off both columns of one row. The claim compares two orderings across four dishes, and one dish's waste rate is not a place in either order.
Correct. The claim says two orders disagree, so the evidence names the top of each: pasta bake leads on portions served, and fish pie is the dish that returns the fewest.
Both figures are on fish pie's row with the two columns the wrong way round. The canteen served 186 portions of fish pie and 12 came back, which makes it the dish least often left rather than the least wanted.
Right column, both rows read correctly, and neither dish is at the top of either order. The claim is about which dish leads on each measure, and these two lead on neither.

Why this answer

The claim is that two orderings disagree, so the evidence has to name the leader of each: the dish served most and the dish returned least. Pasta bake tops the served column at 264 and fish pie the returned column at 12, and they are different dishes. The rest give one dish's waste rate, read fish pie's two figures in each other's columns, and compare two dishes that lead neither order.

Question

  • Standard

An asparagus bed takes several seasons to reach its full crop. A market garden planted its older bed in 2013 and its younger one in 2016, and weighs what it cuts from each every June. The grower claims the younger bed has caught up: the gap between the two beds in 2024 is a fraction of what it was in 2019.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

Correct. A closing gap is two differences, one at each end of the run: 46 against 22 is 24 kilograms, and 64 against 60 is four.
True of both lines, which is the difficulty here. Two rising lines can hold the same distance apart, widen, or close, so a direction cannot settle a claim about a gap.
One end of one line. The claim measures the distance between two beds at two moments, and this reaches neither the older bed nor 2019.
Both figures are on the graph and both belong to the older bed, which is the solid line. The younger bed is the dashed one, and it cut 22 and 60.

Why this answer

A gap is a difference, so a closing gap is two of them, one at each end of the run. The two beds stand 24 kilograms apart in 2019 and four apart in 2024. The others report that both lines rise, which is true and settles nothing about the distance between them, give one bed in one season, and read the older bed's figures as the younger bed's.

Question

  • Standard

A fire station's crew rota is drawn up on the assumption that no month is much busier than any other, and the station officer rewrites it if the swing between the busiest month and the quietest passes ten turnouts. A student writing about the station claims that the first half of this year bore the assumption out.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

A gap between two months, and not the one the rota is written against. A swing is measured from the busiest month to the quietest, and June is neither of those.
This orders the six months and stops there. The rota turns on how large the swing is rather than on which months sit at its two ends, and an order carries no size at all.
Correct. A swing is the distance between the extremes, so two readings settle all six months: 36 less 29 is seven, inside the ten the rota allows.
A monthly average, read correctly off all six bars. An average says where the six months sit and not how far apart the highest and the lowest are, which is the only thing the rota measures.

Why this answer

A swing is a spread, so it is settled by the two ends of the chart and by nothing in between: 36 turnouts in April against 29 in February is seven, well inside the ten the officer allows. The others give one month against a month that is neither extreme, name which months are the extremes without saying how far apart they are, and average the six rather than measuring between them.

Question

  • Standard

A village keeps a book exchange in a disused telephone box, with one shelf for children's books and one for adults'. A volunteer counts what is taken each quarter. A student writing about the exchange claims that the year's growth in books taken is almost entirely growth on the children's shelf.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

A share of one quarter, where the claim is about a change across four of them. More than half of the fourth quarter came off that shelf, and the first quarter is where the comparison starts.
True of the total and true of both shelves separately. That the exchange grew is where the claim starts; what it asserts is which shelf did the growing, and a rising total names neither.
Both figures are on the chart and both belong to the adults' shelf, which is the hatched part of each bar. The children's shelf gave up 42 books and then 126, a rise of 84.
Correct. A claim about which part carried a rise needs that part's rise set against the whole rise: the children's shelf gained 84 where the exchange gained 96.

Why this answer

The claim is about how a rise divides, so the evidence needs the part's rise and the whole rise together: 84 books of the 96 the exchange gained came off the children's shelf. The others give the children's share of one quarter, report that everything rose, and read the adults' segment as the children's, which is what the legend settles.

Question

  • Standard

A council tried a new grit mix on four hill roads, comparing the winter before the change with the winter after it and counting the days each road was shut. The roads engineer claims that the new mix made no difference the council could act on: adding the four roads' closures together, it saved one day of closure in a winter.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

One road of four, read correctly, and the largest movement on the chart. The claim is about what the four roads did together, and a road that improved by six sits beside three that got worse.
Correct. A claim about four roads together needs both columns added: 42 days against 41 is the one day the sentence names. These are days summed across the roads, not dates on which some road was shut.
One of the two totals the claim compares. A winter's total means nothing without the winter before it, and 41 days is as consistent with a saving of six as with a saving of one.
Both figures are Road A's and they are the wrong way round: the solid bar is the old mix and stands at 12, and the hatched one at 13. Road A was shut a day longer under the new mix, not a day less.

Why this answer

The claim is about four roads together, so it is settled by the two column totals and by nothing smaller: 42 days of closure summed across the roads against 41. Of the rest, one gives the single road that improved most while three others worsened, one gives the new mix's total with nothing to set it against, and one reads Road A's two bars the wrong way round, which the legend settles.

Question

  • Advanced

A sawmill records how many logs it cuts each year and what share of each log it gets out as usable board. It fitted a thinner saw blade in 2020. A student writing about the mill claims that its recovery in 2021 was twelve points better than its recovery in 2018.

Year Logs cut Recovery rate (percent)
2018 5,400 48
2019 5,900 48
2020 6,300 54
2021 6,700 60

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

The right column and the wrong two rows. The claim measures 2021 against 2018, and the single year the blade was fitted carries six of the twelve points that span those two.
Correct. The claim names two years and a gap between them, and the recovery column carries both readings: 48 and 60.
The other column, read correctly. How many logs a mill puts through says nothing about how much board it gets out of each one, which is what the recovery column measures.
Both figures are on the table and both count logs cut rather than boards recovered. The recovery column is a percentage of each log, and for those two years it reads 54 and 60.

Why this answer

The claim fixes two years and one column, so the evidence is those two cells: 48 percent in 2018 and 60 in 2021. The others take the rise across the wrong pair of years, report the logs column, and read that column's figures as boards, which the heading settles.

Question

  • Advanced

A farm shop grades its eggs by size and records what it sells. It sold 28,000 boxes last year and 21,000 this year, and large eggs took 31 percent of last year's sales against 38 percent of this year's. A student writing about the shop claims that it sold fewer boxes of large eggs this year than last, even though large eggs took a bigger share of what it sold.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

The claim's second half, which it grants before asking the harder question, and it is in the passage rather than on the graph. A rising share over a falling total can leave the count either way up, so a share on its own cannot say which way it went.
True of this year, and about the wrong comparison. The claim sets large eggs against large eggs a year earlier, and how they did against medium and small settles none of it.
Correct. The claim is about boxes, and the graph counts boxes: the large-egg pair reads 8,680 then 7,980. The share rose over a smaller year, and the count still fell.
Both numbers are on the page and both are the percentages the passage gives, not boxes. The graph's axis counts boxes, and its large-egg bars read in the thousands.

Why this answer

The claim is about boxes and the graph counts boxes, so the answer is the pair of large-egg bars: 8,680 last year and 7,980 this year. The shares are in the passage and they rise, which is exactly the difficulty: a bigger slice of a smaller year is fewer boxes. The others report the share the claim already grants, compare large eggs with the other sizes rather than with last year, and read the percentages themselves as boxes.

Question

  • Advanced

Gray seals pup on five beaches along one stretch of coast. A wildlife trust fenced the two beaches at Windrush and Kelder against dogs three winters ago and left the other three as they were.

Beach Pups counted
Windrush 340
Kelder 295
Sperrin 218
Nant 186
Torla 241

Across the five beaches the average count is 256 pups. A student writing about the trust claims that each of the fenced beaches now holds more pups than an average beach on this coast does. For example, ____

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the example?

Correct. The claim measures the fenced beaches against an average, so the evidence has to carry that average. It is on no row of the table; the passage supplies it.
One of the two beaches the claim is about, with nothing to measure it against. An average is a figure that has to stand beside a reading, and this gives the reading alone.
Right column, both rows read correctly, and neither beach is fenced. What the unfenced beaches did among themselves leaves the fenced ones exactly where they were.
A share where the claim needs a level. Two beaches holding half the pups between them is consistent with one of them sitting under the average, and the claim is about each of them.

Why this answer

The yardstick the claim uses is an average, and no row of the table carries it: the passage does, at 256 pups, and both fenced beaches clear it. The others give one fenced beach with nothing beside it, report the two unfenced beaches that were never in question, and offer a share of the coast's pups where the claim is about each beach against a level.

Question

  • Advanced

A folk-song archive lists how many field recordings it took in each decade of its collecting. Its annual report says the archive has added more recordings in each decade than in the decade before. A student writing about the report claims that the report is out of date: collecting climbed for four decades and then turned down.

Decade Recordings taken
1950s 210
1960s 348
1970s 502
1980s 611
1990s 684
2000s 470

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

The first half of the claim, and the half the annual report already makes. What the student adds is a turn at the end, and a run of rising decades cannot carry it.
A ratio across the decades the annual report is about. It measures how far the climb went and stops at the top of it, where what the claim adds is the decade after.
Right column, both rows read correctly, and the wrong end of the table. A rise near the beginning is what the annual report is about, and the claim is about a fall near the end.
Correct. A claim that a trend turned needs the climb and the fall that followed it, so the evidence names both: up by 474 across four decades, then down by 214 in one.

Why this answer

A turn is two movements in opposite directions, so the evidence has to carry a rise and the fall after it: 210 to 684 across four decades and then 684 to 470. The others give the climb the annual report already claims, measure how far that climb went without what followed it, and take a rise at the far end of the table from the one in dispute.

Question

  • Advanced

A drinks plant logs the minutes its bottling line stands idle and the cause of each stoppage. The plant's engineer has proposed a new capping head, which would remove the cap feed and crooked cap faults, leave the other four untouched, and so halve the time the line stands idle. A student writing about the proposal claims that it cannot halve anything, because the faults it removes are less than half of the line's lost time.

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the student's claim?

The longest bar, and a cause the new head does not touch. That one other fault is larger than either of them puts no useful bound on the pair at all, which is what a share of the whole needs.
Correct. A share needs the part and the whole, and the whole is on no bar: the six causes come to 680 minutes, and 170 of those is a quarter.
The part without the whole. A total is what turns 170 minutes into a share, and 170 minutes would be most of a quiet month and a fraction of a bad one.
Both figures are on the chart and neither counts stoppages. The axis is minutes of running time lost, and a chart of minutes cannot say how often the line stopped.

Why this answer

The claim is a share and the chart gives only the parts, so the total is something the student has to make: the six causes come to 680 minutes and the two the new head removes come to 170, which is a quarter. The others name the longest bar, give the part with no total beside it, and read an axis of minutes as a count of stoppages.

Question

  • Advanced

A town's parking garage holds 480 vehicles, and a counter on the barrier records how many spaces are taken at the top of each hour. The manager, deciding when to put a second attendant on the entrance, claims that the hour in which the garage fills fastest is not the hour in which it is fullest.

Hour Spaces taken Spaces free
8 am 96 384
9 am 212 268
10 am 305 175
11 am 368 112
12 noon 402 78
1 pm 417 63

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

One of the two hours the claim sets against each other, and the one it grants. Knowing when the garage is fullest cannot say when it filled fastest, which is the half in dispute.
Both figures are on the table and both count the spaces still free rather than the vehicles in them. At 8 am 96 spaces were taken and at 9 am 212, so the garage was filling.
Correct. A claim that two hours differ has to name each of them: 96 to 212 is a rise of 116, larger than any later step, and the peak comes four hours afterwards at 417.
Right column, both rows read correctly, and neither hour is at the top of either measure. A middling rise in the middle of the morning leaves both halves of the claim untouched.

Why this answer

The claim sets a rise against a level, so the evidence has to carry both and show they fall in different hours: the largest hourly rise is the 116 between 8 and 9, and the fullest hour is 1 pm. The others give the level the claim grants, report a rise in the wrong hours, and read the spaces-free column as a count of vehicles, which turns a filling garage into an emptying one.

Question

  • Advanced

A car club runs a mixed fleet and books it by the hour. The club's year-end report said that its electric cars had gone past petrol in the final quarter of the year. A student writing about the club claims the report is a quarter late: electric bookings passed petrol in the third.

Quarter Electric bookings Petrol bookings
First 410 980
Second 645 870
Third 905 760
Fourth 1,240 690

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the student's claim?

Correct. A FIRST crossing needs every earlier quarter, not only the one before: petrol is ahead in both of them and behind in the third, so the lead changes hands there.
A rise in one column across the whole year. The claim is about the quarter in which one column went past the other, and how far electric climbed cannot say when it drew level.
Both figures are on the table and the two columns are the wrong way round. Electric took 410 bookings in the first quarter and petrol 980, which is the gap the rest of the year closes.
One column, read at three moments and never against the other. The claim is about which column is in front, and electric climbing past its own earlier quarters says nothing about where petrol stood while it did.

Why this answer

A claim about which quarter a lead FIRST changed hands needs every earlier quarter, because one quarter in front is consistent with having gone in front sooner. Petrol leads 980 to 410 and 870 to 645, then trails 760 to 905. The others measure electric's climb across the year, swap the two columns in the first quarter, and double electric against itself without reaching petrol at all.
