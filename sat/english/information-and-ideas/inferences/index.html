Inferences

  • Information and Ideas
  • Inferences
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

A text stops mid-sentence at a blank, and the four choices complete it. The answer is the completion the text supports best.

Key takeaways

  • Read the text as evidence and the blank as the conclusion it best supports.
  • Compare the four: the answer is the one the text backs most strongly, not the only one it allows.
  • Match the completion's certainty to the evidence. Do not simply take the weakest choice.
  • An absolute sentence licenses an absolute completion about that same thing.
  • A choice may paraphrase, but the text has to support what it claims.

What the SAT may ask

  • Conclude what two or more premises give only together.
  • Choose between a strong and a hedged version of one claim.
  • Finish a text whose last premise blocks the strong reading.
  • Say what an observation is evidence of.

The rule

The text is a short argument with its last line removed, and the blank is that line. Everything the text asserts above the blank is evidence, and it is the only material you may build with.

The answer is the completion the evidence supports best. It does not have to be the only conclusion the text allows, and usually is not. The question is comparative: of these four, which does the text back most strongly?

Two things decide that. A completion that uses more of the text beats one that stops after the first sentence. One that adds a cause, a number or a certainty the text does not carry loses to one that does not.

Match the certainty to the evidence.

What the text gives What the completion may claim
a hedge: some, often, may no more than that hedge
an absolute: only, every, nothing but, no ... at all the absolute that sentence gives
a later sentence that blocks the strong reading the weakened version left standing

A hedged choice is not automatically the safe one.

Why the rule exists

A gap you fill from memory feels the same as one you fill from the text. The blank makes you write the join out, so the two come apart.

Choose the move

The question asks forFirst move
A late premise that blocks the obvious conclusionTake the weakened version, not the strong one
A sentence your answer never usedLook for a choice that reaches it too
Two choices at different strengthsFind the sentence that carries the claim, and take its certainty

Worked examples

1. A near-certain conclusion

The umbrella stand inside the office door was empty when Dara unlocked the building at eight. By noon it held four umbrellas, and the floor around the stand was wet. It follows that ____

A. the four umbrellas were brought in wet.

B. it rained without stopping all morning.

C. everyone in the office owns an umbrella.

D. the office has no cloakroom for wet coats.

The details: the stand was empty at eight, held four umbrellas by noon, and the floor around it was wet.

Answer: A. Four umbrellas came in from outside between eight and noon and something wet stood in that spot. About as close to certain as three sentences get, and still an inference: it reads the wet floor to the umbrellas above it.

B adds certainty the text has not got. Wet umbrellas say it rained, not for how long.

C is allowed and not supported. Four umbrellas fit an office of four, of forty, or one person who owns four.

D brings in a cloakroom the text says nothing about, either way.

2. A qualified conclusion, and two ways of overreaching

The dairy leaves one bottle of milk on each subscriber's step before six every morning and takes the empties away. On Thursday morning the step at number nine held three full bottles, none of them opened. It is reasonable to conclude that ____

A. the milk at number nine has probably stood there for more than a day.

B. the household at number nine is away.

C. the neighbors have complained about the smell.

D. the dairy has been leaving more bottles than the subscription calls for.

The details: one bottle is left every morning, and three full unopened bottles stood there on Thursday.

Answer: A. Three bottles at one a morning is best explained by nobody taking them in. Probably is doing real work: a supported inference is allowed to be probable.

B is what a reader supplies without noticing. Every word is ordinary, and the step from uncollected milk to an empty house is one the text never takes. That is outside knowledge, the harder trap, because nothing in it looks imported.

C names neighbors and a smell that appear nowhere: unanchored noun.

D is the alternative the text leaves open, offered as a conclusion. Leaving something open is not supporting it.

3. A late premise blocks the strong reading

Until postal sheets were perforated in the 1850s, stamps were cut apart with scissors, so an early stamp's edges are straight on all four sides. A stamp torn from a perforated sheet keeps a ragged fringe of teeth. Collectors have always trimmed the teeth from a damaged stamp to tidy it. A stamp with four straight edges therefore ____

A. must have been printed before the 1850s.

B. may be an early stamp, though its edges cannot show it.

C. is worth less than the same stamp with its teeth intact.

D. was cut from its sheet with scissors.

The details: straight edges mean scissors, teeth mean a perforated sheet, and collectors have always trimmed the teeth from a damaged stamp.

Answer: B. The third sentence decides it. Trimming turns a late stamp into a straight-edged one, so straight edges stop settling the date.

A reads the first two sentences and stops before the third: overreach (must/all).

C brings in a stamp's worth, which the text never weighs.

D is A in plainer clothes: trimmed teeth leave straight edges too.

Common traps

How a wrong completion fails

Trap Correction
overreach (must/all) Match the text's strength, and check whether a sentence is absolute
true-but-partial Name the sentence the choice leaves idle, and find one that uses it
unanchored noun Point at the thing named. The text neither states it nor supports it
outside knowledge Find the step on the page. The text never supplies it

The method: What do these details support most strongly?

  1. Count what the text asserts. Read it as a list of statements rather than as prose. Two statements and an explicit therefore is an entry-level item; four, with one of them apparently doing nothing, is a hard one.
  2. Ask what those details support most strongly, then compare the four. A choice that reaches more of the text usually beats one that stops after the first sentence. The sentence you were about to leave idle is often where the answer is.
  3. Match the certainty to the evidence. Where two choices say one thing at two strengths, the text decides. A sentence carrying only, every, nothing but or no ... at all licenses must and all about the thing that sentence is absolute about. Do not take the weakest choice by reflex.
  4. Ask what the choice ADDS. A cause, a number, a motive or a certainty the text does not carry is what sinks a completion. Bringing in a word the text never printed does not, as long as the text supports the claim.

Practice 1

  • Introductory

A borough's tax roll lists every household that owed the yearly levy, and the borough's poorest families, who owed nothing, were left off it entirely. A historian who counts the names on one year's roll and calls that total the number of households in the borough will therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Trap: overreach (must/all). The roll is still a floor: the borough held at least as many households as the roll names, so anything at all is more than the text can pay for.
Trap: true-but-partial. True on the first premise alone, which leaves the second premise, that the poorest families were left off, with nothing to do.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at the wealthiest households. The text names the poorest families and nobody else, so the reason given for the undercount is imported.
Correct. Every name on the roll is a household, and the poorest households are not named, so the roll's total is smaller than the borough's.

The method, applied

The roll names the households that owed the levy, and the poorest owed nothing and are off it, so its total falls short of the borough's. The closest wrong choice stops at the first sentence and calls the roll accurate for the levy payers, which leaves the second sentence idle. Nothing at all throws away a roll that is still a floor.

Practice 2

  • Information and Ideas
  • Inferences
  • Introductory

A leather sole wears away only where it meets the ground, and it wears fastest where the walker's weight rests longest. A curator who finds a pair of eighteenth-century shoes worn through along the outer edge of each heel, and barely marked anywhere else, can therefore conclude that ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Trap: overreach (must/all). The text says the sole wears fastest where the weight rests longest, not that it wears nowhere else, and barely marked leather met the ground.
Correct. A sole wears fastest where the weight rests longest, and these soles are most worn at the outer heel, so the weight rested there.
Trap: outside knowledge. Every noun is on the page, but the step from one wear pattern to one wearer is not: two people who walk alike leave the same shoe.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at several years. The premises fix where a sole wears and where it wears fastest, and nothing on the page turns wear into time.

The method, applied

A rule and a case: leather wears fastest where the weight rests longest, and these soles are worn through at the outer heel, so that is where the weight sat. The closest wrong choice takes it to no part but the outer heel ever met the ground, which the words barely marked contradict. Years and a single wearer are steps this text does not supply.

Practice 3

  • Information and Ideas
  • Inferences
  • Standard

Firing a village's bread oven took a cartload of wood and half a morning, and the bricks then held their heat until evening. Once the oven was hot, each further batch of loaves cost nothing but the time to slide it in and pull it out. A week's bread for one household was a dozen loaves; the oven, once fired, would take two hundred before the bricks cooled. It is therefore unsurprising that ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct. The whole cost is the firing, the firing costs the same whatever follows, and a household's dozen loaves stand against the oven's two hundred. So loaves get cheaper the more of them one fire bakes.
Trap: outside knowledge. Every noun is on the page, and the claim may be true, but this text counts cartloads and loaves and never weighs what comes out of the oven.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at the oven's keeper. There is no keeper on this page, no payment and no share, and fitting the text's world is not the standard.
Trap: true-but-partial. It follows from the first two premises and stops, leaving the dozen loaves against two hundred, the premise the sentence is reaching for, idle.

The method, applied

Three statements chain: the firing is the whole cost, it costs the same whatever follows, and a household's dozen loaves stand against the oven's two hundred. The cost a loaf falls as loaves are added, so one fire for the village is what the text supports. The closest wrong choice, that the cost was settled before a loaf went in, follows from the first two and leaves the dozen against two hundred idle. A keeper and a share of every batch are supported nowhere.

Practice 4

  • Information and Ideas
  • Inferences
  • Standard

In a coppice wood, a tree is cut off at the ground, and from the stump, which woodsmen call the stool, a crowd of shoots springs up; twelve or fifteen years later each is a pole thick enough to cut in its turn. The cutting does not kill the stool, and some stools still cut on this cycle were first cut before the sixteenth century. A visitor sees a low thicket of slender shoots and takes the wood for a young one. That impression ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Trap: overreach (must/all). The text is explicit that the shoots are young, so a wood of ancient stools and new shoots is not one in which nothing is young.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at the soil. Nothing in the text says anything about it, new or old, so nothing here supports a claim about it.
Correct. The shoots are young by the cycle the text describes, and the text also says a stool outlives its cuttings, some of them since before the sixteenth century. It does not say how old THESE stools are, which is why the completion says the impression need not hold rather than that it is false.
Trap: outside knowledge. Every noun is anchored, but a purpose is a premise and this text supplies none: it says what a cutting does, never why anyone cuts.

The method, applied

Two things stand in front of the visitor: shoots the cycle keeps young, and stools the cutting does not kill, some of which have been cut since before the sixteenth century. The visitor reads one age off both. The text does not say how old THESE stools are, so the supported completion says the impression need not hold for them, not that it is false. The closest wrong choice flattens the same reading into nothing in a coppice wood is young, which the text denies outright. Soil is supported nowhere.

Practice 5

  • Information and Ideas
  • Inferences
  • Advanced

A lantern slide is a photographic positive on glass, and the plates in use in the 1890s recorded light and shade alone: every slide came out of the camera in grays. The color on a surviving slide was laid over the image afterward, in transparent dye, by a colorist working under a lens with a brush of a few hairs. The coloring was done in workshops, and a workshop might tint two hundred copies of one view in a week. Its colorists had never stood in the places they were tinting; they worked from the workshop's own stock of dyes and, at best, from a line of description written on the slide's paper binding. Where two copies of one slide survive today, their skies are often different colors. The colors on a surviving lantern slide are therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Trap: overreach (must/all). The clause after since is true and the verdict is not: the premises kill the colors as testimony about the view without making them useless.
Correct. The camera did not supply the color, since the plate recorded gray, and the view did not, since the colorists had never been there. What caused a color is what that color is evidence of.
Trap: outside knowledge. Every noun is anchored, and the step from no motive to lie to an accurate record is the reader's: a colorist who had not seen the place invents the color honestly.
Trap: true-but-partial. True, and it uses the first premise and stops, leaving the three sentences about the workshop with nothing to do.

The method, applied

Track the color to its source. Not the light, since the plate recorded gray, and not the place, since the colorists had never been there, which leaves the workshop and its stock of dyes. Two copies of one slide differing in the sky is what settles it: if the view had fixed the color, they could not disagree. So the answer is comparative, that the color tells you more about the workshop than about the view. Of no use at all is a verdict the text never reaches, and every choice here is built from the text's own words, so looking for an imported noun finds nothing.

Practice 6

  • Information and Ideas
  • Inferences
  • Advanced

A thatched roof is two roofs at once. Down the long slopes, the reed is combed so that every stem lies steeply nose-down and the weather meets nothing but the cut butts, packed shoulder to shoulder. At the ridge, the spine where the two slopes meet, that arrangement is impossible: the stems have to be bent across the top, and once bent they lie with their open sides to the sky. One estate's books let the two be compared without guesswork. Its thatcher laid the slopes and the ridge from a single year's cut of reed, to one thickness, with his own hands, and the estate then paid to have the ridge renewed about every ten years while it left the slopes alone for thirty-five. It follows that the difference between ten years and thirty-five is ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Trap: overreach (must/all). The books record one estate, one thatcher and one reed, which supports a claim about this roof and not about every thatched roof, and supports it rather than proving it.
Trap: outside knowledge. Every noun is anchored and the step is not: this text separates the two parts by how their stems lie and never says what height does to weather.
Trap: unanchored noun. Point at the tougher stems. Both parts came from a single year's cut of one reed, worked to one thickness, so there are none on this page.
Correct. Same reed, same cut, same thickness, same hands: the books have taken the material and the workmanship off the table, and the one difference they leave standing is how the stems lie. That is what the estate's books support, and better explained is as far as one estate's books reach.

The method, applied

One sentence in this text exists to remove explanations: both parts came from one cut of reed, to one thickness, by one thatcher. That takes the material and the workmanship off the table and leaves the difference the first two sentences named, which is how the stems lie. One estate's books support better explained by and not proof, and they reach this roof rather than every thatched roof. Tougher stems the text has already refused, and what height does to weather it never says.
