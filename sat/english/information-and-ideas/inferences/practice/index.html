Question

  • Introductory

A sunshine recorder is a glass sphere that focuses the sun onto a card behind it and scorches a trace along the card for as long as the sun is on the glass. The station's recorder has stood on the same post since 1908, and its card is changed and measured every evening. A beech behind the post has grown until, for the last thirty years, the sphere has stood in the beech's shadow from four in the afternoon onward. The station's totals over those thirty years therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The key's own claim turned up too far. The card takes a trace for as long as the sun is on the glass, and the sun is on it every morning, so what the shadow takes is the late part of a day and not the day.
Correct. The card takes a trace only while the sun is on the sphere, and for those thirty years the sphere is in shadow after four. Nothing else has to be true about the weather for the totals to be short by whatever the afternoon was doing.
The weather in either stretch is nowhere on this page. The passage says what the sphere can see and what a beech now hides from it, and it counts no clouds in any year.
There is no station on the hill on this page. A second station is the kind of thing the sentence seems to want, and no premise supplies one.

Why this answer

Two sentences carry it together: a trace is scorched only while the sun is on the glass, and for those thirty years the glass is in the beech's shadow after four. Neither is left idle, which is what the closest rival cannot say. Taking the record down to nothing throws away the mornings the sphere still sees, the region's cloud cover is a thing this passage never counts, and a second station is not on the page at all.

Question

  • Introductory

The act that built the bridge let its trustees take a toll from every vehicle that crossed, and exempted nothing but the mail. The keeper of the tollhouse entered each vehicle in a day book as it came, and wrote in the toll column whatever toll was due on it. Sixteen entries in the book for 1841 have that column left blank. Those sixteen entries ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The claim's strength kept and its reach widened out of the book. What sixteen entries can settle is sixteen crossings the keeper wrote down; a vehicle that crossed and was never entered is outside the evidence altogether.
True, and it stops one step short. A blank column means nothing was due, which the column's own definition gives; what it never uses is the act, and the act is the premise that says what a vehicle owing nothing has to be.
Correct. The column holds whatever toll was due, so a blank means nothing was due, and the act leaves exactly one way for nothing to be due.
This supplies a reason for the blank that the passage rules out by defining the column. The keeper wrote in what was DUE, not what he had collected, and what is due is fixed by the act rather than by when he got round to it.

Why this answer

The column records what was due, so a blank says no toll was due, and the act allows nothing to be due only for the mail. Both sentences are in use, which is what the closest rival cannot say. Stopping at what the entries owed leaves the act idle, the keeper's unfinished charging is a reason the column's own definition excludes, and no entry can speak for a crossing that was never entered.

Question

  • Introductory

A tower mill grinds only while its sails face the wind, so the cap that carries them has to be turned whenever the wind shifts. Some mills turn their own: a fantail mounted behind the sails catches any wind coming across them and drives the cap round until the sails are square to it again. The mill on the ridge has no fantail. Its cap comes round on an endless chain that hangs to within reach of the ground and is hauled by hand. A reader who knows only this much about the mill can conclude that ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct. The cap has to come round when the wind shifts if the mill is to keep grinding, the thing that brings a cap round by itself is absent, and what is left is hauled by hand from the ground.
A missing fitting invites a date and the passage supplies none. It says this mill has no fantail; it never says why, and a mill can be older than a fitting or simply cheaper than one.
A purpose the passage never gives. That the chain reaches the ground is on the page; what it was FOR is a thought a reader supplies, and a plausible one.
There is no boy in this passage, and nothing in it counts working days. What is forced is that somebody had to be there when the wind moved, which is not the same as somebody standing by all day.

Why this answer

The first sentence makes turning the cap a condition of grinding, the second names the thing that does it without a person, and the third takes that thing away and leaves a chain that is hauled by hand. All three sentences are in use. A date for the absence, a purpose for the chain, and a boy to pull it are all things the passage leaves open.

Question

  • Standard

The nymph of this mayfly spends a year on the bed of the stream, grazing and laying down the fat its adult will fly on. The adult that climbs out of the water lives about two days and has no working mouthparts at all: the gut is sealed and there is nothing to swallow with. Adults have been kept in cages and offered every food a mayfly might meet, and not one has ever taken any. An adult of this mayfly therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

True, and it is the second and third sentences read back. The year on the stream bed, and the fat laid down in it, are then doing nothing, and they are what the sentence is reaching for.
An account of how the fat is spent, which sounds like physiology and is not on this page. The passage says what the adult cannot take in and where its fat came from; it says nothing about the rate at which anything goes out.
The passage never divides the year's feeding into shares. Two quantities are being set against each other here and the text fixes neither.
Correct. The fat is laid down in the year before and nothing goes in after the adult leaves the water, so that year's store is all it has. The sealed gut is what makes the absolute fair here rather than an overreach.

Why this answer

The nymph lays down the fat, and a sealed gut with no mouthparts at all means nothing is added to it afterward, so that store is everything the adult has for its two days. Reading the mouthparts back leaves the nymph's year idle, and both the rate of spending and the share of the year's feeding are quantities the passage never fixes.

Question

  • Introductory

A novel of the 1830s was sold as folded sheets stitched into boards, with the folds along the top and outer edges of the leaves still closed. A reader had to cut each fold open with a knife to reach the pages inside it. The copy in the case has been cut as far as page 96, and every fold beyond that is closed. Whoever has owned this copy ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The likeliest thing in the world and not a thing the passage says. A reader can stop for a hundred reasons, and the page offers none of them.
Correct. Reaching the pages inside a fold means cutting it open, and every fold past 96 is closed. The claim is about access to those pages and stops there, which is as far as an uncut fold reaches.
The same claim pushed past what the copy shows. Somebody took a knife to every fold as far as page 96, which is not the act of a reader who never opened it.
A later owner is not on this page. The premises reach as far as what has been cut, and no further.

Why this answer

The knife stands between the reader and the page, so an uncut fold is a fold whose pages nobody could get at, and every fold past 96 is uncut. That is a claim about access and the passage supports it outright. Cut folds show the opposite kind of thing and no more: somebody opened the way to those pages, which is why the choice saying the book was never opened goes too far. Why the reading stopped, and who the copy was left for, the passage does not carry at all.

Question

  • Introductory

To copy a cut inscription before photography, a scholar beat damp paper into the letters with a stiff brush and lifted the sheet away when it had set, so that the paper held the shape the stone's face had at the hour of the beating. The squeezes of the abbey doorway were taken in 1878. The doorway has stood in the open ever since, and the lettering that was crisp then is shallow now. The squeezes are therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct. A squeeze holds the face the stone had at the hour it was taken, and the face has changed since, so what the sheet carries is the older one.
The key's claim at a strength the premises do not reach. The lettering is shallow, which is not the same as gone, and a shallow letter can still be read off the stone it is cut in.
A motive for a decision the passage reports without explaining. It says the doorway has stood in the open; it never says anybody weighed the alternative or why.
True, and it comes off the first sentence alone. The weathering, which is the whole of the second half of the passage, is then left with nothing to do.

Why this answer

A squeeze holds one moment's surface and the surface has moved on, so the sheet and the stone now carry different faces and the sheet's is the 1878 one. Both halves of the passage are in use. Calling the squeezes the only reading left writes off the stone, the shelter is a decision nobody on this page makes, and an impression rather than a drawing is the first premise with the second unused.

Question

  • Standard

The surveyors measured each village well by lowering a weighted line until the weight came to rest and the line went slack, then reading the mark that stood at the well-head. At the well behind the chapel their forty-meter line ran out to its end and never went slack, which the field notebook records in as many words. The printed table gives that well's depth as forty meters. The table's figure for that well ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

True of the survey and idle about this figure. The notebook's sentence, which says the line ran out here, is the premise the passage stopped to print, and this choice never touches it.
A line paid out to its end that never slackened does put the bottom below forty meters, so the figure is short. The passage fixes no second length, so by how much is a number nobody on this page has.
Correct. A reading is taken when the weight lands and the line slackens, and here nothing slackened, so the forty meters in the table is the only measurement the survey made there: its own line, paid out to the end. What the well's depth is, this survey did not find out.
The natural story for how a table comes to say this, and a story the passage does not tell. It says what the notebook records and what the table prints, and nothing about who moved one to the other.

Why this answer

The method turns a slack line into a depth, and here nothing slackened, so the forty meters is a reading of the line rather than a finding about the well. That the well runs deeper than forty meters follows; what its depth IS the survey never found, which is why the completion says the figure does not establish it. How much deeper, and how the figure got into print, are both left open, and the method used at the other wells does nothing about this one.

Question

  • Standard

The dictionary was built out of slips: a volunteer copied a sentence from a printed book onto a half-sheet, wrote at its head the word the sentence illustrated, and posted it in. A sense went in only where slips had come for it, and the number of slips settled how much room it got. The circular sent to volunteers asked them to watch for words used oddly and to let the ordinary run of the language go by, and the slips bear it out: for a word in everyday use, the sentences that came in are nearly all of its odder turns. The dictionary's treatment of a common word therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A reason for the thinness, and the passage gives none. It says what the volunteers were asked for and what came in; whether anybody meant the result is not on the page.
No editors and no earlier dictionary are on this page. The only evidence the passage lets into the book is the slips.
An account of why coverage would be thin that the passage does not give. What it gives is a rule about which sentences were copied and a report of what came in, and those are enough on their own.
Correct. Room follows the number of slips, and the slips for a common word are nearly all of its odd turns, so its plainest senses are the ones with least behind them.

Why this answer

Room is decided by slips and the slips that came in for a common word are nearly all of its odd uses, so the two premises together push its plainest senses to the back. A purpose for the thinness, a second dictionary to check against, and the number of senses a word carries are all outside what the passage prints.

Question

  • Introductory

A crack running through rock cannot be older than the rock it runs through, since until the rock was there nothing was there to crack. The face of the cutting shows four beds of sandstone laid one on another, the lowest first, and a single crack rises from the foot of the face through the lowest three; it is filled to the brim with the green sand that makes up the fourth bed, and that bed lies unbroken across the top of it. The crack ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct. It cuts the third, so it is younger than the third; and it stood open to be filled with the fourth bed's own sand, so it was there before that sand arrived.
The passage puts the crack between two beds and fixes no interval at all. How long the beds took to arrive is not on the page.
A cause for the crack, and the passage names none. It says when the crack was open and what ran into it; what pulled it apart is a thought a reader supplies, and drying ground is the usual one.
True, and it follows from the first sentence and the lowest bed alone. The fill and the unbroken fourth bed, which are what the passage went out of its way to print, are then doing nothing.

Why this answer

A crack is younger than what it cuts, which fixes one end; and a crack filled with the fourth bed's sand, under a fourth bed that runs unbroken over it, was open before that sand came down, which fixes the other. Only the answer uses both ends. An interval in years and a cause for the opening are things the premises do not reach, and the weaker claim about the lowest bed leaves the fill idle.

Question

  • Standard

A chimney pulls air through a fire because the column of hot gas inside it weighs less than a column of outside air of the same height, and the pull grows as the gap between the two weights grows. Colder outside air is heavier air. The stove in the cottage is fed the same weight of wood each evening, laid the same way, and its air door was set once in the autumn and has not been touched since; on the coldest nights of the winter it burns through that wood fastest. That the stove burns fastest on the coldest nights is therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

What anybody would say about a cold night, and nothing on this page is about what the cottage wants or takes. The passage measures the stove's evening by the wood it is given and by the pull above it, and by nothing else.
Correct. The wood, its laying and the air door are all held where they were, so the extra burning is bought by the pull above rather than by anything the stove is given: heavier air outside, a wider gap in weight, a harder draw through the fire.
A second mechanism, and a believable one, put where the passage has already put a different one. The passage traces the cold to the pull through the fire and never to anything happening in the wood.
True, and it comes off the ration alone. It says what the answer is not, and leaves the two sentences about the chimney, which say what it is, with nothing to do.

Why this answer

The fuel, its laying and the air door are all held fixed, so whatever makes one evening fiercer than another is not in the stove, and the first two sentences supply the only candidate the passage names: colder air, a heavier column outside, a stronger pull. A household's need and a second mechanism in the fuel are outside the premises, and the ration read back on its own leaves the chimney unused.

Question

  • Standard

The draft that survives from this weaver's pattern book is a grid on paper. Its upper part says which shaft each warp thread is drawn through; its lower part, the tie-up, says which shafts each treadle lifts. It stops there. The order in which the treadles were pressed is nowhere on the sheet, and that order the weaver kept in her head. Two lengths of cloth with nothing in common to look at can come off one threading pressed in two orders. The draft by itself ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

There is no museum on this page and nothing here is being valued. The passage says what the sheet holds and what it leaves out, which is a different kind of statement from what it is worth.
A reason for the gap, and a fair guess: the passage does say the order lived in the weaver's head. What it never says is that this is WHY the sheet stops where it does.
Knitting is not on this page. The passage sets one sheet against two cloths, never one craft's notation against another's.
Correct. Both halves of the sheet are named and they are the threading and the tie-up; the treadling is not on it, and two orders give two unlike cloths, so the cloth is not fixed.

Why this answer

The first premise puts the threading and the tie-up on the sheet, the second takes the treadling off it, and the third shows what that costs: one threading, two cloths that look nothing alike. The sheet's silence and the cloth's difference are both in use. A museum's valuation and a knitting pattern are things this page never raises, and the reason the sheet stops is a motive it never gives.

Question

  • Standard

The scheme puts a number on each book by measuring two things: the average length of its sentences, and how often its words appear on a list of the commonest few thousand. It measures nothing else. A child who already knows about ships will read a long sentence about rigging more easily than a short one on a subject she has never met. This suggests that a book's number ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The key's claim at a strength the premises do not reach. Sentence length and rare words are features of the book and do bear on difficulty; what the third premise shows is that they are not the whole of it, which is a smaller claim than none of it.
True, and it is the first two sentences read back. The child who knows about ships is then left with nothing to do, and she is the reason the passage has a third sentence.
Correct. The number is built from two things and nothing else, and a third thing the number cannot see changes how easily a particular child reads. So the two can come apart, and the modest form is what the premises reach.
Older schemes are not on this page. Whether this one is rougher than anything else would need a second measure, and the passage supplies none.

Why this answer

The measure is closed at two features and a third feature moves the reading, so the number and the difficulty can part company for a given child. What the scheme measures and what familiarity moves are both in use, and the modest form is what the text reaches. Saying the number is not about the book at all goes past what the premises give, the two features read back leave the child idle, and a comparison with older schemes has nothing on the page to rest on.

Question

  • Standard

A violin carries a printed label inside the back, read through the left sound hole, giving a maker's name and a year, and makers have glued them in since the trade began. From the early nineteenth century a workshop building to an admired maker's pattern printed that maker's name on the label as well, openly and by the custom of the trade, as a statement of the model rather than of the hand. The label in this instrument is original to it and has never been disturbed. What the label establishes ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The key's own claim with its may taken off. The custom makes the pattern reading available; it does not make it the only one, because makers have labeled their own work throughout and this label may be one of those.
Correct. An original, undisturbed label rules out a later forger and rules out nothing else, because the second premise puts an honest label on an instrument its named maker never touched.
The overreach from the other side, and it takes the custom for a rule. Makers have glued in labels of their own work throughout, so a label of these years may still name the hand; and an original, undisturbed label establishes at least that nobody swapped it. A hedge does not rescue a completion that concludes nothing.
True, and it is the third sentence read back. The custom, which is the whole middle of the passage, is then left doing nothing.

Why this answer

An undisturbed label settles that nobody swapped it, and the custom then blocks the step from there to the hand: an honest label of these years may name the model instead. That is why the supported completion keeps its may. Taking the may off picks one of two readings the page leaves open, taking the custom for a rule empties the label of everything including the nonreplacement it does establish, and reading the undisturbed label back leaves the custom, which is the whole middle of the passage, doing nothing. Two of the four carry a may and only one of those is the answer, which is the point: a hedge is not what makes a completion safe.

Question

  • Advanced

She published thirty-one stories and collected none of them. Each went to whichever magazine had room, and a magazine printed a story it had bought whenever an issue came up short, holding the rest over, sometimes for a year and more. Her notebooks give a date at the head of every story, and those dates run in a different order from the printings: the story printed twelfth was written before the story printed fourth. The modern edition prints the thirty-one in the order they were published and calls that the order she wrote them in. The edition's arrangement is therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct. What decided a printing was an issue coming up short, the notebook dates run otherwise, and the edition prints the one while naming the other.
The claim's strength kept and its reach widened. The passage shows one pair out of order, which is enough to break the edition's name for the order and not enough to make the whole of it useless.
No editor settles anything here. What settles a printing on this page is whether an issue came up short, which is nobody's plan for the sequence.
There is one edition in this passage. A comparison needs a second, and none is on the page.

Why this answer

The printings were decided by what a magazine had room for, the notebooks give a different order, and the edition prints the first under the second's name, so every one of the four is in use, the uncollected stories included, since nothing gathered them into an order of her own. The notebook dates are what break the edition's name for its order. Calling the arrangement no guide at all goes past a single documented inversion, and both an editor and a rival edition are things the passage never brings on.

Question

  • Advanced

A hoard cannot have gone into the ground before its newest coin was struck. Fixing the burial any closer is the trouble, and this is why: a coin could stay in use for decades after it left the die, so a hoard may hold worn coins of a reign beside fresh ones. The newest coin in the hoard from the field below the church was struck in 1461. A historian who has only the coins can say that ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The key's own claim with its floor read as a date. The first premise says the burial cannot come before that year; the second is printed precisely to stop that year being read as the year itself.
What anyone would think, and nothing on this page is about intention, hedge it how you like. The premises are about when coins circulate and when a hoard can have gone down.
A figure the passage does not have. It says a coin could stay in use for decades, which is a reason the interval is unknown rather than a measurement of it.
Correct. The first premise fixes the floor and the second puts no ceiling anywhere near it, so the pair of them reaches a bound and stops there.

Why this answer

The newest coin fixes a year the burial cannot precede, and the decades a coin may stay in use are what stop the same coin fixing the burial itself, so the premises reach a floor and nothing more, with both doing work. A flat 1461 reads the floor as a date, a thirty-year interval is an arithmetic the page never offers, and the burier's intention is not a thing these premises are about.

Question

  • Advanced

A wind-chill figure says how cold bare skin at body heat feels: it is the still-air temperature that would draw heat out of skin at the rate this wind and this temperature do together. Wind carries heat away faster from anything warmer than the air, and that is all it does to a dry object: it cannot bring one below the temperature of the air itself. The pipes under the eaves are dry, and the air will not fall below two degrees above freezing tonight. The bulletin's wind-chill figure is five below, and it warns that the pipes will freeze. Those pipes ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

A motive for the warning, and the passage gives none. It says what the figure means and what the bulletin printed; why the bulletin printed it is a thought a reader supplies.
True, and it is the third sentence read back. What follows from that reading is what the sentence is reaching for, and the second premise, which is what makes anything follow, is left with nothing to do.
Correct. The air holds above freezing all night and wind cannot bring a dry object below the air it blows through, so the wind has a floor under it and the figure of five below is about skin rather than about these pipes.
The kind of thing that is true of bulletins and is not on this page. The passage says what tonight's figures are and what wind can do; it never looks at another night.

Why this answer

Two premises fix a floor together: wind cannot bring a dry object below the air it blows through, and that air holds above freezing all night, so the wind has a floor under it and the figure of five below is a number about skin. The bulletin's reasons, other nights, and the plain reading back of tonight's air temperature are each outside, or short of, what the premises give.

Question

  • Advanced

The laundry's two coppers stand side by side and are worked as a pair. The water book, the coal book, the soap book and the timesheet record four things about each copper and nothing else: the gallons drawn, the coal burned under it, the soap weighed into it, and the hours it ran. Through the whole of 1897 all four stand equal for the two, entry by entry. The linen out of the east copper came up gray that year and the linen out of the west copper did not. Whatever accounts for the difference is therefore ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The key pushed past what four books can settle. That the cause is not in the books is what the premises give; that nothing else could ever find it is a claim about evidence the passage never made, since the books are not said to be all there is.
Correct. The books hold four quantities and nothing else, all four stood equal entry by entry, and the linen did not, so whatever moved is none of them. It could still be the KIND of coal or the KIND of water, which a book counting hundredweights and gallons would not show, and the completion rules out the four quantities rather than every cause.
Nobody on this page is asked anything, and no foreman is named. What somebody might have known is not a thing these premises reach.
Two makes of copper is a distinction the passage never draws. It says the two stand side by side and are worked as a pair, and nothing about where either came from.

Why this answer

Four quantities are named, the books hold those four and nothing else, and all four stood equal while the linen did not, so the difference is in none of them, and the pairing in the first sentence is what makes the four comparable at all. It may still be a difference in KIND, a different coal or a different water, which a book counting hundredweights and gallons would not show; that is why the completion rules out the four quantities rather than the world. Each of the other three goes past what four books settle in its own way: one declares the cause unfindable, one hands it to a foreman nobody asks, and one invents a second make of copper.

Question

  • Advanced

The society paid sick money to a member only while his contributions were clear, and the arrears book is what the steward read to find out whether they were. The arrears book has this member three quarters behind from the spring of 1889 to the spring of 1890. The sick book has him paid for six weeks in the August between. A reader who has only those three facts ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Correct. The first says the society paid nobody in arrears, one book says he was in arrears that August, and the other says he was paid then. At least one of the three has to give, and which one is a question three facts do not answer.
The key's claim at a strength the premises do not reach. That one of three cannot stand with the other two is a long way from any of them being made up: a clerk's slip and a practice quietly broken do the same work.
One member and one August carried across every entry in two years of books. What three facts reach is this man and this month.
The most human reading of the three, and the one a reader arrives at without noticing they have chosen. It is one of the ways out, and the page gives no reason to prefer it to the other two.

Why this answer

A practice and two entries are printed, and the three cannot stand together: the society paid nobody in arrears, and the books put this man in arrears and in receipt of sick money in one month. All three are in use, and the completion says only that they cannot all stand. Calling one a forgery, picking the steward, and condemning two years of bookkeeping all go past what three facts settle, which is that something among them is wrong and not which.

Question

  • Advanced

A gardener began in 1876 to write down the day each spring on which one shrub in the garden opened its first flower, and his successors have kept the book up ever since. Over that stretch the date has come forward by about ten days. The garden stood then among fields at the edge of a village. The village has become a town, the garden now sits inside it, a town holds warmth that open ground lets go, and this shrub opens its first flower earlier in a warmer spring. This suggests that the book ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The key's own claim with its some turned into all of it. The town is a second thing bearing on the date, and a premise that opens a second cause does not close the first; nothing on this page weighs the two.
True, and it comes off the first sentence alone. The ten days and the town are then both idle, and they are what the passage spends its second half on.
A share the passage never divides. It says a town holds warmth; it puts no figure on how much of ten days that is.
Correct. The shift is real, warmth moves this shrub's first flower, and a town that holds warmth has grown up around the garden since 1876, so some of the ten days may be the town's and the book cannot say how many.

Why this answer

The book records a shift in one spot; warmth moves this shrub's first flower, and a town that holds warmth has grown up over the spot since 1876, so some of the shift may be the town's and the record cannot say how much. The town's warmth and the shrub's answer to warmth are both in use. Giving the town all of it goes as far past the premises as giving it none, the plain description of what a record is leaves the whole second half unused, and half of ten days is an arithmetic the passage never offers.

Question

  • Advanced

The inquiry asked each household how many hours a week its members worked for pay, and set the answers side by side. Work taken in at home for a middleman was paid by the piece: the work went out by the bundle and came back the same way, and neither the household nor the middleman counted hours over it. Most of the town's paid work taken in at home was done by its women. The inquiry's finding that the town's women worked far fewer paid hours than its men ____

Which choice most logically completes the text?

The strength held and the reach widened. The inquiry counted every paid hour anybody counted; what it could not reach is work that nobody put hours to, which is a part of that town's work and not the whole of it.
An outcome the passage cannot supply. Nobody counted those hours, so the page holds no figure to put back, and which way the finding would then fall is not on it.
Correct. The question asks for hours, one kind of paid work has no hours attached to it by anybody, and that kind was mostly women's, so the gap the inquiry found can be a gap in the counting.
There are no girls on this page. The inquiry asked about work done for pay, and who else was at the table is a thing the passage never says.

Why this answer

The inquiry counts in hours, piecework at home carried no hours for anybody to report, and that work was mostly the women's, so the finding may be measuring the unit rather than the labor, and all three are in use. Writing the finding off entirely goes past what the premises give, a reversal needs a count nobody made, and the girls are not on the page at all.
