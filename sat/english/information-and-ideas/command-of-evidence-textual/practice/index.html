Question

  • Introductory

Each spring a starling carries fresh sprigs of wild herbs into its nest hole while it is building, and in one population the nestlings raised in nests that got them fledged heavier than the nestlings raised in nests that did not. Researchers hypothesize that the herbs work on the nestlings themselves, through compounds the leaves give off into the nest air, rather than by driving down the mites that live in the lining.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Both accounts predict this. Compounds working on the chicks and a lining with fewer mites in it would each leave a brood heavier, so weight against sprig count cannot say which is happening.
Other birds are not this nest. That a habit is widespread says nothing about which of two mechanisms it works through, and the hypothesis is about what these sprigs do here.
Correct. The rejected account says the herbs work by taking the mites down. Hold the mites down for the whole of the nestling period and the chicks come out no heavier, which leaves the mites doing none of the work the herbs are credited with.
Read the result rather than the design. This isolates the compounds exactly as the hypothesis would want and then reports them doing nothing, which lands on the claim and points the other way.

Why this answer

The rejected account says the herbs work by taking the mites down, so the way to test it is to hold the mites down without any herbs, for as long as the chicks are in the nest, and nothing follows from it. Sprig counts against fledging weight are what both accounts predict, and other hole-nesting birds are not this nest. The stripped sprigs isolate the compounds as squarely as the answer does and report them doing nothing, which is the hypothesis failing rather than holding.

Question

  • Introductory

A regional blood service began sending each booked donor a text message on the morning of the appointment. Over the following year the share of booked donors who arrived rose from 71 percent to 84. The service's annual report concludes that the message is what brought the extra donors in.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the report's conclusion?

A count of messages sent, which the report never disputed. How many go out says nothing about what they did once they arrived.
Correct. Two changes landed together and the second would move attendance on its own. The rise is still real; what the report can no longer say is which change produced it.
This explains how the appointments come to exist rather than what happened to them. A booking rule that has not changed for years cannot account for a change in who turned up.
This is on the conclusion exactly, and it defends it. A group left without the message and left at the old rate is the comparison the report needed, and the stem asked for the reverse.

Why this answer

A rise measured before and after means the message only if nothing else moved, and something else did: the clinics moved and the wait fell by three quarters in the same month. A count of messages and a booking interval leave the rise untouched. The donors who opted out are the comparison the report needed and they support it, which is why the arrow has to be read twice.

Question

  • Introductory

A shipping company's crew lists survive for every voyage it made between 1875 and 1910, and on them about a third of the men signed with a mark rather than a name. A researcher concludes that roughly a third of the company's crews could not write. A rival reading holds that the mark was simply what the ship's clerk asked for when a queue of men was waiting.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researcher's conclusion?

Both readings predict this. A man who could not write and a man hurried through a queue would each use the pen in front of him, so the ink says only where the signing happened.
What else the lists record, and where the men came from, are real questions about these lists and neither is the one the conclusion answers. A crew drawn from far off may be as lettered as a local one.
This is on the conclusion exactly and it answers it the wrong way. The researcher's reading makes a mark a man who could not write, and here are twelve marks from men who could, which is what the rival reading predicts instead.
Correct. The rival reading puts the mark in the clerk's hands, so it should come and go with the clerk and with the hurry. It stays with the man instead.

Why this answer

The rival reading makes the mark a property of the signing, so it should change with the clerk and with the hurry; a mark that follows one man across every voyage does neither, which leaves it a fact about him. The office ink and the men's home ports are things both readings expect. Twelve mark-signers whose letters survive in their own hands pulls the researcher's share down rather than propping it up.

Question

  • Introductory

A hedgerow shrub sets far more fruit in the summers that follow a hard winter than in the summers that follow a mild one. A gardener claims that the shrub itself answers the cold: a spell of frost, she says, is what makes it lay down more flower buds for the spring.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the gardener's claim?

Correct. The frost is still doing the work and the shrub is no longer the thing doing it: on this account what the cold changes is how many buds survive the caterpillars rather than how many the shrub lays down.
How often the cold comes is the setting the claim is made against, and the claim is about what happens when it does. A frequency leaves the mechanism where it was.
A second species is not the claim. The gardener says nothing about what other shrubs do, and a plant that ignores the winter neither adds to nor takes from an account of one that does not.
This is about when the fruit comes and the claim is about why there is more of it. An earlier season is as consistent with a shrub answering the cold as with anything else.

Why this answer

The frost still does the work and the shrub no longer does it: kill the caterpillars that strip the buds and the extra fruit follows without the plant answering anything. It does not establish that bud production is equal either way; it supplies a route to the same fruit that leaves the gardener's mechanism out. How often frost comes, when the fruit ripens and what a neighboring shrub does are all facts about the hedgerow that leave the mechanism where it was. No choice here defends the claim, which is worth noticing before hunting for one.

Question

  • Introductory

In The Fourth Week, an invented novel, a fourteen-year-old is sent to spend a lambing season at her uncle's hill farm. A student claims that the novel presents her respect for her uncle as something she works out by watching him, never as something she is told.

Which quotation from The Fourth Week most effectively illustrates the student's claim?

A quotation about the shed and about her, and about nothing the claim asserts. Getting used to a place is not a judgment of her uncle, worked out or handed over.
Correct. She watches an act, works out what it is for while it is going on, and grants him the thing she has just found she lacked. Nobody says a word.
The vet and the aunt are not the uncle, and driving to the road is not a judgment of anybody. Nothing here is about how the narrator came by her view of him.
Squarely on the claim, and answering it the other way. The respect arrives before she has seen him work and out of somebody else's mouth, which is the one route the claim rules out.

Why this answer

The claim is about where the respect comes from, so the quotation has to show her arriving at it, and counting to forty beside him is her reading an act correctly while it happens and granting him what she has just found she lacked, with nobody speaking. The generator and the vet's visits are in the novel and not in the claim. Her mother's verdict is respect handed over before she has seen him work, which is the one route the claim rules out.

Question

  • Introductory

The glass in a chapel's east window is held in lead of two clearly different widths. A conservator claims that the narrow lead is the window's original glazing and the wide lead belongs to a Victorian repair, rather than the two widths being one glazier's choice of a heavier lead for the parts that carry the most weather.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the conservator's claim?

Both accounts predict this. A repair starts where a window fails first, and a glazier choosing a heavier lead would put it in the same place, so the pattern separates nothing.
One half of a claim printed in two. It dates the narrow lead early, which is the first half, and leaves the wide lead, which is the half the conservator's reading actually turns on.
A different window, on a different date, with its ironwork and its masonry thrown in. What was done at the other end of the chapel dates nothing in this one.
Correct. If the wide lead is a repair, the glass in it should be later than the glass beside it, and it is, without exception in either direction. A choice of heavier lead would follow the weather and would not sort glass by date.

Why this answer

Two dates should sort the glass by date and one glazier's preference should sort it by weather; the glass in the wide lead is later without exception, which is the sorting only the first account predicts. Where the wide lead runs is what both accounts expect, and the west window is a different window. Dating the narrow lead's alloy is the first half of a claim printed in two, and it leaves the wide lead untouched, which is the half the whole reading turns on.

Question

  • Standard

In October a chain of hardware shops replaced the four separate lines at its registers with a single line feeding all four. Comparing the twelve months before the change with the four months after it, the average time a customer spent waiting fell from six minutes to four. The chain's operations report concludes that the single line is what cut the wait.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the report's conclusion?

That is what a single line is, and the report is not disputing it. A description of the arrangement cannot say what the arrangement did.
How the queue felt is a real result and a different question. The conclusion is about minutes, and what customers thought leaves the minutes untouched.
Correct. The whole four-month window after the change sits inside that quiet stretch, while the twelve months it is set against take in the busy season too, and a third fewer customers an hour would shorten a queue with nothing else done.
On the conclusion exactly, and the comparison the report needed. Shops that did not change and did not improve leave the change looking like the cause, which is the opposite of what the stem asked for.

Why this answer

The passage prints the two windows the report compared, and they are not comparable: the four months after the change fall inside the quiet stretch of the year, while the twelve months they are set against take in the busy season, and a third fewer customers an hour would shorten a queue with nothing else done. What a single line is and how it felt to stand in leave the minutes untouched. Two shops that changed nothing and improved nothing is the control the report wanted, so it defends the conclusion the stem asked to weaken.

Question

  • Standard

When a fox comes near her nest, a ground-nesting plover walks away from it dragging one wing along the ground as though it were broken. Researchers hypothesize that the display is aimed at the fox's eye and steered by the nest: the bird begins it only when the fox is looking at her, and she moves off on whichever heading takes the fox farthest from the eggs.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Correct. One trial, both halves: the eye decides whether the display starts and the nest decides which way it goes.
The first half, cleanly. It shows the display tuned to the fox's eye and is real support for that half; what it says nothing about is the heading, and one choice here carries both halves at once.
The second half, cleanly, and real support for it. The fox is led away; whether the bird waits to be watched before she starts is untouched, and the key covers that too.
This bears on the first half exactly and denies it. A display that starts whether or not it is watched is not aimed at an eye, and half a hypothesis denied is the whole of it weakened.

Why this answer

The hypothesis has two halves and the model-fox trials carry both: the display waits for the eye and the heading is taken against the nest. Stopping when the fox looks away supports the first half and being led forty meters off supports the second, so both are real support and both lose to the one finding that reaches the whole claim. A display that begins whether or not it is watched denies the first half, which weakens rather than supports.

Question

  • Standard

In The Green Ticket, an invented novella, a woman comes back to the seaside town where she once spent a summer selling boat tickets from a kiosk on the front. A critic claims that the story never states how long she has been away and leaves the reader to measure it from the things she finds changed.

Which quotation from The Green Ticket most effectively illustrates the critic's claim?

A fact about this visit, which the story has already established is a visit. It measures a day rather than the years, and the claim is about the years.
The brother and the sale are not the woman and not the interval. A letter about what is going to happen cannot be the reader measuring what already has.
Correct. Three things she remembers, each of them different now, and not a figure anywhere. The reader counts the years off the paint, the clock and the missing sailing.
The town as it is, with nothing in it set against a memory. It is the story's world and not its clock.

Why this answer

The claim is that the interval is measured only from changed things, and the kiosk's new color, the plastic clock and the missing eleven o'clock do the measuring with no figure in them. The train, the brother's letter and the smell of the front are the story's world rather than its clock. Nothing here states the interval outright, so the set is decided on what the quotations show.

Question

  • Standard

On the upper floors of an eighteenth-century terrace, a dozen window openings are filled with brick. The usual explanation is the window tax, which charged a house by the number of windows it showed. An architectural historian argues instead that most of these openings never held glass at all, and were built blind from the start so that each front would come out symmetrical.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the historian's argument?

How the tax worked is worth knowing and the passage has already said it. It is a fact about the charge rather than about whether these openings were ever cut through.
Correct. The argument rests on the openings never having held glass, and sockets cut for a frame with sills worn by a sash are the marks of windows that were there and were used.
Both accounts put them there. A tax is dodged where a window is least missed, and a builder gives a small back room a false window for the sake of the front.
On the argument exactly, and holding it up. Brick bonded in one build was never an opening at all, which is what the historian says of most of them and what a later blocking could not look like.

Why this answer

The argument rests on the openings never having held glass, and frame sockets with worn sills behind eleven of them are the marks of windows that were there and were used. How the tax was charged and where the openings sit are what both readings expect. Brick bonded into the wall in one build is the historian's own case made on the same fabric, which is support on an item that asked for the reverse.

Question

  • Standard

A lighthouse keeper's occurrence book survives for one station for the years 1888 to 1891, and it records the fog signal sounded on far more days in summer than in winter in each of those years. From it a researcher concludes that the summer excess holds all along that coast, and not only at the one station whose book happens to have survived.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researcher's conclusion?

That is what the book counts, and the researcher is counting it too. A definition of the entry cannot make one station's four years reach further than they do.
Both readings predict it. A coast that is foggier in summer and a single station that happens to be would each sound the signal now and then all year, so this separates nothing.
A fact about the paperwork rather than about the weather. That the coast's other lights were all under the same instruction says nothing about what any of their books would show.
Correct. The conclusion is about a coast and the book reaches one station on it. Four more stations along its length is the pattern in the places the claim is actually about.

Why this answer

A claim about a whole coast cannot be carried by the one station whose book survives, and four more stations along that coast carry it. What the keeper counted as fog is the book's own definition of its entries. That the signal sounded in every month is what a coast-wide pattern and a purely local one would each produce, and that the other stations were under the same instruction says only what they were obliged to write down, not what they wrote.

Question

  • Standard

A bracket fungus is found in one wood only on the undersides of fallen trunks of a single kind of tree. Mycologists propose that what the fungus needs is a compound in that tree's inner bark, which no other tree is known to produce, and that this is why it is found nowhere else.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the mycologists' proposal?

Correct. The proposal makes the compound a requirement, and here the fungus does without it on three timbers at once. What those three share is the shade, which the proposal never named.
On the proposal exactly, and supporting it. A fungus that cannot grow without the compound is a fungus that needs it, which is the mycologists' case rather than an objection to it.
How the spores arrive is a real question and not this one. A carrier that is not fussy explains how the fungus reaches a trunk, not what it needs once it is there.
A count of fruiting bodies on a trunk the fungus is already established on. Abundance says nothing about what let it start.

Why this answer

The proposal makes the compound a requirement, and the fungus is doing without it on three other timbers in a plantation where that tree has never grown. A beetle carrying spores and two hundred brackets on one trunk are about this fungus and not about what it needs. A culture that will not grow without the compound is the proposal's own requirement confirmed, which is support.

Question

  • Standard

In one county the older speakers have a distinct word for a farm track running between two hedges, and speakers under forty use the standard word instead. A dialectologist argues that the older word is going out because the county's schools taught against it for two generations, rather than because the thing it names has gone from the landscape.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the dialectologist's argument?

How the tracks came to be maintained explains the landscape rather than the word, and an arrangement three centuries old is as compatible with one account as with the other.
Correct. The rival account is that the word went because the thing went. The thing has not gone, and the younger speakers are naming it daily with a different word.
Both accounts predict this. A word schools drove out and a word whose object disappeared would each fall out of print, so the last printing sorts nothing.
True of the county's older vocabulary rather than of this word. Six more of it going the same way, for reasons nobody here has given, leaves the two accounts offered for this one exactly where they were.

Why this answer

The rival account is that the word went because the thing went; the tracks are as common as ever and the younger speakers name them daily with a different word, which leaves the loss to something other than the landscape. Who maintained the tracks, when the word was last printed and what happened to six other old words all belong to the county rather than to the argument. No choice here runs the other way.

Question

  • Advanced

Fifty clay lamps from a single workshop carry a hard black crust inside the nozzle. An archaeologist argues that the crust shows the lamps were burned with rendered animal fat rather than with olive oil, on the ground that a fat flame leaves soot at the nozzle where an oil flame does not.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the archaeologist's argument?

The argument's own premise put to the test and confirmed. Fat sooting and oil not sooting is precisely what the case rests on, and a trial finding it is support.
That they share a maker and share the crust is the passage's own starting point. A fact both sides begin from cannot decide between them.
Other lamps, in another metal, from another workshop. What a bronze lamp does says nothing about what was burned in these fifty.
Correct. The argument runs from crust to fat, and it runs only while oil cannot make a crust. Here oil can, given a thick enough wick, and these channels are cut to take one.

Why this answer

The argument runs from crust to fat and holds only while oil cannot make a crust, and oil can, given a wick thick enough to smoke, which is what a channel half again the usual width will take. That all fifty came from one workshop and all carry the crust is where the passage starts, and bronze lamps elsewhere are other lamps. The replica trial confirms the premise for channels of the standard width, which is exactly what these are not.

Question

  • Advanced

On one sheltered shore the wet sand is combed into low ridges about a hand's width apart, and the pattern is still there after the tide has come in and gone out again. Researchers hypothesize two things at once: that the ridges are cut by the backwash of each wave, and that they survive the next high water because a mat of diatoms binds the sand between the tides.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Correct. One trial, both halves. The backwash is seen doing the cutting with the diatoms killed off, and without them nothing lasts, which is what a hypothesis separating the two predicts.
The binding, and only the binding. Matched for wetness, so the lump is not simply water holding the grains together, it speaks to the second half and leaves the cutting entirely alone.
What the diatoms do with their day. It explains how a mat comes to be at the surface at all and settles neither what cuts the ridges nor what holds them.
On the second half exactly, and denying it. A pattern that lasts where there is no mat to hold it is a pattern the mat was not needed for, and half a two-part hypothesis denied is the hypothesis weakened.

Why this answer

The shaded plot watches the waves still cutting and the pattern no longer lasting, which is a hypothesis that separates the two being right about each in one trial. The diatoms' daily rise is background to both halves, and sand that lumps where equally wet sand does not is the second half by itself. Ridges standing through several tides on a shore with no mat denies that second half.

Question

  • Advanced

What the Line Wants is an invented memoir, by a translator who spent eight years on a poet she never met. A reviewer claims that the book presents translating as a kind of listening rather than a kind of choosing, and that its account of the work turns on what the translator waits to hear.

Which quotation from What the Line Wants most effectively illustrates the reviewer's claim?

The span of the work, which the passage has already given us. A count of years says nothing about what the work is presented as being.
The archive and its rules are not the translating. A description of where the poems are held is not an account of what is done with them.
Correct. She stops working at it and something arrives. The waiting and the hearing are both on the page, which is what the claim says the book presents.
On the claim exactly, and answering it the other way. Weighing two words and picking one is choosing, said in as many words, where the claim is that the book presents the opposite.

Why this answer

The claim is that the book presents the work as listening, and she stops working at the third line and something arrives, which is the waiting and the hearing on the page. The eight years and the reading room's rules are in the book and outside what was claimed. Taking the shorter of two words because the breath ends is choosing, named as such, which is the claim's contrary rather than the claim.

Question

  • Advanced

A furniture works moved its finishing shop into a room lit by north-facing skylights. The share of pieces sent back for a fault in the finish fell from nine percent to three. The works manager's report credits the daylight, and points out that nothing else about the shop changed: the same finishers, the same benches, the same lacquer, the same inspectors grading by eye.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the report's conclusion?

Correct. The report's case is that nothing else changed, and it prints its list. One item on the list did change, in the month of the move, and in the direction that accounts for the fall.
A fact about the room that the report is not relying on. More floor is not more light, and nothing in the conclusion turns on the size of the shop.
How the room is to work in is a real result and a different one. The conclusion is about the reject rate, and how the finishers feel leaves it where it stood.
A different shop, doing different work, that nothing was done to. Its steadiness is neither a check on the finishing shop nor a rival explanation of it.

Why this answer

The report leans on a printed list of what did not change, and the lacquer on that list was reformulated in the month of the move, in the direction that would account for the fall on its own. The room's size, what the finishers thought of it and a shop nobody touched are all about the works and none of them reaches the reject rate. Nothing here defends the report, so the item is settled by reading its list.

Question

  • Advanced

A market town's charter of 1247 grants it a market on Thursdays. The town's own accounts, which begin in 1390, record stall rents taken on Tuesdays and on no other day. A historian argues both that the market had already moved to Tuesday well before those accounts begin, and that it moved because the abbey three miles off had been granted a Thursday market of its own.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the historian's argument?

Both readings expect it. A market that changed its day early and one that changed it late would each keep to the square it was granted, so where the stalls stood sorts nothing.
The cause's own precondition, established and nothing more. An abbey market running the whole period is as consistent with a town that moved in 1385 as with one that moved in 1280.
True of the county's market towns rather than of this one. That days changed elsewhere in the century neither dates this town's move nor names what caused it.
Correct. One document, both halves: it puts the Tuesday market eighty years before the accounts begin, and it has the burgesses complaining at the time that the abbey's Thursday market is taking their trade. That is not the town declaring why it moved, but it puts the argued cause on the record in the right years.

Why this answer

The argument has two parts, a date and a cause, and the court roll reaches both: a Tuesday toll in 1310, and the burgesses complaining in the same document that the abbey's Thursday market has taken their trade. The complaint is evidence for the cause rather than the town stating one, and it is still the strongest of the four because it dates the move and names the pressure at once. Where the stalls stood is what either reading expects. The abbey's grant of 1268 shows the cause was available without saying when the town acted on it, which is one half of an argument made in two.

Question

  • Advanced

A burrowing shrimp on a tidal flat is counted at low water from the openings of its burrows, in quadrats laid along fixed lines, and where two openings lie within a hand's width of each other the survey scores them as one animal. Over ten years the count has halved. The survey team concludes that the population on the flat has halved with it.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the survey team's conclusion?

How large the count once was, which the conclusion does not rest on. A figure for the peak leaves the meaning of the fall exactly as it was.
Correct. The passage prints the rule that scores two openings inside a hand's width as one animal, and coarsening mud has walked the shrimp across that rule. The count can halve with the population unchanged, which is enough to break the team's step from one to the other; it does not show the population held.
On the conclusion exactly, and holding it up. A second method that does not count openings at all, finding the same fall, is what the team would most want.
Another animal, and one the survey passes over. What it does under the same mud neither explains the fall in this count nor bears on whether the fall is real.

Why this answer

The count is the population run through a rule the passage prints, and mud that has coarsened has walked the shrimp's two openings inside the width the rule scores as one, so the count can fall without the population falling. That undoes the step from a halved count to a halved population; it does not establish that the population held. The peak figure and an animal the survey does not count leave the meaning of the fall untouched. Cores that count animals rather than openings finding the same fall is the team's best defense, not an objection to it.

Question

  • Advanced

A Year on the Levels is an invented journal, kept by a village schoolmistress of the marsh behind her house. A scholar claims that the journal records the marsh twice over, once in figures she trusted and once in impressions she did not, and that she marks the difference between them inside the entries themselves.

Which quotation from A Year on the Levels most effectively illustrates the scholar's claim?

The figures, kept carefully, and nothing else. There is no impression in the entry, so there is nothing for a mark of difference to fall between.
A day on the marsh, recorded whole. It is neither a measurement nor an impression she is weighing, and the claim is about how she sorts the two.
Correct. A figure with the word that vouches for it, then an impression with the reason she will not stand behind it, in one entry.
The coast, the brother and a wall. None of it is her marsh, and none of it is either kind of record she is credited with keeping.

Why this answer

The claim is that the journal keeps two registers and marks the seam, and the third of May measures the water, vouches for it, offers an impression and then says why she will not stand behind it. Nine swans and an east wind is one register kept well and is half of that. The peat cutters and the brother's letter are neither register.
