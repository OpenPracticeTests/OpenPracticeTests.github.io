Command of Evidence: Textual

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

The stem names one claim and asks which choice supports it, weakens it, or illustrates it. Test each choice against that claim: whether it bears on the claim, and which way it points.

Key takeaways

  • Support makes the claim likelier; weaken makes it less likely.
  • Test a choice against the claim, not the topic.
  • Take the choice that supports the claim most strongly. Support is not proof.
  • Check direction last: a choice can decide the claim and point the wrong way.
  • An illustrate item wants a quotation in which the claimed thing happens.

What the SAT may ask

  • Support a researcher's hypothesis with the most direct finding.
  • Weaken a team's conclusion with the most direct finding.
  • Illustrate a student's claim with a quotation from a novel.

The rule

A finding supports a claim when the claim is likelier with the finding true. It weakens the claim when the claim is less likely. It does not address the claim when the claim's truth and its falsity would predict the finding equally.

Put the claim in your own words before you read the choices.

Arrow What the choice has to do
Support Make the claim likelier than it was
Weaken Make the claim less likely than it was
Illustrate Show the claimed thing happening in the quoted words

Support is not proof, and a finding does not have to reach the whole claim to count. Where the passage prints a claim in two parts, a finding that carries one part is real support. It loses to one that carries both.

So compare the choices for strength rather than striking every one that stops short. Denying either part, on the other hand, weakens the whole claim.

Why the rule exists

Evidence is a relation, not a property. A fact is evidence for one claim; set beside a different claim it is only a true sentence.

Choose the move

The question asks forFirst move
A choice on the passage's subjectAsk whether it changes what the claim asserts
A support stem, and the claim has two partsPrefer the finding that reaches both to one that reaches half
Two choices both bear on the claimRead the stem's arrow again
The choices are quotationsFind the one where the claimed thing happens

Worked examples

1. Restate the claim and fix its direction

A mountain hare's coat turns white in autumn and brown again in spring. Researchers hypothesize that the switch is timed by day length alone, and not by the arrival of the snow.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Task: support the hypothesis. Name what it claims and which way the stem points.

Evidence: the claim is day length alone, not the snow.

Answer: in a winter when no snow fell at all, the hares turned white on their usual dates. That is a result rather than a plan, and it pulls the two cues apart: one cue was missing and the coats changed anyway.

Closest trap: northern hares turn white earlier than southern ones. True, and about hares and about timing. It does not address the claim, because the north both loses its light faster and gets its snow earlier, so either cue predicts it.

2. Illustrate: find the claim happening

Nine Rivers is an invented novel. An editor claims that it shows its narrator learning her father's trade by copying his hands before she understands what they are doing.

Which quotation from Nine Rivers most effectively illustrates the editor's claim?

"He had bound nets since he was nine and he could tell a bad mesh from across the shed."

"I set my thumb where his thumb went and pulled where he pulled, and it was a year before I knew why the knot had to be turned that way."

Task: illustrate. The quotation has to show the claimed thing happening, not report that it did.

Evidence: the claim has two moving parts. She copies the hands, and the understanding comes later.

Answer: the second. She puts her thumb where his goes and pulls where he pulls, and the year before I knew why is the understanding arriving afterward. Both parts happen in the sentence.

Closest trap: the first quotation is about the father and his skill. It is on the novel's subject and it does not address the claim, which is about how she learned.

3. Two parts, and a reversed finding

Chili peppers are hot to mammals and not to birds. Researchers hypothesize that the heat is a defense with a target. It stops mammals eating the fruit, because their molars destroy the seeds. It leaves birds unbothered, because birds swallow the seeds whole and drop them intact far from the plant.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Mice offered hot and mild fruit from the same plant eat only the mild ones.

Seeds that have passed through a bird sprout at the same rate as seeds taken straight from the fruit.

The compound that makes the fruit hot also slows the mold that rots fruit on the branch.

Task: support a claim printed in two parts, one about mammals and one about birds.

Evidence: the claim is that the heat is a defense with a target. Sort the three findings by whether they bear on that.

Answer: the mice. It reaches one part only, and it wins because it is the one finding a plant whose heat defends nothing would not predict.

Closest trap: the sprouting seeds. Birds passing seeds intact holds whether the heat defends anything or not, so it supports a premise of the account and not the claim. That is on-topic-not-on-claim in the costume of half a claim.

Also wrong: the mold. A compound can do two jobs at once, so slowing mold takes nothing from a defense against mammals; it does not address the claim.

Common traps

Four ways a choice misses the claim

Trap Correction
on-topic-not-on-claim Ask what the claim asserts, then ask whether this changes it
true-but-partial Ask where the finding stops, and whether another choice goes further
wrong direction Name the direction the stem wants before you read any choice
unanchored noun Ask what bearing the new thing has on the claim. A finding may bring in something the passage never named

The method: Test the claim, not the topic

  1. Restate the claim, and fix its direction. Say the sentence the stem names back in your own words, and note whether it asks for support, weakening, or illustration.
  2. Predict what the evidence must show. Say what a finding would have to report to move the claim the way the stem asks. On an illustrate item, say what a quotation would have to do.
  3. Compare the choices against the claim, not the topic, for relevance and then for strength. On topic is not on claim: a finding both accounts predict does not address it. Among the ones that do bear on it, a finding reaching a claim's two parts, or its whole span or population, beats one reaching part.
  4. Check direction last, on whatever is left. Ask which way each surviving choice points. On an illustrate item, ask whether the quotation shows the claim or its opposite. Do not hunt for a reversal that is not there.

Practice

Work each item with the method, untimed, until the method is automatic. Practice 1

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
  • Introductory

Homing pigeons carried hundreds of kilometers from their loft, over country they have never seen, usually find their way back. Researchers hypothesize that a young pigeon learns at its loft which odors ride in on which wind, and that it later uses this remembered map of smells to work out which way it has been carried.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Improving with practice is a fact about homing, and a pigeon steering by the sun or by landmarks would improve too, so it separates nothing.
Correct. The hypothesis puts the learning at the loft, so a bird kept from the wind there should not be able to work out where it was carried. That is what these birds do, and their loftmates do not.
Trap: wrong direction. A pigeon that can smell nothing at the release site has no odors to read against a remembered map, so the hypothesis predicts it gets lost. It gets home.
Trap: unanchored noun. Seabirds and plankton appear nowhere in the text, and the hypothesis is not that smell navigation is possible but that these pigeons use it this way.

The method, applied

The hypothesis puts the learning at the loft, so a bird kept from the wind there has no map to read. Those birds scatter and their loftmates do not, which only this account predicts. The closest rival is the plugged nostrils: squarely on the claim and pointing the wrong way, since a pigeon that can smell nothing gets home.

Practice 2

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
  • Introductory

A male bowerbird builds an avenue of sticks and lays out collected objects at its mouth. In one forest population the males gather almost nothing but blue. Researchers hypothesize that blue is chosen because blue objects are scarce there: a large blue pile is hard to assemble, so it advertises how far and how stubbornly a male has searched.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the researchers' hypothesis?

Trap: wrong direction. Harder searching is the cost that makes a blue pile worth advertising, so this agrees with the hypothesis. The stem asked what weakens it.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. The claim is about why the collected objects are blue, and the sticks are not the objects. Annual rebuilding would hold for a male who collected red.
Trap: unanchored noun. A related species that builds no bower, and where its females nest, are two things the claim never involves.
Correct. Scarcity is the premise the hypothesis rests on. If blue is the easiest color to pick up, a blue pile measures no effort, and the reason offered for choosing blue fails.

The method, applied

The hypothesis rests on blue being scarce, so a blue pile measures effort. Blue as the commonest litter on that forest floor takes the premise away and the reason offered for choosing blue goes with it. The closest rival is the longer searches, which is on the claim and defends it: harder searching is the cost the pile advertises.

Practice 3

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
  • Standard

A mammoth thawing out of a riverbank still carried its last meal: a stomach packed with grasses and sedges, with only a trace of shrub twigs among them. Pollen preserved alongside the plants places the meal in late summer. From the find, researchers conclude that the species was a year-round grazer, and not a browser that turned to shrubs once the grass died back. Skeptics answer that a stomach preserves one meal, eaten on one day, by one animal.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' conclusion?

Correct. The skeptics name three gaps, one meal, one day, one animal, and a month-by-month record from a dozen tusks closes all three. It reads grass in the months the browsing account needs shrubs, which is the only place the two accounts disagree.
Trap: true-but-partial. The cores do run across thousands of years, and they stop at summer, which is where the passage already places the stomach. Winter is the season in dispute.
Trap: wrong direction. This reaches past one meal and lands in winter, which is where the conclusion has to be tested, and it finds twigs. That is the browsing account the researchers rejected.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Grit from cropping low confirms the meal already in front of everyone. Nobody disputes late-summer grazing, and the conclusion is about the rest of the year.

The method, applied

The conclusion is about the whole year and the stomach is one day, which the passage prints as the objection. Tusk layers from a dozen animals read grass in the winter months, which is the only place the grazing and browsing accounts disagree. The closest rival is the second frozen mammoth: it also reaches winter, which is what makes it tempting, and what it finds there is twigs.

Practice 4

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
  • Standard

A fifteenth-century treatise on the assaying of ores survives in one copy. Notes in a different script from the main text run down its margins, correcting figures and adding cross-references. Historians have long read these as a later owner's, made by someone working through a finished book. One historian argues instead that the scribe who copied the manuscript wrote them himself as he worked, which would make the margins a record of the workshop's own knowledge rather than of how one reader used the book.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the historian's argument?

Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. The argument is about who held the pen and when. A later assayer would have annotated the hardest chapters too, so where the notes cluster decides nothing.
Trap: wrong direction. This dates the notes against the copying, which is the question at issue, and settles it the other way: a note cannot cite a book written sixty years later.
Correct. A note the trim cut through was on the page before the trim, and the trim happened at binding, in the workshop. That does not name the hand, but it puts the writing inside the workshop, before the book left it, which is the one thing the later-owner reading cannot allow.
Trap: unanchored noun. Sheep, a breed, and a valley are three things the argument never involves. Where the parchment was made is not when the pen moved.

The method, applied

The argument needs the notes tied to a stage the workshop owned. A note the trim cut through was written before the trim, and the trim happened at binding, so the writing was inside the workshop: not proof of whose hand it was, but the later owner is ruled out. The closest rival is the note citing a treatise sixty years younger, which dates the pen against the copying exactly as the argument asks and settles it the other way.

Practice 5

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
  • Advanced

A small ground orchid of the coastal heath offers no nectar, and nothing on it that a visitor could eat. Male wasps of a single species nonetheless arrive at its flowers all season and leave carrying the pollen the plant needs moved. The flower's lower petal is furred, and about the size and color of a female of that wasp; the bloom also gives off a heavy scent that carries downwind. Researchers hypothesize two things at once: that the males are drawn in from a distance by the scent rather than by the sight of the petal, and that the scent works by copying the odor a female wasp releases to call a mate, rather than by advertising a meal. Neither half stands alone. A flower that merely smelled strong would pull in whatever was hungry; one that merely looked like a wasp would have to be found first.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Trap: true-but-partial. Hiding the petal while letting the scent out supports the first part: sight is not what draws the males in. It says nothing about what the scent imitates, and another choice reaches both.
Trap: wrong direction. This bears on the second part exactly and answers it the other way. If the scent is the smell of a meal, the males are coming to eat.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. The emergence calendar explains why a flower that smells like a female should work at all. The claim is about what recruits the males and what the scent imitates.
Correct. One result, both parts. The males crossed the distance to a scent they could not see while a visible scentless model drew nobody, and on arrival they courted the vial and never fed at it.

The method, applied

The hypothesis has two parts, that scent rather than sight draws the males in and that the scent copies a female rather than a meal, and the vial trial carries both: they crossed the distance to a scent they could not see, and on arrival they courted and never fed. The covered flower is real support for the first part alone, which is why the vial beats it rather than why the covered flower is wrong. The closest rival is the fermenting-fruit chemistry, which is squarely on the second part and answers it meal.

Practice 6

  • Information and Ideas
  • Command of Evidence (Textual)
  • Advanced

Shipworms are clams that bore into submerged wood, and they stop once sediment closes over a hull and cuts off the oxygenated water they need. Archaeologists therefore read a wreck's boring as a clock: the deeper and denser it runs, the longer the timbers lay open on the seabed before the mud arrived. The Marren, raised last year, is bored to about the same depth as the Ilsby, a hull that went down in the same bay and that harbor records show was buried by a dated mudslide ten years afterward. From the match, the team concludes that the Marren also lay exposed for roughly a decade. The two ships sank about ninety years apart.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the team's conclusion?

Trap: wrong direction. A boring rate that held steady across ninety years is this comparison working, which is squarely on the claim. The stem asked what weakens it.
Correct. The conclusion assumes equal boring means equal exposure, which holds only if both hulls bored at the same rate. Timber cut through several times faster reaches the Ilsby's depth in a fraction of ten years.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Larvae arriving within days says the clock starts promptly, which nobody questions and which both hulls share. The dispute is how fast the clock runs afterward.
Trap: unanchored noun. The conclusion never mentions a builder's yard or its mark. It is about how long the Marren lay open, not about when either ship was built.

The method, applied

The conclusion reads equal boring as equal exposure, which holds only if both hulls bored at the same rate. Soft pine cut several times faster than dense oak reaches the Ilsby's depth in a fraction of ten years, so the match no longer measures time. The closest rival is the steady water temperature, which bears on the rate and says the comparison works.
