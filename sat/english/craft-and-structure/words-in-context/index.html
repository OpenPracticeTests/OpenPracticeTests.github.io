Words in Context

  • Craft and Structure
  • Words in Context
All Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

A Words in Context item asks which word this sentence needs. Use the context and the meanings of the choices together. The words around the blank fix the meaning, the tone and the degree.

Key takeaways

  • Read the sentence and its neighbors, then say roughly what the missing word must mean.
  • A signpost such as but, because, or a colon narrows the field. It does not name the answer.
  • Match tone as well as meaning: praise, complaint, or neither.
  • Match degree. A measurement or a so ... that clause says how strong the word must be.
  • A familiar word may carry a less common sense, so check the context before you settle.
  • When two choices fit, keep the one the sentence's own words support. More precise means better supported, not rarer or more technical.

What the SAT may ask

  • Complete a blank with the most logical and precise word or phrase.
  • Say what a word most nearly means as used in the text.
  • Choose between close synonyms separated by precision.
  • Read a common word in its less familiar sense.

The rule

Use the context and the meanings of the choices together. The words around the blank say what the missing word must mean. They also fix its tone (praise, complaint, or neither) and its degree (how strong). Knowing what a word means is still part of the work; the context decides which of its meanings this sentence needs.

None of these signals names the answer. Each one cuts the field, and you still test every choice in the sentence.

Signal Example words What it narrows
Contrast but, although, yet, rather than the word stands against the other side. This fixes direction, not tone: but can join two words that both praise
Cause or result because, since, so (joining two clauses) the word must follow from the clause beside it
Explanation or example a colon, such as, that is what comes after the mark explains or illustrates the blank
Tone praise or complaint elsewhere in the passage whether the word may approve or blame
Degree a number, a date, a measurement, almost, only, so ____ that how strong the word may be. In so ____ that, the clause after that measures the blank rather than following from it
Two denials neither X nor Y, not X and not Y the two things named are out. The answer need not sit between them

In an as used in the text item there is no blank. The sentence holding the word often does not define it. Work from the sentences that say what the word does, then match a choice to that.

A Transitions item also has a blank, but its choices are connecting words such as however. A blank that takes a content word is Words in Context.

Why the rule exists

Most common words carry more than one sense, and the words around them decide which one applies. So a word you know well can be the wrong answer here, and a word you half know can be the right one.

Worked examples

1. Say roughly what the blank needs, then test the choices

Years of sun and rain had gone to work on the paper timetable taped inside the bus shelter, so its departure times were now almost ____. (illegible / inaccurate / indelible / unofficial)

Task: complete the blank.

Evidence: the clue is years of sun and rain acting on paper. Something like worn away or hard to read is enough to work with.

Answer: illegible, read back into the sentence, says the times can no longer be made out.

Closest trap: inaccurate belongs to timetables, but the weather damaged the paper; it did not change the times.

2. A familiar word in its less common sense

A note stitched into the collar promised that the jacket's dye was fast, so after one wash I stopped sorting it away from the whites.

Task: say what fast means here.

Evidence: the common sense, quick, breaks the sentence: a quick dye is no reason to stop sorting laundry. The so clause says what follows from the promise, and whatever fast means, it is what makes a colored jacket safe beside white ones.

Answer: fixed, not going to run in the wash.

Closest trap: second sense. When the familiar meaning breaks the sentence, look for another sense of the word rather than an error in the text.

Common traps

Common wrong choices and the check for each

Trap Correction
wrong direction Decide whether the word stands with or against the clause beside it. Where no signpost says, a measurement or an example does
wrong-charge word Right meaning, wrong tone. Check whether the passage praises, complains, or does neither
second sense If the familiar meaning breaks the sentence, look for another sense of the word
near-synonym imprecision Two words look close. Keep the one the sentence's own words support
accurate-but-irrelevant True of the subject, and not what the sentence around the blank asks for

The method: Predict roughly, then test every choice

  1. Read the sentence and the sentences around it, then say roughly what the missing word must mean. A rough phrase is enough. In an as used in the text item, the sentence holding the word may not define it. Work from the ones that say what it does.
  2. Fix the tone and the degree. A signpost often narrows them, and where there is none a number, a date, or a measurement can. These clues cut the field; they do not name the answer.
  3. Test every choice in the sentence, against meaning, tone and degree. Two traps: the wrong-charge word (the right meaning in the wrong tone) and the second sense (a familiar word in its less common sense).
  4. Reread with your choice in place. If two still fit, keep the one the sentence's own words support. More precise means better supported, not rarer or more technical.

Practice

Work each item with the method, untimed, until the method is automatic. The Reading and Writing section averages about 71 seconds a question, but that is an average across the section and not a deadline for any one item; save the clock for mixed practice later. Practice 1

  • Craft and Structure
  • Words in Context
  • Introductory

A foley artist records the footsteps an audience hears in a finished film. The tools are strikingly ____: a box of gravel for a driveway, a folded towel for snow. Nothing on the studio floor would look out of place in a hallway closet, because the ear is listening for a surface, not for an object.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct. The colon says the examples define the blank, and gravel and a folded towel are household objects. The next sentence confirms it: nothing on the floor would look out of place in a closet.
Trap: wrong direction. A studio suggests purpose-built gear, but the colon's examples are household objects, so this points the opposite way from the evidence.
Trap: wrong-charge word. It has the right idea, unremarkable, with contempt added, and the passage admires the work rather than pitying its materials.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. Gravel and a towel are cheap, but the sentence is about where you would find them, not what they cost.

The method, applied

Step 1: the colon's examples, a box of gravel and a folded towel, point to everyday or plain. Step 2: the tone stays neutral, since the passage admires the work rather than pitying the materials. Step 3: ordinary fits, and the nearest rival, specialized, runs against the household examples. Step 4: reread. Strikingly ordinary fits what follows the colon.

Practice 2

  • Craft and Structure
  • Words in Context
  • Introductory

A dark, glossy varnish coats the sandstone boulders of many deserts, built up from windblown clay and traces of manganese. The coating thickens by roughly a hundredth of a millimeter each century, and the change is so ____ that two photographs of one rock face taken a lifetime apart are indistinguishable.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Trap: wrong-charge word. Tedious reports how slowness feels to someone waiting through it, and the text measures a rate. Boredom would also not explain why two photographs match.
Trap: wrong direction. Fleeting says a change is over too quickly to catch, and this change is invisible because it is too slow.
Correct. The measurement sets the direction, and so ____ that promises the word will explain the identical photographs. A change spread that thinly across time is gradual.
Trap: near-synonym imprecision. Steady names consistency, not slowness; a steady rate could be fast, and only slowness makes two photographs match.

The method, applied

Step 1: the sentence needs a word for a change that leaves two photographs identical. Step 2: there is no signpost. The measurement, a hundredth of a millimeter each century, sets the degree, and the tone stays neutral. Step 3: gradual fits, and the nearest rival, steady, names consistency rather than slowness; a steady rate could be fast. Step 4: reread with gradual in place.

Practice 3

  • Craft and Structure
  • Words in Context
  • Standard

In her first year of recording interviews, Imani filled every pause. A narrator would stop mid-memory, the silence would stretch past a breath, and she would rush in with the next question. She listens to those tapes now and hears what she was interrupting. Her silences these days are ____: she counts to ten, watches the narrator's hands, and waits. More often than not, what arrives after the pause is the thing the narrator came to say.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Trap: wrong-charge word. Silence can look shy from outside, but the colon lists chosen acts, and the passage puts the nervous behavior in the first year.
Correct. The colon defines the word with a list of chosen acts: counting, watching, waiting. The paragraph contrasts them with the first year, when she filled every pause without thinking.
Trap: near-synonym imprecision. Rehearsed means following a script set beforehand, and she reads the room in real time, watching the narrator's hands.
Trap: wrong direction. A reflex happens without deciding, and the paragraph runs the other way, from rushing in unthinking to counting to ten by choice.

The method, applied

Step 1: her silences now are counting, watching and waiting, so the sentence needs a word for something done on purpose. Step 2: the colon's list explains the blank, and the contrast between her first year and these days runs from unthinking to chosen. Step 3: deliberate fits, and the nearest rival, rehearsed, names a script set beforehand where she is judging in the moment. Step 4: reread. Deliberate: she counts to ten holds.

Practice 4

  • Craft and Structure
  • Words in Context
  • Standard

When a steep face of a large dune slips, the sand can boom loudly enough to be heard a mile away. There is no engine in a dune, no water beneath it, and no drum for it to resonate in: the sound is made by nothing more than dry grains sliding past one another. The cause is slight and the noise is enormous, and it is that mismatch, rather than the volume alone, that makes the boom seem ____.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Trap: near-synonym imprecision. Excess is measured against what is needed or allowed, and this sentence measures the noise against what produced it.
Trap: wrong-charge word. The middle sentence refuses anything uncanny, no engine, no water, no drum, only dry grains sliding, so a word meaning against nature contradicts it.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. The boom is audible a mile off, but the sentence sets loudness aside and asks about the gap between cause and noise.
Correct. The sentence names its own criterion, a slight cause, an enormous noise, and the mismatch between them. Disproportionate is the word for a thing out of scale with what produced it.

The method, applied

Step 1: a slight cause, an enormous noise, and that mismatch point to out of scale. Step 2: the signpost is rather than the volume alone, which rules loudness out as the answer. Step 3: disproportionate names the relation between cause and effect, and the nearest rival, deafening, is the loudness the sentence has just set aside. Step 4: reread. Seem disproportionate fits that mismatch.

Practice 5

  • Craft and Structure

  • Advanced

A lexicographer does not decide what a word means; she decides when a meaning has become a fact about the language. New senses arrive constantly, most of them from a single writer or a single week, and they leave as quickly. So the test is not whether a sense is clever, or whether the editors like it. Citations are gathered from sources that have nothing to do with one another, a court filing, a recipe blog, a plumbing catalog, and are dated. If the sense turns up across all of them for years, in the writing of people who plainly never read one another, it has the currency an entry requires, and it goes in. If it stays inside one trade or one circle of friends, it waits, however old it is.

As used in the text, what does the word "currency" most nearly mean?

Trap: second sense. This is the everyday meaning, and trade and catalog nearby keep it close, but a sense of a word cannot be legal tender.
Correct. The if clause is the definition. Turning up across unrelated sources, for years, among writers who never read one another is what gives a sense currency.
Trap: wrong direction. Current suggests recent, but recency is what the passage sets aside, since a sense waits however old it is.
Trap: near-synonym imprecision. Popularity can mean wide use, but it carries no span of time, and the passage requires years. General approval is ruled out outright: the test is not whether the editors like a sense.

The method, applied

Step 1: work from the sentences that say what currency takes. The if clause names what a sense needs to go in: use across unrelated sources, for years, by writers who never read one another. Step 2: the passage works from both sides, since the last sentence gives the failing case, a sense that stays inside one trade and waits however old it is. Step 3: widespread and lasting use fits both sides. The nearest rival, popularity or general approval, can reach the width but not the years, and approval is what the passage sets aside. Step 4: reread. The widespread and lasting use an entry requires keeps the meaning.

Practice 6

  • Craft and Structure
  • Words in Context
  • Advanced

A perfume is built to fall apart in a fixed order. The first thing you smell, minutes after the spray dries, is the accord a perfumer calls the top: citrus oils, a green herb, something sharp. Those materials are the most volatile in the bottle, so they lift off the skin and are gone within the hour, and they are chosen partly because they are. Underneath them sit the heart notes, which hold for most of an afternoon, and beneath those the base, resins and woods heavy enough to stay on a scarf for days. A perfumer who wants a scent to open brightly and end warm is therefore not choosing smells so much as scheduling them: each material's place in the sequence is set by how long it is willing to stay.

As used in the text, what does the word "volatile" most nearly mean?

Trap: second sense. This is the everyday sense of volatile, but the so makes the word explain why the materials lift off the skin within the hour, and being explosive would not.
Trap: second sense. This is volatile said of a temper. Here the word describes an oil, and the so asks it to explain the oil's departure.
Correct. The so clause says what being the most volatile causes: these materials lift off the skin within the hour. The last sentence ranks materials by how long they stay, which fixes the sense as speed of leaving.
Trap: near-synonym imprecision. The top notes do not decompose; they leave, lifting off the skin while the base stays, so the axis is departure, not decay.

The method, applied

Step 1: work from what the passage says volatile does. The most volatile materials lift off the skin and are gone within the hour, so the sense needed is quick to leave. Step 2: the signpost is so. The first clause causes the second, so volatile must name the property that makes a material vanish within the hour. Step 3: quick to evaporate fits, and the closing sentence confirms it by ranking materials on how long each stays. The nearest rival, unstable and quick to break down, swaps leaving for decay, and the top notes leave intact. Step 4: reread. The most volatile, so they lift off the skin keeps the cause and effect.
