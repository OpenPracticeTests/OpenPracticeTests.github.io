Question

  • Introductory

A tea taster never judges two cups back to back. Between them she rinses her mouth and pauses, so the tongue is ____ before the next cup arrives and each tea is met on its own rather than blurred by the one just tasted.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Reverses the direction: a jaded tongue is dulled, the opposite of the fresh start the pause creates.
Correct. The rinse and pause return the tongue to a neutral state, which is exactly a clean start.
Right idea of tending the tongue, wrong charge: it frames care as indulgence, not a reset.
True of a taster's tongue, but not what "before the next cup" is asking about.

Why this answer

The "so ... rather than blurred by the one just tasted" tells you the rinse returns the tongue to a fresh, neutral state before the next cup. "Cleared" names that clean start; the sentence wants the effect of the pause, not a lasting trait of the taster or a worn-down tongue.

Question

  • Introductory

Every evening at dusk the lamplighter set out on the same ____, turning up each gas lamp in the same order he had for thirty years, so that neighbors could tell the hour by the light reaching their street.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct. A circuit is a fixed route walked the same way each time, which the thirty years of sameness demands.
Reverses the direction the "same ... same order" sets up: a detour departs from the usual route.
A trek is a long, hard journey, and the sentence supplies no hardship, only a route walked the same way each evening.
An errand can be a daily one, so repetition does not rule it out. It names the task a trip is made for, and the sentence needs a word for the route itself, the one walked in the same order for thirty years.

Why this answer

The signpost is "the same ... in the same order he had for thirty years": the sentence needs a word for the route itself, fixed and walked in a set order. "Circuit" names exactly that regular round, and the neighbors telling time by it depends on its sameness. "Errand" names the task rather than the route, "trek" adds a hardship the sentence never supplies, and "detour" departs from the route the sentence keeps.

Question

  • Introductory

A cider maker gathers the windfalls, the apples the wind has already knocked to the ground, instead of paying to have sound fruit picked. Bruised and free, they would otherwise rot in the grass, so putting them to the press ____ what an orchard usually throws away.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Reverses the direction: the windfalls are being used, not thrown out.
Wrong charge: to plunder is to take by force, and the windfalls are free, unwanted, and lying in the grass.
Correct. To salvage is to rescue for use what would otherwise be lost, which is what the press does with the windfalls.
Near miss: to restore is to return something to a former condition, and the apples stay bruised; they are used as they are.

Why this answer

The sentence sets up fruit that "would otherwise rot in the grass" and that the orchard "usually throws away," so the blank needs a word for rescuing what would be wasted. "Salvages" is that word: the cider maker is putting to use the very apples that were headed for the ground.

Question

  • Introductory

A paper marbler thickens a tray of water into a jelly-like bath called the size, then drops in his colors. Because the bath is dense, the inks do not sink but stay ____, riding the surface in swirls the marbler can comb before he lays down a sheet of paper to lift them.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Reverses the "do not sink but" contrast: submerged is beneath the surface, not on it.
The closest rival, and "do not sink" alone would allow it. What rules it out is the rest of the sentence: suspended color hangs through the bath, and these inks are "riding the surface".
Wrong charge: "stranded" makes floating sound like being stuck, where the marbler wants the color there.
Correct. Afloat names color riding the surface of the dense bath, which "do not sink but ... riding the surface" describes.

Why this answer

The clue is "do not sink but stay ____, riding the surface": the "but" rules out sinking, and the word must name resting on top of the dense bath. "Afloat" says exactly that the inks ride the surface rather than dropping through it. "Suspended" is the nearest rival and clears the "do not sink" half; it fails on the other half, since suspended color hangs through the liquid instead of riding on it.

Question

  • Introductory

The night baker mixes her dough at dusk and pulls the finished loaves from the oven at dawn. She sleeps while the shop sells them, and wakes as the last customer leaves. Her working day is no longer than anyone else's, only ____: every hour of it falls where her neighbors' hours of sleep do.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Reverses the sentence, whose whole point is that her hours line up with nobody else's.
Correct. Inverted means turned the other way round, which is what a day spent entirely in the neighbors' night hours is.
The sentence rules this out in as many words: her day is "no longer than anyone else's".
Adds a complaint the passage never makes: she sleeps while the shop sells and wakes as the last customer leaves.

Why this answer

The colon tells you what the blank means: "every hour of it falls where her neighbors' hours of sleep do." That is a day turned the other way round, which is what "inverted" names. The sentence has already said the day is "no longer than anyone else's," so the blank is about which hours she works, not how many.

Question

  • Introductory

You cannot throw a mooring rope; it is too heavy to sail across the gap to the dock. So the dockhand first tosses a thin, weighted cord, the ____ line; once it is caught, the heavy rope is tied to its end and dragged over behind it.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct. The cord goes first and the heavy rope follows on its end, so it is the leading line.
Reverses the direction: this line goes first, it does not trail behind.
True of the heavy mooring rope, not the thin cord, and beside the point the sentence is making.
Wrong charge: nothing says the line is snarled, only that it is light and goes first.

Why this answer

The sentence lays the job out plainly: the thin cord is tossed "first," and once caught the heavy rope is "dragged over behind it." "Leading" names the line that goes ahead and pulls the other along, which is exactly that role.

Question

  • Standard

A watercolorist who lays down a wrong line can lift most of it again with a damp brush. The enameller cannot. Once the kiln has fused the powdered glass to the copper, a misplaced line is ____: the only remedy is to grind the whole panel back to bare metal and begin the design again from nothing.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Nothing here says the line stops being seen; the trouble is that it cannot be taken back.
Reverses the weight of the sentence, which is that one wrong line costs the enameller the whole panel.
Correct. Irreversible names what cannot be undone, and grinding the panel back to bare metal is starting over, not undoing.
True of the remedy, and not what the blank asks: the sentence is naming what the line has become, not what fixing it costs.

Why this answer

The sentence is built on a contrast: the watercolorist "can lift most of it again," the enameller "cannot." So the blank needs a word for a mark that cannot be taken back. "Irreversible" is that word, and the remedy the sentence offers confirms it, since grinding the panel back to bare metal and starting again is not reversing the line but abandoning everything around it.

Question

  • Standard

The newspapers in the oldest boxes, printed on cheap wartime stock that browned and stiffened as its own acids ate at it, have grown so ____ that the archivist turns their pages with a breath held. A corner snapped off in a careless grip does not tear; it powders, and no repair puts the fibers back.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

The result clause is about breaking, and nothing here says the paper or the print has lost color; it browned and stiffened.
Correct. Brittle names stiff material that snaps at a touch, which "it powders" and "no repair puts the fibers back" describe.
Fraying is fibers pulling loose at an edge, and the sentence rules it out: the corner "does not tear; it powders."
Reverses the direction: supple is flexible, the opposite of paper that powders when gripped.

Why this answer

The result clause fixes the meaning: "so ____ that" the paper "powders" at a touch and "no repair puts the fibers back," so the blank must name how easily it breaks. "Brittle" is that word, matching stock that "stiffened" and now snaps rather than bends; the browning is real but is not what "powders" is describing.

Question

  • Standard

Gold leaf is hammered so thin that a sheet weighs almost nothing, and the sign painter works behind closed doors for that reason: a draft from an opened window, or even a sharp breath, is enough to ____ the leaf from the brush and send it tumbling before it ever reaches the lettering.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Names breaking, but nothing here breaks: the leaf is carried off the brush intact.
A real step in gilding (polishing the leaf once it is laid), but not what a draft does to it.
Reverses the direction: securing the leaf is what the closed doors accomplish, not what the draft does.
Correct. To dislodge is to knock something out of the place it was resting, which is what a breath does to leaf on a brush.

Why this answer

The sentence names a cause (a draft, or "even a sharp breath") and its result (the leaf goes "tumbling before it ever reaches the lettering"), so the blank needs a verb for moving the leaf out of position. "Dislodge" is that verb. Nothing in the sentence breaks, nothing is polished, and nothing is held in place.

Question

  • Standard

What ages a wine well is not cold so much as sameness. A cellar that warms in July and chills in January forces the wine to expand and contract until it tires, so the vintner prizes a room whose temperature stays ____ through every season, indifferent to the weather swinging overhead. The number on the thermometer matters less than its refusal to move.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct. Constant names a temperature that holds through every season, which "refusal to move" restates.
Reverses the direction: erratic is the swinging the cellar is built to prevent.
Near miss: temperate means mild in degree, not steady over time; a mild cellar could still swing.
The passage sets cold aside ("not cold so much as sameness"), so coldness is what the sentence is not asking for.

Why this answer

The sentence contrasts a cellar that "warms in July and chills in January" with the room the vintner wants, one "indifferent to the weather swinging overhead," and restates it as a "refusal to move." "Constant" names that steady, unchanging temperature; the passage has already ruled out mere coldness, saying the aging depends on sameness, not cold.

Question

  • Standard

Before the scaffolding comes down, every carved panel in the hall's ceiling is photographed for the building's record. The camera is set at the same height and the same lamp angle for all four hundred of them, each frame is numbered against a plan of the ceiling, and a panel half hidden by a beam is shot a second time from the other side rather than left out. The carving is the showy part; the record made of it is ____.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Belongs to the carving. The shots themselves are four hundred repeats of one plain setup.
Ruled out by the fixed height, the fixed lamp angle, and the numbered plan: nothing is left to the eye.
Correct. One setup held across four hundred frames, each numbered against a plan, with the awkward panel shot again rather than skipped, is exactness in every small particular.
Reverses the point: the panel that is hard to reach is shot twice rather than dropped.

Why this answer

The last sentence sets the carving against the record and then asks about the record, which is four hundred plain frames taken to one setup, numbered against a plan, with the awkward panel shot again rather than skipped. Meticulous names that exactness in every small particular. Elaborate belongs to the carving, since the shots are deliberately identical and plain. Selective reverses the point, because the hard panel is added rather than dropped. Instinctive is ruled out by the fixed height, the fixed lamp angle, and the numbered plan.

Question

  • Standard

A fen warden harvests sedge for thatch, but never cuts the whole fen in one year. The reserve is divided into blocks, and each season one block is cleared while the others are left standing, so the sedge warblers always have somewhere to nest and the cut ground has years to grow back before its turn comes again. The harvest is ____ for that reason.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Reverses the sense: cutting the fen thoroughly is the one thing the warden takes care never to do.
Correct. A staggered harvest spreads the blocks out across the years so they never come bare together, which is what leaves the warblers a nesting place.
Names how much is cut, and the reason clause is about when: the blocks take turns, so one is always standing.
True that a block is cut each season, but that names the yearly timing, not the spreading out that the nests depend on.

Why this answer

The reason clause does the defining: the blocks are cut at different times so that the fen is never bare all at once and each patch gets years to recover. "Staggered" names exactly that spreading out across time. "Seasonal" catches only the yearly timing. "Sparing" names how much is cut, where the reason clause is about when each block is taken; every block is cut in the end, and what the warblers depend on is the order. "Thorough" points the opposite way.

Question

  • Standard

For thirty years Odell kept the lighthouse log the same way: wind, visibility, the hour the lamp was lit. Nothing else went into it. On the November page his granddaughter found fog, then clear by four, and the lamp lit on time, and under that a week of blank ruled lines before the entries began again. She knew what that week was. From a man who wrote down every shift in the wind, the empty lines were more ____ than a paragraph would have been.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Thirty years of complete entries rule this out; the blank week is not an oversight in a log kept that closely.
The passage says the opposite in as many words: "She knew what that week was."
Blank lines are more silent than a paragraph whatever they mean, so the comparison would say nothing.
Correct. Eloquent can describe something wordless that conveys a great deal, which is what a week of empty lines does in a log kept every day for thirty years.

Why this answer

The sentence weighs the empty lines against a paragraph he might have written, so the blank must say how much they conveyed. A week of nothing, in a log that records every shift in the wind, says a great deal without a word, which is eloquent in its less common sense. Cryptic is ruled out by "She knew what that week was," and careless by thirty years of complete entries.

Question

  • Advanced

Across the table the envoy met the offer with a shrug and a slow look toward the window, as if the terms hardly touched her. But the indifference was ____: she had settled on it before she entered the room, and every idle gesture was placed to make the other side doubt how badly she wanted the deal she had in fact come determined to close.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Correct. Studied means deliberate AND put on for effect, a shade "deliberate" alone does not carry, and indifference staged gesture by gesture is exactly that.
Reverses it; the passage says she came determined to close, so the calm is a front, not real feeling.
Names a calm that comes and goes, but hers is held on purpose through the whole meeting.
Wrong charge: sullen is sulking resentment, not the cool, deliberate front the envoy puts on.

Why this answer

The signpost is that she "settled on" the indifference beforehand and "placed" each gesture for effect, over a want she actually holds, so the word must name a pose adopted on purpose. Studied, in its less common sense, means exactly that: deliberately assumed. Genuine reverses it, fleeting misreads a sustained tactic as a passing mood, and sullen supplies a resentment the scene does not show.

Question

  • Advanced

When the museum sold one lesser canvas to endow the care of the rest, the letters came fast: the curator had betrayed a public trust, had treated the collection as a bank account. She did not fire back, and she did not repent. Instead she published the figures. The canvas had hung in storage, unseen, for forty years; its sale would conserve a hundred works the public actually came to see, and every dollar was ring-fenced for that purpose. Set beside both the outrage and any easy apology, her response was strikingly ____.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Overstates her: she answers with figures, not with defiance.
Reverses it; she publishes an accounting rather than conceding a wrong.
Correct. A response set deliberately between outrage and apology, argued with ring-fenced figures, is a calm and moderate one.
Close, but she is not indifferent; she makes a committed case for stewardship, only a cool-headed one.

Why this answer

The signpost is the double negation, 'she did not fire back, and she did not repent', which fences off both extremes and leaves the middle. Measured names that calm, moderate stance. Defiant and apologetic are the two extremes the sentence rules out, and detached mistakes her composure for indifference.

Question

  • Advanced

The gauge report calls this creek flashy. After a summer storm it can rise two feet in under an hour, jump its low banks, and then drop back to a trickle by dusk, all in a single afternoon. A spring-fed stream a valley over takes the same rain and barely stirs, releasing it over days. The two carry similar volumes in a year, and the gauges separate them on one axis only: how long the water takes to arrive, and how long it stays.

As used in the text, what does the word "flashy" most nearly mean?

The everyday sense of the word, and nothing here is about how the creek looks. What the passage reports is what the gauges read and how long the water took.
Correct. Two feet in an hour and back to a trickle by dusk is the fast response the closing sentence names as the one axis separating the two streams.
Reverses it; that describes the spring-fed stream the passage contrasts, not the flashy one.
Close, but it names how much water comes, while flashy names how fast the stream rises and falls.

Why this answer

Nothing here defines the word outright, so work from what the passage measures: a rise of two feet in an hour, a fall to a trickle by dusk, and a spring-fed stream that takes the same rain over days. The closing sentence names the axis, how long the water takes to arrive and how long it stays, which fixes flashy as quick to rise and fall. The showy sense is the everyday one, and the passage reports gauge readings rather than looks. The steady stream is the contrast case, and severe flooding names magnitude where the passage separates the two streams on speed and says their yearly volumes are alike.

Question

  • Advanced

A typesetter calls the white space set between one line of type and the next the leading, and this page has been given generous leading. The lines sit far enough apart that the eye never catches the line below while reading the one above, and a reader can rest at the end of each line before the next begins. On a cramped page the lines crowd together and the eye trips. The specification sheet puts this page at fourteen points of leading over a ten-point face.

As used in the text, what does the word "generous" most nearly mean?

The word's usual sense. Leading is a quantity of space set out in points, so the word here is measuring an amount.
Reverses it; that describes the cramped page the passage sets against this one.
The spacing is doing a job, keeping the eye off the line below and giving it a rest, so it is not more than is needed.
Correct. Fourteen points of leading over a ten-point face is a plentiful quantity of space, and the lines sit far enough apart to do the work the passage describes.

Why this answer

The passage says what the leading does, keeping the eye off the line below and letting a reader rest, and it closes with a measurement: fourteen points over a ten-point face. That fixes generous as ample in extent. Kind and giving would make a measured quantity a disposition, the tight sense is the cramped page set against this one, and extravagant overshoots spacing the passage credits with doing a job.

Question

  • Advanced

A conservator holds a nineteenth-century watercolor up to the window and frowns at the meadow: that particular green is fugitive. Hang the picture in daylight, she explains, and within a generation the green will pale toward a wan yellow, the grass draining out of the field while the ink lines around it stay black. The pigment is chemically sound and firmly bound to the paper; nothing will crack or lift. It simply cannot hold its color under light. That is why the sheet is stored in a dark box and shown only a few weeks a year.

As used in the text, what does the word "fugitive" most nearly mean?

Correct. A green that pales to yellow within a generation of daylight is a color that will not hold, which is what the passage spends its length describing.
A real sense of the word, and the right one in other contexts, but not this one: nothing here is escaping, and the pigment is 'firmly bound to the paper'.
Reverses it; the whole warning is that the green will not last.
Close, but the passage rules this out: nothing will crack or lift. The loss is of color, not of paint.

Why this answer

The passage defines the word by its consequence: in daylight the green 'will pale toward a wan yellow', and it 'cannot hold its color under light'. That fixes fugitive as quick to fade. The runaway sense is the familiar one the passage sets aside, colorfast reverses the warning, and flaking is a physical loss the passage explicitly denies.

Question

  • Advanced

The editor circled the manuscript's first sentence and wrote one word in the margin: arresting. A reader who picked up the book in a shop and read that sentence would keep reading it standing up, and would carry it to the till. The rest of the page could be ordinary and the book would still sell on the strength of those twelve words, because they take hold of the eye and refuse to let it wander. She had been reading submissions for nineteen years and had written that word in a margin four times.

As used in the text, what does the word "arresting" most nearly mean?

The root's literal sense. What the passage says is held is the eye, not the reader's progress: the words "take hold of the eye and refuse to let it wander", and the reader reads on and buys the book.
Reverses it; the line is praised for holding a reader who cannot put the book down.
Correct. Twelve words that take hold of the eye and refuse to let it wander are words that seize attention.
Wrong charge: writing the word four times in nineteen years is high praise, and nothing here says the sentence unsettles anyone.

Why this answer

The passage says what the sentence does to a reader: it keeps them reading standing up, and the twelve words 'take hold of the eye and refuse to let it wander'. That fixes arresting as seizing attention. Halting is the literal root, and the reader is carried on rather than stopped; flat and forgettable reverses the praise; and alarming adds a negative charge the editor's four-times-in-nineteen-years note does not carry.

Question

  • Advanced

Mathematicians reserve the word deep for a particular kind of theorem. The ones that earn it reach down to something two fields had both been standing on without knowing it, and they keep turning up in questions nobody was asking when they were proved. Difficulty is a separate matter: some results carry the label on proofs of half a page, and some proofs that run four hundred pages earn it from nobody and are merely long. An editor can tell you which papers were hard to referee, and that is not the same list.

As used in the text, what does the word "deep" most nearly mean?

The literal, physical sense; the passage describes the reach of an idea, not a distance downward.
Correct. A result that reaches down to what two fields had both been standing on is resting on ideas beneath them, and one that keeps turning up in unasked questions is far-reaching. Both halves are what the passage says these theorems share.
Close, and the passage sets difficulty aside as a separate matter, with counts on both sides: half-page proofs that earn the word, four hundred pages that do not.
Reverses it; a deep result ties separate fields together rather than sealing itself off.

Why this answer

No sentence defines the word, so work from what the passage says these theorems share: they reach down to something two fields had both been standing on, and they keep turning up in questions nobody was asking. The first is the foundation; the second is the reach. Together they fix deep as far-reaching and resting on what lies underneath. The physical sense is the literal one, and the passage measures no distance. Difficulty the passage sets aside as a separate matter. And narrow reverses the connective reach it describes.
