Text Structure and Purpose

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

These items ask what a text is doing, not what it says. Decide which act the stem names: the job one sentence does, the shape of the whole text, or its single purpose.

Key takeaways

  • A choice names an act and an object, and both have to fit. A structure choice does that once per move.
  • The stem names the ask: a sentence, the structure, or the main purpose.
  • A structure answer names the main moves in order. A purpose answer may combine two acts.
  • Reject an act stronger or weaker than the text: describing is not criticizing.
  • A concession acknowledges a drawback. It does not mean the text gives up its claim.

What the SAT may ask

  • Name the function of one sentence, given by its position.
  • Name the overall structure of the text.
  • Name the main purpose of the whole text.

The rule

A function is an act. In a choice, the verb names the act and everything after it is the object. Both have to fit.

A true object cannot rescue an act the text never performs, and the right act under an object the text does not contain is wrong as well. A structure choice lists several moves, so run the same check on each one.

The stem names which of the three asks you are in.

Stem asks for The answer names, and must cover
The function of one sentence the job that sentence does for the rest, and the whole of that sentence
The overall structure the text's main moves in order, though not every minor detail
The main purpose what the whole text is for, not its most vivid sentence. It may combine two acts: describing a system in order to explain its design
  • concedes acknowledges a point, a drawback, or an objection. It does not mean the text gives up its claim, and a text often concedes and then holds its ground.
  • qualifies keeps a claim and limits how much of the ground it covers.
  • illustrates gives a case of a claim. It supports the claim and does not change how much ground the claim covers.

More than one of these can describe a sentence truly. Take the one that best fits both the act and its object.

Why the rule exists

Prose does things: it introduces, complicates, and grants a small point on the way to a bigger one. You follow those moves without naming them, so a choice with the wrong verb can still read as true.

Choose the move

The question asks forFirst move
Stem names a sentence by positionFind it, then read how it joins the sentences around it
Two choices share every nounCompare the verbs, and check which way each one runs
Two acts both fit the sentenceTake the one whose object the text contains
Stem asks for the main purposeSay what the whole text is for. One act, or a pair: describing a thing in order to explain it

Worked examples

1. Say the act before reading the choices

The mill now stacks fresh-cut boards with a thin strip laid between every layer instead of face to face. The strips leave a channel for air at every layer, so a board buried in the pile dries at the rate one on the outside does, and the stack comes apart flat instead of cupped.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence in the text as a whole?

Task: name the job the second sentence does for the text.

Evidence: the first sentence reports a change in how the boards are stacked. The second says what the change buys: an even drying rate through the pile, and a stack that comes apart flat.

Answer: it explains the point of the change the sentence before it reports.

Closest trap: lists the conditions a board needs in order to dry flat has an object the sentence contains and an act it does not perform. Nothing here is listed.

2. Say what the whole text is for

Late opening at the county museum began as a one-year trial and has run for nine. The building costs the same to heat at seven as at two. The volunteers who staff the desk prefer the quiet. And the people who come in after work never came at all before.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Task: say what the whole text is for.

Evidence: the first sentence reports that a trial became permanent. The three that follow give the reasons it did: the heating costs the same, the volunteers prefer it, and it reaches people the museum was missing.

Answer: it describes an arrangement in order to explain why that arrangement lasted. A main purpose may combine two acts like this.

Closest trap: recommends late opening to other museums names an act the text never performs. It reports one museum and advises nobody.

3. Name the shape of the whole text

For two years the bins on Alder Street sat full on collection day while every other street's were emptied by noon. Residents assumed the crew was skipping them. The route map settles it. In November the city reversed the loop, so Alder went from first stop to last. Bins set out at eight were not collected until four.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Task: name the shape of the whole text.

Evidence: a puzzling pattern in the first sentence, the explanation residents assumed in the second, then the explanation the route map supplies.

Answer: a puzzling pattern, the assumed cause, then the supported one. Every sentence sits inside that shape.

Closest trap: a structure built from the reversed route alone names the last move and leaves the first two with nothing to do.

Common traps

Right topic, wrong verb

Trap Correction
wrong verb Check the act and the object together: a true object cannot rescue an act the text never performs
unanchored noun The act fits and the object names something the text never raises
wrong direction Check which way the text runs before you accept the relationship
wrong category Name the family of act: correcting a belief is not comparing options
true-but-partial Ask what the choice leaves with nothing to do
accurate-but-irrelevant Re-read the stem's pointer: a true reading of another sentence misses the ask

The method: Name the act and its object

  1. Identify the part the stem asks about: one sentence, the overall structure, or the main purpose.
  2. State what that part does, in your own words. One act for a sentence; for a purpose, one act or a pair, such as describing a system in order to explain its design; the main moves in order for a structure.
  3. Check each choice's act and its object together. A choice fails when the text never performs the act, and it fails when the act fits and the object names something the text does not contain.
  4. Check the strength, then the coverage. Reject an act stronger or weaker than the text: a description is not a critique. Then ask whether the choice covers the main moves of what the stem named.

Practice 1

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
  • Introductory

The kitchen at four in the morning was the only warm room in the house, and Ama did her homework at its table with her coat still on. The stove had been out since ten, but its bricks gave the heat back slowly all night, which is why she worked there and not in her own room.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence in the text as a whole?

Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. The hour Ama sits down, four in the morning, is in the first sentence. The second gives an hour only for the stove going out, and the ordinal is this item's only pointer.
Correct. The first sentence reports where she works without saying why. The second supplies the reason twice over, the bricks holding heat and the room she avoids.
Trap: true-but-partial. The bricks are in the sentence, and reporting a detail it contains names a piece of the sentence rather than the job it does.
Trap: wrong verb. Disputing needs a claim to argue against, and nobody in the text has made one.

The method, applied

Step 1: the stem names the second sentence. Step 2: it says why the kitchen holds heat and why she works in it. Step 3: disputes is an act the text never performs, since no claim has been made to argue with. Step 4: the nearest rival, reports the hour, is a true reading of the first sentence and misses the ask.

Practice 2

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
  • Introductory

Bakers new to sourdough treat a starter like a jar of pantry goods: buy it, shelve it, use it when needed. But a starter is a living culture of yeast, and it eats. Left unfed, it goes quiet, and shelf space does none of the work a spoonful of flour and water does.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Trap: wrong category. The text weighs no costs and recommends nothing; it offers shelving as a mistaken belief and corrects it, which is a different family of act from setting two methods side by side and choosing.
Trap: wrong direction. The text ends on what neglect costs, a starter gone quiet, and reports no recovery at all; flour and water are named as the work shelf space does not do.
Trap: true-but-partial. Both halves are on the page, but this begins at the second sentence and drops the belief the rest of the text exists to correct.
Correct. Three moves, in order: what beginners assume, the fact that overturns it, and what ignoring the fact costs, which is a starter gone quiet. Every sentence sits inside that shape.

The method, applied

Step 1: the stem asks for the shape of the whole. Step 2: a wrong belief, the fact that overturns it, then the cost. Step 3: the text weighs no costs and recommends nothing, and what looks like two options is one belief being corrected. Step 4: coverage decides against the nearest rival, since defining and then naming ingredients starts at the second sentence and drops the opening move.

Practice 3

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
  • Standard

Mrs. Halloran had taught in the same room for thirty-one years and said she could name a class's trouble inside a week. "They arrive certain that arithmetic is a set of tricks somebody is keeping from them," she said. "A boy asked me on Tuesday for the trick for fractions." She did not argue with him. She handed him a ruler and a length of string and sent him out to measure the door, and he came back with three-eighths and no trick.

Which choice best describes the function of the third sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct. The second sentence says what her students arrive believing; the third is one student doing exactly that, on a named day. A case standing for the claim above it is an illustration, and it leaves that claim the size it was.
Trap: wrong verb. A qualification limits the claim above it, and the boy is an instance of that claim rather than an exception to it.
Trap: wrong verb. She reports the question and does not blame him for it. What follows is a ruler and a piece of string, not a reprimand.
Trap: accurate-but-irrelevant. The ruler and the string are the last sentence, and the ordinal is this item's only pointer.

The method, applied

Step 1: the stem names the third sentence, the boy asking for the trick for fractions. Step 2: it is one instance of what the sentence before it says about the whole class. Step 3: qualifies and illustrates both describe acts this kind of sentence can perform, so read the object. A qualification would need the boy to be a case the claim misses, and he is a case it fits. Step 4: faults is stronger than a teacher who answers the question with a ruler.

Practice 4

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
  • Standard

A whale that dies in open water sinks, and on the sea floor it lands where almost nothing else does: a few tons of food arriving at once in a place that lives on crumbs. What follows runs on a schedule. Scavengers with a long reach strip the soft tissue within months. Smaller drifters and worms then work the bones and the stained sediment around them for years. Last, mats of microbes settle in to break down the fats locked in the skeleton, and they can hold the site for decades.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Trap: wrong category. The text is organized by when, months to years to decades, not by who, and reordering the sentences would break it.
Trap: true-but-partial. Calling a carcass an exception is fair, but three of the five sentences are the timeline, and this choice has no room for them.
Correct. Sentence one is the observation, sentence two announces a schedule, and the last three keep it, months to years to decades. Every sentence sits inside that shape.
Trap: wrong verb. Correcting needs a belief and a correction, and the text names neither; it reports an event and its consequences and disagrees with no one.

The method, applied

Step 1: the stem asks for the shape of the whole, so read for the moves. Step 2: five words, an event, then its stages. Sentence one is the event, and the last three are the schedule it announces. Step 3: corrects needs a belief, and the text names none. Catalogues would let the sentences stand in any order, and months to years to decades will not. Step 4: coverage. Scarce food and an exception is fair to the first sentence and silent about the other four.

Practice 5

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
  • Advanced

The honor box at a roadside stand is a coffee can with a slot cut in the lid, and it is the whole of the operation's accounting: the growers set out the morning's crates, chalk a price on a board, and drive back to the field. Nobody watches the can, and the arithmetic still works, because a stand that runs itself can open at six and stay open until dark on a road that would never carry a paid hour. The can does come up short, most weeks and sometimes badly, and the shortfall is not a rounding error: on a bad Saturday it can eat a morning's takings. Even so, the shortfall costs less than the wage of the person who would stand there to prevent it.

Which choice best describes the function of the third sentence in the text as a whole?

Trap: wrong verb. Rejecting means telling the stand to stop, and the sentence reports a shortfall without recommending anything; the text keeps the can.
Trap: true-but-partial. The bad Saturday sizes the loss the sentence has just admitted, so naming the measurement skips the act it was brought in to serve.
Trap: wrong direction. Pricing a cost is not disowning a sum, and the last sentence weighs the shortfall against a wage and keeps the can.
Correct. The sentence grants that the can is short most weeks and refuses to shrink that to a rounding error. The next sentence holds the claim anyway with Even so, and granting a real cost while keeping your position is a concession.

The method, applied

Step 1: the stem names the third sentence, the one opening The can does come up short. Step 2: it hands back a real cost after two sentences of benefit. Step 3: the can, the shortfall and the arithmetic are all reported accurately, so read the acts. Step 4: strength decides it. The sentence acknowledges a drawback and the text keeps its position, Even so, the shortfall costs less than the wage, which is what a concession is. Rejecting the can would need the text to drop it, and dismissing the arithmetic would need it to disown the sum.

Practice 6

  • Craft and Structure
  • Text Structure and Purpose
  • Advanced

A river crossing a flat valley does not stay straight for long. On the outside of every bend the current runs fast and cuts into the bank; on the inside it slows and drops the sand it can no longer carry. Bend by bend the loop swings wider, until its two ends nearly meet across a neck of ground a few strides wide. One high flow is then enough: the river takes the shortcut, seals both ends of the loop with sand, and leaves it behind as an oxbow lake beside the new channel. It is the most abrupt thing a river does, and the map is redrawn in a season. Abrupt, though, describes only the crossing: the neck narrows for decades first, and a river that will cut off next spring is doing nothing this spring that it was not doing a hundred years ago.

Which choice best describes the function of the last sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct. The sentence before it makes a claim: a cutoff is the most abrupt thing a river does. The last sentence keeps that claim and limits what it covers, which is what qualifying is.
Trap: wrong direction. Abrupt, though, describes only the crossing keeps the word and assigns it a job, where a reversal would have taken it away.
Trap: unanchored noun. The sentence does acknowledge something, that abrupt fits only the crossing. What it acknowledges is not about predicting the moment, which the text never raises.
Trap: true-but-partial. The river due to cut off next spring does illustrate the slow approach, and that is a real part of what the sentence does. It is not the job the sentence does for the text, which is to cut down how much of the process abrupt covers.

The method, applied

Step 1: the stem names the last sentence, Abrupt, though, describes only the crossing. Step 2: it keeps the claim before it and cuts down how much of the process the claim covers. Step 3: three of the four acts are ones a closing sentence can perform, so read the objects. A reversal would take abrupt away, and this sentence keeps it. The illustration is real and partial: it is one clause of a sentence whose job is to limit the claim. Concedes names an act the sentence does perform, and attaches it to predicting the moment, which the text never raises. Step 4: what is left is keeping the claim and limiting its reach, which is qualifying.
