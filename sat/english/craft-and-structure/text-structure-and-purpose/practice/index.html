Text Structure and Purpose: practice session

20 questions on Text Structure and Purpose, in random order. Unlike the lesson, the difficulty of each question stays hidden until you open the solution, so you meet them the way test day hands them to you: with no label. There is no timer and no score. Answer a question, then press Show solution whenever you want to see how you did and why.

Question

  • Introductory

Set into the sidewalk outside some older buildings are small glass squares, cloudy and worn flat. They were laid to send daylight into the storeroom below, back when the only other option down there was an open flame. Most are dark now, sealed over from beneath, and the city leaves them where they are because taking up the paving would cost more than walking over them.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

The text names no trade and lists nothing; it follows one object through time.
Correct. Three moves, in the order the text makes them, and nothing is left over.
Nothing here argues for anything. The closing sentence explains a decision rather than urging one.
Nobody walks on a raised square, but the text never raises the question and never answers it.

Why this answer

Read the moves rather than the subject: what the squares were for, that they are dark now, and why nobody has dug them out. The correct choice names those three in order. A structure choice is graded on covering the whole text, and the other three each describe something the text never does.

Question

  • Introductory

A wheelbuilder brings every spoke to a low, even tension before raising any of them to full. The even pass looks like wasted work, since not one of those spokes will be left at that tension. It is what keeps the rim from being pulled out of true at the start, when a single tight spoke has nothing pulling back against it.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

No tool is named and nothing is listed. The text is about an order of work.
Only the first move is in the text. Nobody is said to skip the step, nothing is counted, and no cost is estimated; the text describes one order of work.
Correct. Three moves, in that order, and the last one is why the text exists.
There is no history here at all: no dates, no earlier form, no change over time.

Why this answer

The shape is a step, an objection to it that the text raises itself, and the answer. Coverage decides it: a choice has to reach all three moves, and the middle one, that the pass looks like waste, is the reason the last one is worth saying. Nothing here is a history, a survey of the trade, or a list.

Question

  • Introductory

A pinhole camera has no lens: light reaches the film through one small hole. Shrink the hole and the picture sharpens, since each point of the scene sends a narrower cone of light. Shrink it further and the picture softens again, because light spreads at the edges of a very small opening.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Film is named once, as where the light lands. What happens in the coating is never touched.
No lens is ever compared with anything. The text argues for nothing.
No size is given and no reader is advised. The text stops at why a limit exists.
Correct. Sharper, then softer again, and the reason for the turn: that is the whole text.

Why this answer

A main-purpose choice names the one act the whole text performs. Every sentence here is building toward the reversal, so the purpose is explaining why shrinking the hole stops helping. The other three name acts the text does not perform: it compares nothing, recommends nothing, and never explains the film.

Question

  • Introductory

Some hospitals paint a colored line along the floor for each department, so a visitor follows green to radiology instead of reading signs. The lines sit at foot level for a reason: a person who is anxious, or carrying a child, watches the ground a few steps ahead, and that is where the instruction has to be.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Signs appear once, as what the lines replace. No cost is mentioned and nothing is recommended.
Correct. The system, then the reason for its height, which is where the text is heading.
The text urges nothing on anyone. It reports why some hospitals' choice makes sense.
Green and radiology are one example. How the colors were assigned is never touched.

Why this answer

The colon in the second sentence is the tell: everything after it exists to say why the lines are low, and that is what the text is for. The other three each attach a real noun from the passage to an act the passage never performs.

Question

  • Introductory

A coaster brake sits inside the rear hub and is worked by pedaling backward. There is no lever on the handlebar and no cable running down the frame, which is why the bicycles built around it are the ones left outdoors all winter. Nothing on the outside of that hub can rust shut.

Which choice best describes the function of the first sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct. Where the brake lives and how it is worked; the two later sentences run on that.
The sentence supplies background, where the brake sits and how it is worked, and the later sentences build on it. It rates the system against nothing.
No problem is stated. Rust arrives at the end as something that cannot happen, not as a difficulty raised.
Nothing precedes the first sentence, so there is no question standing for it to answer.

Why this answer

The sentence supplies two facts, where the brake sits and how it is worked, and both later sentences run on them. That is background rather than a verdict: nothing here ranks the system against another. A problem needs a difficulty the sentence never raises, and an answer needs a question somebody has already asked.

Question

  • Introductory

Timber yards sort their stock into hardwood and softwood, and the two words mislead almost everyone who reads them. The terms record which kind of tree a board came from: a hardwood grows on a broadleaved tree that flowers, a softwood on a cone-bearing one. Balsa is a hardwood. It dents under a fingernail.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence in the text as a whole?

No board is misfiled. Balsa arrives later and is sorted correctly; it is the naming that misleads.
The sentence takes the two categories as given and says what they mean.
Correct. It fixes the definitions, and the balsa sentences put them to work.
Nothing is granted here. A concession gives ground, and this sentence gives none.

Why this answer

The first sentence says the words mislead; the second says what they really mean; the last two show a case where the plain reading fails. The middle sentence's job is fixing the meaning. The other three name acts absent from it: no example, no dispute, no concession.

Question

  • Standard

A steam locomotive on a long run had to stop for water, and every stop cost an express time it could not spare. The answer was a shallow trough laid between the rails for a quarter of a mile: a fireman dropped a scoop at speed and the tender filled without the train slowing. What the arrangement did not remove was the water itself. The troughs had to be fed, kept from freezing, and refilled after every train, so the labor moved from the station to the track.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Correct. Three moves, and the last one is what the closing sentence is for.
Only the first move is in the text. No railway is named, and the troughs are never said to have gone; the last sentence reports upkeep, not disuse.
Short runs never appear. The text opens on the long run and stays there.
No rule is stated and nothing is excepted; the closing sentence adds a cost, not a case that fails.

Why this answer

The give-away is the third sentence, which turns the text: the trough solved the stopping and not the water. So the shape is problem, solution, and the cost the solution moved somewhere else. The choice ending in abandonment describes a text that would have to keep going, and the other two attach it to a short run and to a rule it never states.

Question

  • Standard

A town's plaque scheme runs on one rule: the building must be the one the person actually lived or worked in. A house they were born in qualifies. A house rebuilt on the same plot does not, and neither does a street named after them. The hard case is a school extended twice around its original hall. The committee grants it, on the ground that the room where the person taught is still standing inside the newer walls, which is what the rule was written to protect.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

The one decision reported is endorsed, not faulted. No criticism appears anywhere.
There is a single rule. The school is a hard case under it, not a second rule against it.
The text applies the rule as written and does not ask for it to change.
Correct. Rule, clear cases both ways, borderline case decided on the rule's purpose.

Why this answer

Coverage is what decides this one. The text runs rule, easy cases, hard case, and the reasoning that settles it, and only one choice reaches all four. The others describe a conflict, a criticism, and a proposal, none of which the text contains.

Question

  • Standard

Growers argue about whether to plow a field over winter or to sow a cover crop on it, as though the two were rival routes to the same place. They are not. A plow loosens the top nine inches in an afternoon and leaves the surface open to the first heavy rain. Rye sown in September loosens nothing that autumn, but its roots reach two feet down, and the channels they leave stay open for years after the crop is killed off. One is a treatment. The other is a structure.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Correct. "They are not" is the turn, and the closing pair names what each one is instead.
One sentence of six, given so the comparison has something to stand on.
The text refuses to rank them. Saying two things do different jobs is not choosing between them.
Two feet is a fact in service of the point, not the point. The text is about the two practices.

Why this answer

The second sentence is three words long and does all the work: it denies that the two are alternatives, and everything after it says what each one is instead. A choice that picks a winner performs an act the text explicitly declines, and the plow and root choices each take one sentence for the whole.

Question

  • Standard

The knotter is the mechanism on a baler that ties twine around each finished bale, and on a sound machine it works several thousand times without a miss. It is still the first thing a driver checks each morning. A knotter that fails does not stop the baler: the machine keeps compressing hay and pushing out bales that fall apart in the field, sometimes for an hour before anyone notices.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

That is the opening clause, offered so the rest can be said. The text moves past it immediately.
A reasonable idea the text never raises. Nothing here proposes a change to the machine.
Correct. The whole text is answering the question the second sentence sets up.
Only one mechanism is discussed. There is nothing for it to be compared against.

Why this answer

Sentence two states the puzzle, and everything after it answers: the failure does not stop the machine, so it can run for an hour before anyone notices while the bales fall apart. That is the act the whole text performs. The description choice takes the opening clause for the point, and the other two attach real nouns to acts the text never performs.

Question

  • Standard

A water-ballast funicular needs no engine at all. The car at the top fills a tank until it outweighs the car at the bottom, and gravity does the rest, the two joined by a single cable over a drum at the summit. The arrangement only works on a hill with water at the top of it, which is why so few were ever built. Where the water was there, a line could run for a century on a tank valve and a brake.

Which choice best describes the function of the third sentence in the text as a whole?

The cable and drum are the second sentence's business. This sentence adds no mechanism.
Correct. The limit is conceded, and the claim resumes where the condition holds rather than being dropped.
That claim survives untouched. Needing a hill and needing an engine are different things.
No second kind appears. The last sentence returns to the same system, not a different one.

Why this answer

The sentence gives ground: the system has a condition, and the condition is why the thing is rare. What tells you it is a concession rather than a retreat is the sentence after it, which takes the claim back up where the condition holds. Note that the text does not get around the condition; it stays inside it. Conceding is a move on the way to a claim, not the surrender of one.

Question

  • Advanced

A seed drill sets every seed at one depth, and depth is most of what decides whether a seed comes up. Too shallow and it dries before the root reaches moisture; too deep and the shoot spends itself before it reaches light. The coulter that cuts the slot rides on its own wheel, so it follows the ground rather than the frame, and the depth holds across a field that is far from flat. On ground carrying loose stones the wheel rides up over each one, and the drill lays a run of seed too shallow to come up.

Which choice best describes the function of the last sentence in the text as a whole?

Nothing is proposed. The sentence reports what happens and stops there.
It leans on that claim. Shallow seed failing is the claim being true, not being challenged.
It points the other way: the sentence narrows to stony ground rather than widening to every kind.
Correct. A real limit is admitted: the coulter still follows slope, and stones are what its wheel cannot ride through.

Why this answer

The third sentence claims the coulter holds depth over ground that is not flat; the last one names a condition in which it does not. That is a concession, and it limits the earlier claim without overturning it, since the coulter still follows slope. A choice that reads it as widening the account, or as denying the depth rule, has the direction backward.

Question

  • Standard

A scythe is sharpened twice over, by two different acts. Honing straightens an edge that is already thin but cannot make one thin, so a blade that is only honed goes blunt within a season; peening is what thins it. The mower lays the blade on a small anvil and taps along the last half inch of steel, working down its length until the metal spreads thinner than any grinding would leave it. He peens in spring and hones every twenty minutes all summer.

Which choice best describes the function of the third sentence in the text as a whole?

Correct. The second sentence says peening thins the edge; this one is the thinning happening.
The text ranks neither operation; it gives them different jobs. This sentence grants honing nothing.
There is no objection in the sentence, which reports the method in the voice of a manual.
That is the last sentence, and letting go of the ordinal costs the point even when the reading was fine.

Why this answer

The sentence before it makes a claim about what peening does; this one runs the claim as an instance, in the order the mower does it. Illustrating a claim a neighboring sentence makes is a function, and the other three name acts the sentence never performs or belong to a different sentence.

Question

  • Advanced

A turbine takes energy out of the wind, so the machines standing behind it meet air that is slower and more broken, and produce less. That is the measured rule, and on most days a back row runs well below a front one. On some days a back row matches the front one outright. What reconciles the two is not stronger wind but less steady wind. A wake is a ribbon of slow air, and the only way it refills is by mixing with the faster air around it. In gusty weather that mixing is quick, and the ribbon thins and closes up within a few rotor lengths instead of holding its shape for a mile. Steady air is what lets a wake carry its deficit the whole way to the machine behind it.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Correct. Rule, apparent exception, and the reconciling fact, which is what the last three sentences supply.
Neither measurement is rejected. The text keeps both and explains how they fit.
A wake does get defined, in one clause, and no single installation appears. The definition serves the reconciliation; it is not what the text is.
Taking energy out of the wind is the premise the text starts from, never the thing it explains, and no quantity and no price is ever given, so none of the three moves happens.

Why this answer

Name the shape before reading the choices: a rule, then days that appear to break it, then the condition under which they do not. The reconciliation is the move that matters, and only one choice has it. Concluding that a measurement is wrong points the opposite way, since the text keeps both and finds the condition that makes them agree.

Question

  • Advanced

For years the creamery's suppliers and its weighers argued about short weight, and neither side could settle it. A full churn was weighed on delivery, the empty churn's weight subtracted, and the difference paid for; but the churns came from three makers in three different weights, and no record said which was which. The case for stamping every churn with its own empty weight was made early, and one objection to it was never answered: a stamped figure holds only while the churn does, and repairs push the empty weight up year by year away from the number on the metal. What carried the rule in the end was the auditor, who could not close a year's accounts while the milk paid for and the butter produced disagreed by more than spillage could explain.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

The dispute is real and opens the text, but nobody compromises: the rule arrives from outside the argument.
The text says outright that the objection was never answered. What carried the rule came from outside the argument instead.
Correct. Four moves, opening on the dispute, and the last is the one the text is built to deliver.
The auditor is reported as what happened, not urged as what ought to happen.

Why this answer

The turn is at "one objection to it was never answered", which tells you the objection still stands and something else did the work. So the shape is a dispute, a case, unanswered resistance, and a pressure from outside the argument. Note that the opening dispute is part of the shape: a structure choice starting at the case would be accurate about the last two sentences and silent about the first two. The choice that has the objection refuted describes a text this one explicitly is not, and the compromise choice invents an agreement nobody reaches.

Question

  • Advanced

Most of the field gates on the estate have a hole bored clean through the top rail, an inch in from the latch end. Two explanations circulate among the people who maintain them. One is drainage: end grain drinks water, and a hole lets it back out before the rail goes soft. The other is a tether: a peg dropped through the hole holds a chain, and a chain holds a gate open against the wind. Both are plainly true of the gates that have them. Neither reaches the oldest gates in the yard, whose holes are bored at the hinge end instead, where no water stands and no chain would reach the post.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Half of one of the two accounts, offered so the account can be stated. The text moves straight past it.
That is one of the two accounts the text sets aside, not the position it holds.
The difference between old and new gates is evidence for the point, not the point. The text is not a survey of the estate's gates.
Correct. Both accounts, granted in full, then the older gates that neither one covers.

Why this answer

Most of the text builds the two explanations and the last two sentences take both away, granting each on the gates it fits and then producing gates it does not. A purpose choice has to cover that ending, so the choice endorsing the tether account names the text's target rather than its point, and the drainage and description choices stop at material the text uses in passing.

Question

  • Advanced

A trawl net's mesh is diamond-shaped, and under tow the diamonds pull long and narrow, so the openings a small fish might slip through close as the net fills. A square-mesh panel set into the top sheet does not close: its openings hold their shape under tension, and young fish, which swim upward when startled, find their way out. The panels were required in order to protect those fish, and they do. What nobody foresaw is what they did to the catch that stayed in. Skippers found that a net which sheds its undersized fish at sea lands a more uniform box, and that a uniform box grades and sells at a price which can more than cover the volume lost over the side.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

The opening sentence, and the text uses it to set up a contrast rather than to rest on it.
The text reports an outcome nobody planned. It recommends nothing about how rules are written.
Correct. Required for the young fish, repaid by the price of a uniform box.
The text says the panel lands less volume, and recommends nothing to anyone.

Why this answer

The hinge is "what nobody foresaw": everything before it is the rule's stated reason and everything after is a different payoff. That gap is the point of the text. Reading it as advice to regulators or as a recommendation between designs attaches an act the text never performs to nouns it really contains.

Question

  • Advanced

A dry dock is closed by a caisson, a hollow steel box floated into the entrance and then flooded until it settles on the sill. Nothing bolts it in place. The obvious objection is that the sea outside stands many feet higher than the empty dock inside, and that a box merely resting in a slot ought to be pushed straight through it. That pressure is exactly what holds the caisson shut: it bears on the outer face and drives the box hard against the stone it is seated in, so the harder the sea pushes, the tighter the joint becomes.

Which choice best describes the function of the last sentence in the text as a whole?

It raises nothing. The sentence is where the difficulty already raised gets resolved.
The text says the opposite: the sea's pressure, not the weight, is what seals it.
Correct. The pressure that was the problem turns out to be the mechanism.
No failure is described. Nothing in the text reports a caisson being pushed through.

Why this answer

The third sentence puts an objection; the last one meets it, and meets it by turning the objection's own force into the answer. A sentence that answers an objection is doing something different from raising one, and the choices that have it conceding or illustrating a failure describe acts absent from the text.

Question

  • Advanced

The garden wall's lower four feet are laid in English bond, headers and stretchers in alternate courses, and everything above is Flemish, the two alternating within each course. A county survey read the change as two building phases, the upper part a later heightening. But English bond went out of ordinary use in this district around 1700 just as Flemish came in, so a bricklayer working a single job in those decades would have had both in hand, and the join itself is level and shows none of the weathering a gap of years would have left. What the survey read as a seam may be one week's work.

Which choice best describes the function of the third sentence in the text as a whole?

The date range and the level, unweathered join work against the two phases rather than sharpening them.
Correct. The survey's two phases are met with a date range and a level join, which together suggest one job.
The sentence checks it, and finds against it. That is the opposite of conceding.
That one went out and the other came in is stated as a premise; why is never touched.

Why this answer

The sentence assembles two pieces of evidence against the survey's two phases: both bonds were in ordinary use at once, and the join is level and unweathered. That argues for a rival explanation rather than restating the survey or conceding to it. The last sentence keeps the verdict tentative, which is the text being careful.

Question

  • Standard

On a grand piano the left pedal shifts the whole action sideways by a few millimeters. A hammer that would have struck all three strings of its note squarely, with the hardest and most played-in grooves of its felt, now reaches two of them, and reaches those with felt from the side of the head, which is softer and far less compacted. What changes is not only how loud the note is but what it is made of, which is why the pedal has survived on instruments where a volume control would have been simpler.

Which choice best describes the function of the second sentence in the text as a whole?

That is the would-have half only. The sentence runs the two states together, and taking either alone describes half of it.
Nothing is granted, and the text ends by arguing the effect is larger than volume alone.
The text closes on the opposite: the pedal survived precisely because a volume control would not do what it does.
Correct. One hammer, two parts of its felt and two counts of strings, held against each other in one sentence.

Why this answer

The contrast is carried by the tense pair, "would have struck" against "now reaches", rather than by a label on each state, but it is a contrast and both halves are there. Taking either state on its own describes half the sentence, which is the commonest way to lose this item, and the text neither concedes anything nor argues for the alternative it names at the end.
