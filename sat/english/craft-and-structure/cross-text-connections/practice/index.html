Cross-Text Connections: practice session

20 questions on Cross-Text Connections, in random order. Unlike the lesson, the difficulty of each question stays hidden until you open the solution, so you meet them the way test day hands them to you: with no label. There is no timer and no score. Answer a question, then press Show solution whenever you want to see how you did and why.

Question

  • Introductory

Text 1

Kaminski's library dropped its overdue fines two years ago and has not regretted it. Books that had been kept for months, because the fine had grown past what the borrower could face, came back within weeks of the announcement. What a fine holds onto, Kaminski argues, is not the book but the borrower.

Text 2

Sowande has followed the same change at eleven libraries and finds Kaminski right about the borrowers, and right for a second reason Kaminski does not give. Once the hours spent at the counter are priced in, a fine costs more to collect than it raises. A library that keeps its fines is paying for the privilege of losing readers.

Based on the texts, how would Sowande (Text 2) most likely respond to Kaminski's claim in Text 1 that what a fine holds onto is not the book but the borrower?

Text 2's first sentence says Kaminski is right about the borrowers, so nothing in it rejects the claim. This is the blunt reading the page warns about: a partial disagreement reported as a flat one.
Correct. Text 2 grants the cited claim in as many words and then supplies a reason of its own, which is what makes both halves compulsory in the answer.
True, and Text 2 does not contest it. Both halves are Kaminski's own, so what looks like an addition adds nothing of Sowande's, and the second cost that is the reason Text 2 was written never appears.
Text 2 prices fines and finds them a bad bargain at eleven libraries. That is a long way from never and anywhere, which are words Text 2 spends nowhere.

Why this answer

Sowande's opening words grant the cited claim, so the relationship is agreement and the answer has to open by granting it. What Text 2 adds is a cost Kaminski never counts, the staff time a fine takes to collect, so the answer has to carry that too. Rejecting the claim contradicts Text 2's own first sentence. Reporting the returned books stops at the granted half. And nothing in Text 2 rules out a late charge everywhere and for ever.

Question

  • Introductory

Text 1

A young reef fish finds its way home by ear. The crackle of shrimp and the grind of parrotfish carry for miles, and a larva drifting in the open swims toward the noise. Moor speakers over a bleached reef, a marine biologist proposes, and the larvae will come back and rebuild it.

Text 2

Ferrand's team moored speakers over four bleached patches for a season and counted the settlers each morning. Larvae arrived, three times as many as over the silent controls. A month later almost none were alive. Rubble holds nothing for a young fish to eat and nowhere for it to hide, and the sound had carried them onto it.

Based on the texts, how would Ferrand's team (Text 2) most likely respond to the proposal in Text 1 that speakers moored over a bleached reef will bring the larvae back to rebuild it?

Text 2 reports exactly this, and it is the half of the proposal that worked. It is not a response to the proposal, which was about rebuilding rather than arriving.
Text 1 says it and Text 2 does not contest it. Repeating a premise both texts share answers nothing the stem asked.
Text 2's own count says the opposite: the sound brought three times as many larvae in. What it denies is what happened to them afterward, not that they heard anything.
Correct. The proposal is that the larvae will come back and rebuild the reef, and Text 2 reports that the coming back happened and the rebuilding did not.

Why this answer

The stem cites the proposal, and the proposal has two parts joined: the larvae come back, and they rebuild. Ferrand's team grants the first and reports that almost none survived a month, so the arrival stands and the rebuilding does not. The proposal was for a rebuilt reef, and that half does not survive. Confirming the arrival and counting it delivers the granted half twice. The carrying power of reef noise is a premise both texts share. And Text 2's own counts refute the idea that sound does nothing.

Question

  • Introductory

Text 1

A bus that runs once an hour is a bus nobody plans around. Halve the wait and the same route carries more than twice the passengers, because a ten-minute gap is a service and a sixty-minute gap is an appointment. Every route in the county, a transport officer writes, would fill up if it ran often enough.

Text 2

Adeyemi's group doubled the frequency on nine county routes for a year and counted the boardings. On the six that run between towns boardings rose by more than the officer had predicted, and by the end of the year the evening buses on all six were running full. On the three that end at a village with one shop and no evening trade, they did not move: the extra buses ran emptier, because there was nothing at the far end to go to at that hour.

Based on the texts, how would Adeyemi's group (Text 2) most likely respond to the officer's claim in Text 1 that every route in the county would fill up if it ran often enough?

Correct. The claim named its own range, every route in the county, and Text 2 keeps six of the nine and withholds three, which is a cut of the range rather than a rejection of the claim.
Text 2 reports that boardings rose on six of the nine routes by more than the officer predicted. A rejection this flat denies those six along with the three.
Text 1 puts it that way and Text 2 does nothing to dispute it. A premise the two texts share is not a response to the claim built on it.
This keeps the officer's strength and widens the subject past the county Text 2 measured. It is also the opposite of what the three village routes showed.

Why this answer

The officer's claim states its own range: every route in the county. Adeyemi's group tested nine and found the claim held on six and failed on three, so the response is to keep the claim over part of the range it named and withhold it over the rest. Rejecting the officer's range is fair; the rejection on offer goes further and holds that frequency bears on nothing at all, which denies the six routes Text 2 says the claim held on. Restating the gap arithmetic repeats a premise both texts hold. And widening the claim to any rural route anywhere goes further than either text and further than the counts allow.

Question

  • Introductory

Text 1

The air near the ground can fall several degrees below the air twenty feet up. A tall fan set in an orchard pulls that warmer air down and mixes it, and a grower who runs one keeps the blossom that a neighbor loses. The machines are noisy and they are not cheap, but an orchardist writes that they earn a season's cost back in one bad spring.

Text 2

Delacroix's group logged blossom loss at thirty orchards over four springs. Where the night was still and the sky clear, the fans did what the orchardist describes. On the nights that took the worst of the crop a wind was already blowing, and the cold came in with it rather than settling; there was no warm layer overhead to bring down, and the fans turned in it to no effect.

Based on the texts, how would Delacroix's group (Text 2) most likely respond to the claim in Text 1 that a grower who runs a fan keeps the blossom a neighbor loses?

Text 2 says that on still, clear nights the fans did what the orchardist describes. A choice that denies the effect anywhere contradicts the sentence Text 2 grants it in.
Text 2 offers the logging as its evidence and never questions it. This turns a disagreement about when the fans work into a disagreement about whether the counting could show anything.
Correct. Text 2 grants the effect and supplies a condition Text 1 never names, which is what separates a qualification from a rejection.
Text 1 opens with it and Text 2 builds on it. It is the ground both texts stand on rather than anything either says to the other.

Why this answer

Text 1's claim names no range at all: a grower who runs a fan keeps the blossom. Delacroix's group grants that on still, clear nights and finds that on windy nights there is no warm layer to bring down, so the answer attaches a condition the claim never contemplated. Denying the effect everywhere contradicts what Text 2 concedes. Disputing the logging is an argument Text 2 never has. And the temperature inversion is a premise both texts share.

Question

  • Introductory

Text 1

Brandt's history opens in 1720, when the parish first paid two men to sing at the two great feasts. The books show a payment in almost every year since, and Brandt calls the body they record the oldest choir in the county. The claim is printed on the concert programs.

Text 2

Ilves grants every payment in Brandt's list and reads them differently. Two men paid for two feasts is a duty, not a choir; the same books pay a man to ring the bell, and nobody calls that an orchestra. What the record shows without a break is an obligation. The society that meets on Thursdays was founded in 1948, and its members would say so first.

Based on the texts, how would Ilves (Text 2) most likely characterize Brandt's claim in Text 1 that the body the parish books record is the oldest choir in the county?

The first half is Ilves's, and the second is a doubt he never raises: his opening sentence grants every payment in the list. Refusing the word and the record together goes further than Text 2 goes anywhere, which is stance inflation.
Correct. Nothing factual is in dispute; what Ilves refuses is the name given to the facts, which is the one move on this page where the evidence survives whole.
Ilves grants exactly this and opens by saying so. It delivers the granted half and never reaches the refusal that is the whole of Text 2's move.
The grant is Ilves's own and the refusal is pitched at a strength Text 2 never reaches: it dates this society to 1948 on the strength of what it does, not on a rule about all societies. The no and the ever are Text 2's nowhere.

Why this answer

Ilves concedes every payment Brandt lists and disputes none of the dates, so every fact behind the claim survives; what is refused is the word choir. Two singers hired for two feasts is a duty, and the bell-ringer is the parallel Text 2 supplies to show it. Refusing the word and doubting the books together goes further than Ilves goes anywhere, since he takes every payment as read. Agreeing about the payments stops at what Ilves grants. And granting the record and refusing the claim by a rule binding every society there is reaches a strength Text 2 spends nowhere.

Question

  • Introductory

Text 1

Chapter titles belong to whoever printed them, and in this novel that was not the author. Larkspur's manuscript runs in numbered chapters with nothing written over them; the titles a modern reader knows were supplied by the first publisher, to sell the book in parts. A new edition should set them aside.

Text 2

Ferrers accepts all of that and would keep the titles anyway, at a price the reader should be told. They are the publisher's, and they are also what every reader for a hundred and forty years has read the book by; take them out and the novel loses a shape it has had for longer than it has been without one. Print them, and print a note saying whose they are.

Based on the texts, how would Ferrers (Text 2) most likely respond to the claim in Text 1 that the chapter titles belong to whoever printed them and in this novel that was not the author?

Text 2's opening words accept all of it, the attribution included. This makes Ferrers dispute the one thing the two texts are not arguing about.
Text 2 asks for a note saying whose the titles are, which is a request to keep the distinction this choice erases. Its case for printing them is the shape readers have, not a claim about standing.
Both texts hold it and neither argues about it. A shared premise repeated is not a response to the claim resting on it.
Correct. Text 2 grants the claim whole and attaches a cost, which the claim survives; that combination is what the answer has to carry.

Why this answer

Ferrers accepts the attribution outright, so the relationship at the cited claim is agreement, and what Text 2 adds is what removing the titles would cost. Denying the publisher's hand contradicts the sentence Text 2 grants it in. Giving a printer's title the same claim as the author's erases a distinction Text 2 keeps, since it asks for a note saying whose the titles are; Text 2's case for printing them is the shape readers have, not any claim about standing. And restating the manuscript's numbered chapters repeats a premise the two texts share.

Text 1

Coal came inland by water, and the countryside took it up long after the towns had. From the wharf books and the carriers' ledgers, Sandoval fixes the changeover and states its reach: every parish in the county had gone over to coal by 1830.

Text 2

Nakagawa's group read four hundred parish overseers' accounts, which record what was bought for the poorhouse fire week by week. Along the two rivers the change is complete by 1830, as Sandoval says. In the eleven parishes on the high ground the overseers are still buying wood by the cartload in 1870, and the first coal entry anywhere among them is 1884.

Based on the texts, how would Nakagawa's group (Text 2) most likely respond to Sandoval's claim in Text 1 that every parish in the county had gone over to coal by 1830?

Nakagawa's group grants the changeover along the rivers in as many words, on the strength of the overseers' accounts they read. A denial this flat throws out the river parishes Text 2 accepts.
Text 2 says exactly this, and it is the half it grants. Stopping here drops the eleven parishes that are the reason Nakagawa's group counted anything.
Correct. The claim named its own range, every parish in the county, and Text 2 keeps part of that range and withholds the rest.
Nakagawa's own finding is that the date does not hold across one county, so this keeps Sandoval's strength and widens the subject in the direction Text 2 closed.

Why this answer

Sandoval's claim states its own range, every parish in the county, and four hundred sets of overseers' accounts split that range in two: along the rivers the changeover is where Sandoval puts it, and on the high ground the poorhouse fire is still burning wood forty years later. So the claim survives over part of the range it named and is withheld over the rest. Rejecting Sandoval's range is fair; the rejection on offer goes further and denies his sources any word at all, which throws out the river parishes Text 2 grants. Reporting the river parishes delivers the granted half. And dating every county in the region widens exactly what Text 2 narrowed.

Question

  • Standard

Text 1

Wexler's atlas maps two hundred words across four hundred villages, and the method is the same in every one: find the oldest speaker who has never lived elsewhere, and write down what they say. The atlas is therefore a record of the county's speech as it stood before the roads came. Its lines are drawn from one mouth per village, and Wexler says so on the first page.

Text 2

Adeyinka disputes none of the fieldwork and reads the result differently. One speaker per village is a record of what the oldest and least travelled person in each place said in one afternoon, which is a real thing and is not the county's speech: half of every village was younger, and the younger half is where a change lives. The atlas maps the most conservative speech available, village by village.

Based on the texts, how would Adeyinka (Text 2) most likely characterize Wexler's account in Text 1 of what the atlas records, namely the county's speech as it stood before the roads came?

Correct. Every fact survives; what Adeyinka refuses is the yardstick, because a sampling rule that selects for age cannot answer a question about a whole county.
The first half is Adeyinka's own. The second is a doubt he opens by refusing: his first clause disputes none of the fieldwork. Refusing the yardstick and the fieldwork together is the loud reading this page warns about.
Text 1 says it and Adeyinka does not deny it. It is the half both texts hold, and it never reaches what the atlas is a record of.
Adeyinka never sorts a village into a half the account holds for and a half it does not; one speaker is the wrong record either way. So both halves fail: the younger half is what Text 2 says the atlas never reached, and Text 2 grants in as many words that the atlas did record the oldest speaker in every village.

Why this answer

Adeyinka grants the fieldwork in as many words and objects to the description of what it produced: a rule that picks the oldest and least travelled speaker in each village collects the most conservative speech there, which is a real record and is not a county's speech. Nothing factual is in dispute. Refusing the yardstick and the fieldwork together contradicts Text 2's opening clause, which disputes none of the fieldwork. Agreeing about the stated method repeats what both texts hold. And splitting each village into a half the account holds for and a half it does not is a distinction Text 2 never draws, and it fails in both directions at once: the younger half is the one the atlas never reached, and Adeyinka grants that it did record the oldest speaker everywhere it went.

Text 1

A laid hedge is worth more to a farm than a fence, and the arithmetic is not close. A hedge shelters stock, holds soil on a slope, and feeds every hedgerow bird the parish has, where a fence does one job. Laying costs a week of one person's time every fifteen years, and Costa, who has done it, will tell you it pays.

Text 2

Bertelsen's group costed both on twenty-two farms across nine years. On the mixed farms the hedge came out ahead on every count Costa names. On the four that run stock over thin chalk, the hedge took eleven years to close and had to be fenced anyway while it grew, so the farm paid for both; the arithmetic there runs the other way until the hedge is up.

Based on the texts, how would Bertelsen's group (Text 2) most likely respond to Costa's verdict in Text 1 that a laid hedge is worth more to a farm than a fence?

Nothing here turns on what to call the two. Text 2 grants the counts and then names the ground where a hedge takes eleven years to close, which limits the verdict rather than refusing the word for it.
Correct. Text 2 accepts the counts and attaches a condition Text 1 never names, which is a qualification and not a refusal.
This is exactly what Text 2 grants, and stopping there drops the four chalk farms that are the reason Bertelsen's group wrote anything.
Text 1 states the labor cost and Costa's verdict on it, and Text 2 never argues about either. This is a real point about hedges aimed at a claim the stem did not cite.

Why this answer

Bertelsen's group grants Costa's arithmetic on the mixed farms and finds that on thin chalk the hedge takes eleven years to close and has to be fenced while it grows, so the farm pays twice. That is a condition on the verdict rather than a strike against it. Reading the response as a quarrel about the word better makes it smaller than it is: Text 2 attaches a condition to the verdict, which is a limit on the claim and not a relabelling of it. Question

  • Standard

Text 1

Okonkwo's market has lost a third of its stalls in six years, and the empty pitches are what drive the rest away: a market with gaps in it reads to a shopper as a market that is closing. Rebate half the rent on any pitch that has stood empty for a month, Okonkwo proposes, and the gaps will fill.

Text 2

Sedlak's team tracked the rebate at seven markets that tried it. The empty pitches filled, and they filled with traders who moved off full-rent pitches a few yards away, so the gaps moved rather than closing and the market took less rent for the same stalls. What empties a pitch is the footfall, and a rebate brings nobody through the gate. The gaps are worth closing; this is not the way.

Based on the texts, how would Sedlak's team (Text 2) most likely respond to Okonkwo's proposal in Text 1 that rebating half the rent on a pitch empty for a month will fill the gaps?

Text 2 grants the filling in its second sentence. It is the half that happened, and it drops where the traders came from, which is the whole of Sedlak's point.
Text 1 opens on how a gappy market reads to a shopper, and Text 2 never takes that up at all; what it argues about is what empties a pitch. A real claim of Text 1's, aimed at a sentence the stem did not cite.
Sedlak's team reports traders moving pitch precisely because of the rent. The finding is that the rebate moved them, not that rent moves nobody.
Correct. The proposal does not survive: the gaps moved rather than closing, and Text 2 keeps the aim while saying the means will not reach it.

Why this answer

The stem cites the proposal, and Sedlak's team finds it does not do what it was for: the pitches filled with traders who left other pitches empty, so the gaps moved and the rent fell. Text 2 keeps Okonkwo's goal in its last sentence and denies the means. Confirming the filling and how wide it went reports the granted half twice. How a gappy market reads to a shopper is a point Text 1 makes and Text 2 never takes up. And Text 2's own finding depends on rent moving traders, which the third option denies.

Text 1

A badger will not go around a new fence; it will go through it, and a fence laid across an old path costs a farmer the fence. A gate set into the wire on the line the animals already use saves those repairs, and the animals take it within a week. The gates are cheap, and a wildlife officer writes that any farm with a path across it should fit one.

Text 2

Vieira's group fitted gates on nineteen farms and photographed them for two years. The repairs stopped, as the officer says. The photographs also turned up a saving nobody had costed: hares, foxes and a great many hedgehogs used the same gates, and on the four farms that had been losing lambs through a hole in the wire the hole never reopened.

Based on the texts, how would Vieira's group (Text 2) most likely respond to the claim in Text 1 that a gate set on the line the animals already use saves the repairs?

Text 2's second sentence says the repairs stopped, as the officer describes. Nothing in it denies the saving the stem cites.
Text 1 opens with it and Text 2 builds on it. A premise the two texts share is not an answer to the claim resting on it.
Correct. Text 2 grants the cited claim and then supplies a second saving from its own photographs, so the answer has to carry both halves.
Text 1 closes on exactly this, and Text 2 does nothing to dispute it. It is a real claim of Text 1's, aimed at a sentence the stem did not cite.

Why this answer

Vieira's group grants the cited claim outright, the repairs having stopped as the officer describes, so the relationship is agreement and the answer must open by granting it. Two years of photographs then add a saving the officer never counted, the lamb-sized hole that stayed shut. Denying the saving contradicts Text 2's own sentence. Restating how a badger meets a fence repeats a shared premise. And what a gate costs, with the recommendation Text 1 draws from it, is Text 1's own closing point, which the stem does not cite.

Text 1

A poem a poet has carried into their own second language is that poem in another language, and an editor should print it as such. Salvatierra wrote in both and made the second version of forty of the poems, so those versions are not a stranger's guesses at what was meant. They are the meaning itself, set down twice.

O Cadhain grants every one of those facts and refuses the word. The two versions differ, in eleven of the forty by a whole stanza, and a poet revising is a poet writing: what Salvatierra made in the second language is a second poem by the same hand, which is a rarer thing than a translation and should be printed as one. Print both, and call them what they are.

Correct. Text 2 grants the authorship, the count and the differences, and refuses only the name; that is the one move where nothing factual is in dispute.
The first half is O Cadhain's own. The second is a dispute he opens by refusing: he grants every one of those facts, the count included. Refusing the word and the count together is the loud reading this page warns about.
Both texts hold it, and Text 2's whole argument depends on it. Repeating what neither side disputes answers nothing the stem asked.
Text 2 does say it, in its last line. It is a position O Cadhain holds about what an edition should do, where the stem cites the claim about what the second version IS.

Why this answer

O Cadhain disputes none of it: Salvatierra wrote both, and the second versions are not a stranger's work. What is refused is the word, on the ground that eleven of the forty differ by a whole stanza and a poet revising is writing. Nothing factual moves, which is what marks the move out from a disagreement. Refusing the word and the count together contradicts Text 2's opening, which grants every one of those facts. For the rationed goods Text 2 finds the returns and the account books in close agreement, and says so. A limit is not a demolition.

Text 2 uses the account books to measure what the returns cover, not to catch the shops out, and it reports close agreement where the two overlap.
This keeps Text 1's strength and widens the subject to every town, in the direction Text 2's own finding closes.

Why this answer

The claim's range is what a household took home, and Ivarsdottir splits it: on the rationed goods the returns and the account books agree closely, and on everything off the ration the returns are silent while the account books show that share growing. So the claim survives over part of the range it named. Rejecting it ignores the agreement. Disputing the returns is an argument Text 2 never makes. A counter that costs a building to keep open is a counter that closes. Larusson would keep every one of the moved counters and thinks the officer has costed only half of what was moved. The rent is somebody else's; so are the hours, and the hours are what a village uses.

Based on the texts, how would Larusson (Text 2) most likely respond to the account in Text 1 of why a moved counter survives, namely that the service carries on at somebody else's rent?

Larusson would keep every one of the moved counters and says so in the first clause. Reading a cost as a removal is the move this page names.
Both halves sit inside what Text 2 grants, so the second move adds nothing of Larusson's, and the hours, which are the whole of what Larusson does add, never appear.
Text 1 says it outright and Text 2 disputes none of it. It is a real claim of Text 1's, aimed at a sentence the stem did not cite.
Correct. Text 2 grants the mechanism whole and attaches what travels with it, a cost the account survives, so the answer has to carry both halves.

Why this answer

Larusson grants the officer's mechanism whole, keeping every moved counter, and attaches what travels with it: a counter inside a shop cannot be open when the shop is shut, so the hours come from the host along with the rent. The account survives and is priced, which is agreement with a cost attached rather than a rejection. Denying the mechanism contradicts Text 2's own first clause. Reporting the rent and the branches it kept open delivers the granted half twice over. And the released buildings are a point Text 1 raised that Text 2 never takes up.

Based on the texts, how would Ilesanmi (Text 2) most likely characterize Halloway's verdict in Text 1 that what was designed is the building and what was built is a partial execution of it?

Ilesanmi accepts every difference and every date in his first sentence, so the sheets are the one thing he does not question. Turning a quarrel about what counts as the work into a quarrel about the paper is stance inflation.
Ilesanmi does not sort the differences into ones the verdict fits and ones it does not; the argument is that the same reading is wrong about all of them. This carries the verdict into the part Text 2 refuses it.
Correct. No fact is disputed; what Ilesanmi refuses is the yardstick, which identifies the work with the drawing and leaves the mason's decision nowhere.

Text 1

Marchetti's case for the prompt book is that it is the only document in the case that was in the room. The quarto was set by a compositor working from a manuscript nobody has seen, while the prompt book was marked in the wings with the actors in front of the person marking it. Where the two disagree about an entrance, Marchetti takes the prompt book, and holds that an editor should.

Text 2

Bourgeois grants that entirely and would pay for it. A prompt book is the record of one company's staging in one season, marked by somebody solving that night's problems, so an editor who follows it prints a performance rather than a play. Where a quarto's error is a compositor's, a prompt book's is a decision, and a decision is harder to see and harder to undo.

Based on the texts, how would Bourgeois (Text 2) most likely respond to Marchetti's argument in Text 1 that the prompt book was marked in the wings while the quarto was set from a manuscript nobody has seen?

Correct. Text 2 accepts the argument and attaches what accepting it costs, which the argument survives; the answer has to carry both halves.
Text 2 grants Marchetti's point about the wings in its first four words. This has Bourgeois pointing back the way Text 2 came from.
Both texts hold it and neither disputes it. A shared premise repeated is not a response to the argument built on it.
Text 2 grants that the prompt book is the document that was in the room and would use it with the cost declared. No and ever overshoot a text that is pricing something rather than banning it.

Why this answer

Bourgeois grants Marchetti's argument at once and adds the price of following it: a prompt book records one company's season, so its errors are decisions rather than slips, and an editor who prefers it prints a performance. The argument survives, which is what makes this an agreement with a cost attached.
