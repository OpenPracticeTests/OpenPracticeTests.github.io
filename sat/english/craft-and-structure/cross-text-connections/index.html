Cross-Text Connections

  • Craft and Structure
  • Cross-Text Connections
←PreviousAll Reading and Writing lessonsNext→

Each item gives you two texts on one subject. Answer the relationship the question asks about.

Key takeaways

  • Read the question first: it says which relationship you are being asked for.
  • State each author's position in your own words before you read the choices.
  • Match the strength of the response. A condition is not a refutation.
  • Where Text 2 grants a claim and adds to it, an answer that stops at the grant is only half the response.
  • An author can dispute a reading while accepting the observations behind it. That is disagreement about the reading, not about the facts.

What the SAT may ask

  • Say how Text 2's author would respond to a claim in Text 1.
  • Say how Text 2's author would characterize a claim, an account or a verdict in Text 1.
  • Name a statement both authors would agree with.

The rule

Answer the relationship the question asks about. Where it names a claim, judge against that claim rather than against the texts as wholes.

State each author's position in your own words, then say precisely how they differ:

Text 1's claim Text 2's claim The difference
the towpath suits every rider it suits confident riders Text 2 keeps the claim over part of the range Text 1 stated and withholds the rest

Five words cover most of what a second author does with a claim. Ask what survives.

Word What survives
reframe every fact stands, and only the word for them is refused
agree the claim survives whole, with an addition
narrow it survives over part of a range the claim stated
qualify it survives under a condition the claim never stated
disagree it does not survive

These describe the answer. They are not boxes it has to sit in.

A claim does not always state its range, so narrow and qualify can both fit one response.

An author can dispute a reading and still accept the observations behind it. That is disagreement about the reading. Where what is refused is the name for the facts, that is reframe.

Why the rule exists

A response can accept every fact and still object, so the strength of the response decides the answer, not the direction alone.

Choose the move

Two of the five words both fitEither will do. Take what the text supports
A choice is stronger than the responseStrike it: stance inflation

Worked examples

1. A condition the claim never stated

Text 1: brushing the dog on the porch before the walk adds five minutes to getting out the door, and it is worth the five minutes.

Text 2: five minutes on a forty-five-minute walk is a fair price, provided the brushing is what keeps the hair off the couch. Where the dog sheds the same either way, it is five minutes for nothing.

Task: name what Text 2 does to Text 1's claim that the five minutes are worth paying.

Evidence: Text 2 grants the estimate and calls it a fair price. Then it attaches a condition Text 1 never stated: provided the brushing is what keeps the hair off the couch.

Answer: the claim survives under a condition the claim never contemplated, so qualify.

Note: granting a time estimate is not endorsing what it was offered to support. A choice disputing the five minutes fails, because Text 2 grants them.

2. A mild caution is not a rejection

Text 1: leaving a phone on the charger all night ruins the battery. Text 2: suppose it does cost you something. A battery that ends the year holding a little less than it did is not a ruined battery, and that is the whole of the damage.

Task: name what Text 2 does to Text 1's claim that overnight charging ruins the battery.

Evidence: Text 2 does not deny a cost. It denies the size of the cost, and ruins is a word about size.

Answer: the claim does not survive, so disagree, at Text 2's own strength.

Closest trap: stance inflation. A choice reading no effect on the battery points the right way and overstates it, because Text 2 allows a cost.

3. Judge against the claim the question names

Text 1: I timed it for a month. The stairs take two minutes forty; the lift takes fifty seconds. That is nine hours a year on the stairs, so they save no time and I am going back to the lift.

Text 2: Going back to the lift would be a pity. The nine hours are real, and nobody takes the stairs to save time anyway. What the hours buy is the people from the other floors, and nobody meets anybody in a lift.

Task: name what Text 2 does to the claim about saving time.

Evidence: Text 2 grants it outright, in three words: the nine hours are real. Everything after that is about what the hours buy.

Answer: against that claim, agree, though the two reach opposite conclusions about which way to go up.

Note: the question names the time estimate, and Text 2 grants it. The decision Text 1 drew from the estimate is a different matter, and a choice built on that disagreement answers a question this stem did not ask.

4. What both authors would agree with

Text 1: Sunday is the branch's busiest day. The door count beats every other day, and Sunday opening should stay.

Text 2: The Sunday count is the highest of the week; nobody disputes it. But those are the readers who came on Saturday before Sunday opening began, and the money would do more in the van that reaches the villages.

Which statement would both authors most likely agree with?

Task: find a statement both texts support. No sentence is cited, so state each position and look for the overlap.

Evidence: Text 1 says the Sunday count beats every other day. Text 2 calls it the highest of the week and undisputed.

Answer: more readers come through the door on Sunday than on any other day.

Note: the two disagree about what to do, and the ask is not about that. Keeping Sunday opening has support in Text 1 only; moving the money, in Text 2 only.

Common traps

Strength, coverage, and the claim the stem names

Trap Correction
stance inflation Match Text 2's strength: a condition is not a refutation
overreach (must/all) Before accepting a never, all or must, check that the text reaches that strength
true-but-partial After the part Text 2 grants, ask what Text 2 adds
wrong direction Check who is conceding to whom, then reread Text 2's opening
on-topic-not-on-claim Reread the sentence the stem names, then reread the choice

The method: State each position, then answer the question asked

  1. State each author's position in your own words. Read Text 1 and say where it stands. Read Text 2 and do the same. Do it before you look at the choices.
  2. Answer the relationship the question asks about. If it names a claim, ask what survives of that claim. If it asks what both agree on, look for the claim both texts support.
  3. Where the question names a claim, judge against that claim and not the texts as wholes. Reread the sentence it points at. Two texts can differ overall while one of them grants that line.
  4. Check every part of the choice against the relevant text. Strike a choice stronger or milder than the text is, and a choice about a dispute neither text has. Kills: stance inflation, which is a mild position reported as a strong one.

Practice

Work each item with the method, untimed, until the method is automatic. The Reading and Writing section averages about 71 seconds a question, but that is an average across the section and not a deadline for any one item; save the clock for mixed practice later. Text 1

Curators who dim a gallery are not being timid. Nowicki points out that the fading light causes in dyed cloth is cumulative and permanent: every hour under a lamp spends a budget that can never be repaid. Against that, the discomfort of a dim room costs a visitor a single afternoon. It is a small price.

Text 2

Tarrant flatly disagrees. At the light levels Nowicki recommends, a visitor's eyes never fully adjust, and the stitching that makes the cloth worth keeping is not visible at all. The price is not one afternoon of squinting. The price is the object itself, perfectly preserved and permanently unseen.

Based on the texts, how would Tarrant (Text 2) most likely respond to Nowicki's claim in Text 1 that a dim gallery costs the visitor only a single afternoon of discomfort?

Trap: wrong direction. This has Tarrant pressing Nowicki's case further, but Text 2 opens by disagreeing, and nothing in it asks for less light.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Tarrant does not take the permanence up at all, and neither text argues about it. The question cites the sentence pricing the visitor's loss at one afternoon.
Correct. Text 2 answers that sentence by saying the price was misdescribed: at Nowicki's light levels the stitching is not visible, so the visitor loses the object rather than an afternoon of squinting.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Tarrant denies that this trade is cheap, not that damage could never justify dimming a room. Text 2 argues about one gallery's light levels and about nothing wider.

The method, applied

Step 1: Nowicki, dimming protects cheaply; Tarrant, dimming costs everything. Step 2: Text 2 opens with flatly disagrees and never softens, so the claim does not survive. Step 3: the question cites the sentence pricing the visitor's loss at one afternoon, and Tarrant prices it at the object nobody can see. Step 4: the choice left denies that the price is small.

Practice 2

  • Craft and Structure
  • Cross-Text Connections
  • Introductory

Text 1

Aurand argues that the backyard telescope has not been made obsolete, and that its advantage is time. A research instrument is booked by committee months ahead, and a target that wins an hour is lucky. An amateur can point at the same star every clear night for a year, and nobody rations that.

Text 2

Padilla agrees, and would add a second advantage Aurand leaves out. The observatory's long-focus reflector buys depth by narrowing its field, and sees a keyhole of sky beautifully. The amateur's short-focus refractor sweeps wide. When something changes where nobody had thought to look, the wide sweep is what finds it first.

Based on the texts, how would Padilla (Text 2) most likely respond to the argument Aurand makes in Text 1 that the amateur's real advantage is observing time nobody rations?

Correct. Text 2 opens with agreement and supplies a reason Aurand never gives: the observatory's long-focus reflector trades field for depth, and the wide sweep is what finds an unexpected change first.
Trap: wrong direction. Text 2's second word is agrees, and Padilla never weighs one rationed hour against a year of backyard nights.
Trap: true-but-partial. Aurand says it and Padilla does not contest it, so it is true. It is also only the half Padilla accepts, and it drops the second advantage.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Padilla says the wide sweep finds an unexpected change first, not that amateurs must carry all the coverage.

The method, applied

Step 1: Aurand, amateurs own observing time; Padilla, agreed, plus coverage. Step 2: Text 2 opens with agreement and adds a reason Aurand never gives, so the claim survives whole with an addition and the answer has to carry both halves. Step 3: the question cites the claim about unrationed observing time, which Padilla grants outright. Step 4: the choice left accepts the claim and adds the wide sweep.

Text 1

Brannigan reports that a five-cent charge per bag cut a city's bag use by more than half within a year, and insists the money is not the mechanism. Five cents changes no one's budget; anyone who wants a bag can still have one. What the charge does is make the cashier ask. A bag stops being what happens automatically and becomes something the shopper has to choose.

Text 2

Oyelaran does not doubt that a bag becomes a decision once it has a price, and reports the same drop Brannigan does. But a decision needs an alternative. Where shoppers drive, the reusable bags are already in the trunk, and the charge simply reminds them. Where shoppers arrive on foot with nothing in hand, the store is the only source of a bag, and the charge collects a nickel and changes almost nothing.

Based on the texts, how would Oyelaran (Text 2) most likely respond to Brannigan's account in Text 1 of why the charge works?

Trap: stance inflation. Text 2 reports the same drop Text 1 does. The charge that changes almost nothing is one setting inside Oyelaran's argument, not Oyelaran's verdict.
Trap: true-but-partial. Oyelaran reports the same drop, so this is granted. It is also what Oyelaran concedes before the argument starts, and it leaves out the condition that is the argument.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Brannigan never offers revenue as evidence and Oyelaran never argues about revenue. It is a real point about bag charges aimed at a claim Text 1 did not make.
Correct. Oyelaran grants the mechanism in the opening clause and attaches the condition it runs on: a price turns a bag into a decision only where a second option is within reach.

The method, applied

Step 1: Brannigan, decision, not money; Oyelaran, only with alternatives. Step 2: Oyelaran concedes the mechanism and the finding, then supplies a where, so the claim survives under a condition Brannigan never stated: qualify, not disagree. Step 3: the stem cites the mechanism claim, and Oyelaran grants it outright before adding that a decision needs a second option. Step 4: the flat rejection inflates a condition into a verdict, and Text 2 reports Brannigan's own drop. The choice left accepts the mechanism and names the condition.

Text 1

Solberg's case for handwriting does not rest on neatness or on tradition. It rests on speed, or rather on the lack of it. A hand cannot keep up with a lecture, so a student writing by hand has to decide, sentence by sentence, what is worth the ink. That deciding is the learning. A keyboard removes the constraint and, with it, the decision, which is why Solberg would keep handwriting in every grade.

Text 2

Ashworth would keep it too, in the first three grades, and for a reason Solberg never reaches: a child who has drawn a letter recognizes it faster in print afterward, because the hand teaches the eye what to look for. That is a beginner's dividend, and it is spent by about grade three. After that the lecture is real, the hand is still slow, and Ashworth cannot see what the slowness buys a sixteen-year-old.

Based on the texts, how would Ashworth (Text 2) most likely respond to the conclusion Solberg draws in Text 1 that handwriting earns its place at every grade level?

Trap: stance inflation. Ashworth's first sentence keeps handwriting in the first three grades and gives a reason for it, so a narrowing has become a removal.
Correct. Ashworth grants Solberg's conclusion through about grade three and withholds it afterward. A claim kept over part of its stated range and refused over the rest has been narrowed, and Solberg's stated range was every grade.
Trap: true-but-partial. Text 2 does say the hand teaches the eye, and it is why Ashworth keeps handwriting at all. It is still only the half Ashworth grants.
Trap: wrong direction. Text 2's last sentence cannot see what slowness buys a sixteen-year-old, so this carries the argument into the grades Ashworth refuses it.

The method, applied

Step 1: Solberg, slowness forces selection at every grade; Ashworth, keep it in the first three grades, for a different reason. Step 2: Ashworth keeps handwriting early because the hand teaches the eye, which is not Solberg's reason, and takes the conclusion back for older students. The claim survives over part of the range it stated: narrow. Step 3: the question cites the reach of Solberg's conclusion, every grade level, and the reach is what Ashworth cuts. Step 4: the choice left grants the early case on Ashworth's own grounds and withholds the rest.

  • Cross-Text Connections
  • Advanced

Text 1

Kettleborough's case is that the objection has run out of facts. The genome of the marsh's lost grazer has been read from museum skins; a related species carries the embryos to term; the animals released last spring have grazed the sedge exactly as the old accounts describe, and the marsh's flowering plants have answered within two seasons. Nothing about the outcome differs from the marsh of 1870. Kettleborough draws the obvious conclusion: this is restoration, the putting back of what was taken.

Text 2

Ostrander concedes the genome, the surrogate, the sedge, and the flowering plants; the work is exactly as good as Kettleborough says. The quarrel is with one word. To put a thing back is to return it, and nothing here has returned. These animals descend from no animal the marsh lost; they have learned none of what the lost herd knew; they enter a marsh that spent a century becoming something else. They are an addition, and a defensible one. Calling the addition restoration dresses a decision up as a repair.

Based on the texts, how would Ostrander (Text 2) most likely respond to Kettleborough's claim in Text 1 that releasing the animals puts back what was taken?

Trap: stance inflation. Text 2's opening line concedes the genome, the surrogate, the sedge and the plants, and calls the work exactly as good as Kettleborough says.
Trap: on-topic-not-on-claim. Kettleborough makes no claim about cost and Ostrander raises none. The stem points at the sentence calling the release a putting back.
Correct. Ostrander accepts every result and rejects one word. Animals descended from no animal the marsh lost, carrying none of the herd's learned behavior, are an addition rather than a return.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Ostrander refuses the word for this release, on this marsh, and calls the addition defensible. Never and any ... anywhere reach a strength Text 2 spends nowhere.

The method, applied

Step 1: Kettleborough, outcome equals restoration; Ostrander, outcome yes, word no. Step 2: Text 2 concedes the genome, the surrogate, the sedge and the plants, then says the quarrel is with one word. Every fact survives and only the word for them is refused, which is reframe. Step 3: the stem cites the sentence calling the release a putting back. To put a thing back is to return it, and animals with no ancestry among the lost are not returning. Step 4: disputing the results contradicts Text 2's opening line, and refusing the word for any revived population anywhere overshoots a text that refuses it for this release and calls the addition defensible.

Practice 6

  • Craft and Structure
  • Cross-Text Connections
  • Advanced

Text 1

Sabatelli declines the question as usually posed. Authorship is not a matter of who pressed anything; it is a matter of whose decisions survive into the finished work. A novelist's choice of a word survives; a printer's choice of press does not. Judged that way, most prompt-driven images are thin: the operator supplies a sentence, the system supplies everything it left open, and what reaches the image is mostly not the operator's. Sabatelli stops short of denying such an operator any authorship at all, and says only that the operator usually stands nearer to a client than to an author.

Text 2

Iwasaki accepts the test and thinks Sabatelli applies it one step too early. Count the decisions at the prompt and the operator is indeed thin. But the working operator Iwasaki describes generates three hundred images and keeps one, then generates three hundred more from that one. Every discard is a decision that survives in the image left standing. Iwasaki does not claim this of every operator, and says plainly that one prompt and one download makes a client. The test is right. The counting is early.

Based on the texts, how would Iwasaki (Text 2) most likely respond to Sabatelli's verdict in Text 1 that the operator of a prompt-driven system usually stands nearer to a client than to an author?

Trap: stance inflation. Iwasaki keeps Sabatelli's test and says plainly that one prompt and one download makes a client, so not every operator has become every operator.
Correct. Iwasaki accepts that authorship tracks the decisions surviving into the work and objects that Sabatelli counts them at the prompt. Discarding three hundred generations to keep one leaves a decision in the image left standing.
Trap: overreach (must/all). Sabatelli wrote mostly, usually and stops short, so this hands Text 1 a never it declined to write, and has Iwasaki agreeing besides.
Trap: true-but-partial. Text 2 concedes it in its second sentence, and that concession is the setup for Iwasaki's move rather than the move.

The method, applied

Step 1: Sabatelli, usually client, not author; Iwasaki, right test, early count. Step 2: both authors hedge, and Iwasaki keeps Sabatelli's test while handing the single-prompt case back. Whether you write narrow (granted for the operator who types once, withheld for the one who discards three hundred images) or qualify (the verdict holds on a condition Sabatelli never stated), you have written something measured. Step 3: the stem cites the verdict that the operator usually stands nearer a client, and Iwasaki's reply is that the prompt is not where the decisions are. Step 4: cut on strength. The choice left keeps the test and disputes the timing.
