Area and Volume

  • Geometry and Trigonometry
  • Area and volume
←PreviousAll Math lessonsNext→

Perimeter measures a boundary, area measures a flat region, and volume measures a solid. Decide first which of the three the question wants, then whether it wants the measure itself, a missing length, or the same measure in another unit.

Key takeaways

  • A perimeter adds lengths, an area multiplies two of them, a volume multiplies three.
  • Areas of pieces that do not overlap add, so split a region or subtract the piece removed.
  • A surface area is the sum of the exposed faces, and the reference sheet carries none of them.
  • For a missing length, put what you know into the formula, then divide out the rest or take the root.
  • Multiply every length by kkk: the perimeter is multiplied by kkk, the area by k2k^2k2, the volume by k3k^3k3.
  • Say whether a ratio counts lengths, areas, or volumes before you use it.
  • A radius is half a diameter, and a height meets the base at a right angle, so a slant is not a height.

What the SAT may ask

  • Find the area, the perimeter, or the volume of a figure built from two or more simple shapes.
  • Find what is left when one piece is removed from a region or a solid.
  • Recover a length from a stated perimeter, area, or volume.
  • Find the surface area of a box, a cube, or a cylinder.
  • Say what happens to a measure when every length is scaled by the same factor.
  • Restate an area or a volume in another unit.

The rule

A perimeter or a circumference is measured in plain units, an area in square units, and a volume in cubic units. The reference sheet our practice tests carry hands you these formulas, so none of them has to be memorized.

It carries no perimeter, no surface area, no trapezoid, and no prism whose base is not a rectangle. Build those from the shapes above.

A perimeter is the sides added, so a rectangle's is 2(ℓ+w)2(\ell + w)2(ℓ+w) and a square's is 4s4s4s.

Areas of pieces that do not overlap add. A region built from several shapes is a sum, and a region with a piece taken out is a difference. Volumes work the same way, and on a figure that allows both routes they reach the same number, which makes one a check on the other.

A boundary does not add like that. Where two pieces are joined, the edge they share is inside the figure and belongs to neither one's outside perimeter.

A surface area is the sum of the faces that are exposed. A closed box has six in three matching pairs, so its surface is 2(ℓw+ℓh+wh)2(\ell w + \ell h + wh)2(ℓw+ℓh+wh), and a box with no lid has five.

Where two solids are joined, whatever the two of them cover of each other is inside and is on neither surface.

To find a missing length, put what you know into the formula, then undo what is left. Divide out whatever multiplies the unknown. Where the unknown is squared or cubed, take the square or the cube root instead.

V=ℓwhV = \ell whV=ℓwh 96=8⋅3⋅h96 = 8 \cdot 3 \cdot h96=8⋅3⋅h 96=24h96 = 24h96=24h h=4h = 4h=4

Multiply every length of a figure by kkk and its perimeter is multiplied by kkk, its area by k2k^2k2, and its volume by k3k^3k3. The law needs every length multiplied by the same kkk, which is what it means for two figures to be similar.

The two cubes below are drawn at k=2k = 2k=2. One face of the larger holds four copies of the smaller cube's face, and the larger cube holds eight copies of the smaller cube.

Change only some of the lengths and there is no law to reach for: put the new dimensions into the formula. A rectangle's length appears once in A=ℓwA = \ell wA=ℓw, so multiplying the length by kkk and holding the width multiplies the area by kkk.

A circle has no second dimension to hold, and its radius appears twice in the area formula, so doubling the radius multiplies the area by 444. Double a cylinder's radius and triple its height and the volume is multiplied by 22⋅32^2 \cdot 322⋅3, which is 121212.

Before you use a ratio, say whether it counts lengths, areas, or volumes. Between two similar figures, an area ratio of 9:49:49:4 comes from a length ratio of 3:23:23:2, which goes with a volume ratio of 27:827:827:8. Take a root to move down to lengths; take a power to move up.

A percentage is a factor in disguise: every length up by 20%20\%20% means k=1.2k = 1.2k=1.2, so the area is multiplied by 1.441.441.44 and the volume by 1.7281.7281.728.

A unit change is a scale change. One yard is 333 feet, so one square yard is 999 square feet and one cubic yard is 272727 cubic feet. Where two lengths are stated in different units, convert one of them before you multiply anything.

Why the rule exists

An area is two lengths multiplied together, and a volume is three, whatever constant sits in front. Multiply each of those lengths by kkk and every factor picks up one kkk.

new area=(kℓ)(kw)\text{new area} = (k\ell)(kw)new area=(kℓ)(kw) new area=k2ℓw\text{new area} = k^2 \ell wnew area=k2ℓw new volume=(kℓ)(kw)(kh)\text{new volume} = (k\ell)(kw)(kh)new volume=(kℓ)(kw)(kh) new volume=k3ℓwh\text{new volume} = k^3 \ell whnew volume=k3ℓwh

The ℓw\ell wℓw and the ℓwh\ell whℓwh at the end of each line are the old measures. The factor is counted twice for an area and three times for a volume.

The count comes from how many lengths are multiplied together, not from which formula they sit in. It holds for a circle, a cone, and a shape the sheet has no formula for. A perimeter is a sum of lengths rather than a product, so it picks up one kkk and no more.

A unit change is that same count. A square yard is a square one yard on a side, which is 333 feet on a side, so it holds 333 rows of 333 square feet.

Splitting works because an area counts squares. Cutting a region into pieces that do not overlap neither creates nor destroys any of those squares, so the pieces add back to the whole. A boundary is not a count of squares, which is why a shared edge disappears from an outside perimeter while every square on both sides of it stays.

Worked examples

1. Find a perimeter where two pieces share an edge

A patio is laid from two rectangular slabs pushed together along a full edge. One slab is 777 feet by 444 feet and the other is 555 feet by 444 feet, and the two 444-foot edges are the ones that meet. What is the perimeter of the patio, in feet?

Question asks: the distance around the outside, not the area.

First move: see what the two slabs make together. Their 444-foot edges meet, so the patio is one rectangle, 121212 feet by 444 feet.

7+5=127 + 5 = 127+5=12 2(12+4)=322(12 + 4) = 322(12+4)=32

Answer: 323232 feet

Check: add the two slabs' own perimeters, which are 222222 and 181818 feet, then take off the shared edge, which each of them counted once.

22+18−4−4=3222 + 18 - 4 - 4 = 3222+18−4−4=32

Common mistake: adding the two perimeters and stopping, which gives 404040. That counts the joint twice, and the joint is inside the patio rather than around it.

2. Recover a height from a stated volume

A right circular cylinder has a volume of 176π176\pi176π cubic centimeters, and its base has a radius of 444 centimeters. What is the height of the cylinder, in centimeters?

Question asks: a length rather than a volume.

First move: take the cylinder's formula off the reference sheet and put in what the question states.

V=πr2hV = \pi r^2 hV=πr2h 176π=π(4)2h176\pi = \pi (4)^2 h176π=π(4)2h 176π=16πh176\pi = 16\pi h176π=16πh

Divide both sides by 16π16\pi16π.

h=11h = 11h=11

Answer: 111111 centimeters

Check: put 111111 back into the formula. It gives π(4)2(11)\pi (4)^2 (11)π(4)2(11), which is the volume the question states.

Common mistake: dividing 176π176\pi176π by 161616 rather than by 16π16\pi16π, which leaves 11π11\pi11π. Here π\piπ is a factor of both sides, so it cancels and the height is a plain number. That is a fact about this equation and not a rule about lengths: a circumference is a length and it carries π\piπ.

3. Add the faces of an open cylinder

A cylindrical tin is open at the top. Its base has a radius of 999 centimeters and the tin is 444 centimeters deep. What is the total area of the tin's curved side and its base, in square centimeters?

Question asks: an area, on a solid whose surface area the reference sheet does not carry.

First move: unfold the tin. Cut the curved side down a straight line and roll it flat, and it is a rectangle. Its height is the depth of the tin, and its width is the whole way round the circle, which is 2πr2\pi r2πr.

2π(9)(4)=72π2\pi (9)(4) = 72\pi2π(9)(4)=72π

Now the base, which is a whole circle.

π(9)2=81π\pi (9)^2 = 81\piπ(9)2=81π 72π+81π=153π72\pi + 81\pi = 153\pi72π+81π=153π

Answer: 153π153\pi153π square centimeters

Common mistake: adding a second circle, which gives 234π234\pi234π. A closed tin has two circular ends and this one has one, so the curved side is added to one circle here and to two on a closed tin.

4. Scale every length, then square and cube the factor

A model of a sculpture has a surface area of 787878 square inches and a volume of 454545 cubic inches. The finished sculpture has every length 444 times the matching length of the model. What are the surface area and the volume of the sculpture?

Question asks: two measures of the larger figure, from the same two measures of the smaller one.

First move: notice that the model's dimensions are never stated and are not needed. Every length is multiplied by 444, so the area carries that factor twice and the volume carries it three times.

42=164^2 = 1642=16 78⋅16=124878 \cdot 16 = 124878⋅16=1248 43=644^3 = 6443=64 45⋅64=288045 \cdot 64 = 288045⋅64=2880

Answer: 124812481248 square inches and 288028802880 cubic inches

Check: a length was scaled, so count the power. A surface is an area, which is two lengths multiplied, so the 444 is counted twice. A volume is three lengths, so it is counted three times. Two and three are the powers used.

Common mistake: multiplying both measures by 444, which gives 312312312 and 180180180. That treats a length factor as the factor for every measure.

5. Find a border's width from the border's area

A rectangular photograph measures 101010 inches by 666 inches. It is mounted on a rectangular board so that a border of uniform width shows on all four sides, and the border alone has an area of 808080 square inches. How wide is the border, in inches?

Question asks: a length, from an area.

First move: call the border's width xxx and build the board's two dimensions. The border runs along both ends of each side, so each dimension grows by xxx twice: the board is 10+2x10 + 2x10+2x by 6+2x6 + 2x6+2x.

The board's area is the photograph's 606060 plus the border's 808080.

(10+2x)(6+2x)=140(10 + 2x)(6 + 2x) = 140(10+2x)(6+2x)=140 4x2+32x+60=1404x^2 + 32x + 60 = 1404x2+32x+60=140 x2+8x−20=0x^2 + 8x - 20 = 0x2+8x−20=0 (x+10)(x−2)=0(x + 10)(x - 2) = 0(x+10)(x−2)=0

Answer: 222 inches

The equation has two roots, −10-10−10 and 222. A border cannot be −10-10−10 inches wide, so that root solves the equation and answers no question. Throw out any root a length cannot have.

Check: the board is 141414 by 101010, which is an area of 140140140, and taking the photograph's 606060 away leaves the 808080 the question states.

Common mistake: growing each dimension by xxx rather than by 2x2x2x. That gives (10+x)(6+x)=140(10 + x)(6 + x) = 140(10+x)(6+x)=140 and a width of 444, which is a border along two sides only.

Special cases

Shapes and measures the reference sheet does not carry

A trapezoid. Draw a diagonal. It cuts the trapezoid into two triangles: one stands on b1b_1b1​ and the other on b2b_2b2​. Both are hhh tall, where hhh is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel sides. Add them.

A=12b1h+12b2hA = \frac{1}{2}b_1 h + \frac{1}{2}b_2 hA=21​b1​h+21​b2​h A=12(b1+b2)hA = \frac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)hA=21​(b1​+b2​)h

This works on every trapezoid, including one whose slanted side leans out past the longer base.

A surface area. Unfold the solid and add the pieces. A closed box gives six rectangles in three matching pairs, so its surface is 2(ℓw+ℓh+wh)2(\ell w + \ell h + wh)2(ℓw+ℓh+wh), and a cube gives six equal squares.

A cylinder gives two circles and one rectangle. Cut the curved side down a straight line and roll it flat: its height is hhh and its width is the circumference, so the curved part is 2πrh2\pi r h2πrh. Add 2πr22\pi r^22πr2 when both ends are there, and πr2\pi r^2πr2 when only one is.

A prism whose base is not a rectangle. A prism's volume is V=BHV = BHV=BH, where BBB is the area of the end face and HHH is how far the prism runs. Work out BBB with that shape's own formula first.

In a trough whose ends are trapezoids, the hhh inside BBB is the trapezoid's own height, which is not the length of the trough. The sheet's V=ℓwhV = \ell whV=ℓwh is this rule for a rectangular end, and its V=πr2hV = \pi r^2 hV=πr2h is the same idea for a circular one.

A height nobody gave you. A right circular cone's slant height runs along its surface, and the height in the volume formula runs straight up from the center of the base to the point. Those two and the base radius form a right triangle, so the Pythagorean theorem recovers whichever one is missing.

The Right Triangles and Trigonometry lesson is where that theorem is taught.

Common traps

Where a correct formula still gives a wrong number

Every formula names its letters and states its constants. Read what each number measures before it goes anywhere, and read the formula back before you multiply.

Trap Quick check
A stated length put in the wrong letter Halve a diameter; take the height that meets the base at a right angle; subtract the other part's height
The 12\frac{1}{2}21​, the 13\frac{1}{3}31​, or a doubling left out Read the formula back and count its constants
A length factor used as an area or a volume factor Square it for an area, cube it for a volume
An inside edge counted in an outside perimeter Trace the boundary; a shared edge is not on it
A part reported when the question asked for the whole, or the reverse Re-read the last line of the question; what is left over is smaller than what you started with

About a quarter of the real Math section is typed rather than chosen. On a typed question there is no wrong choice to catch a number that went into the wrong slot. The reading has to be right the first time.

The method: Ask, sort, match, answer

  1. Name what the question asks for, and the unit it has to come back in. A boundary comes back in plain units, an area in square units, a volume in cubic units. Convert any length given in a second unit now, before anything is multiplied.
  2. Sort the picture into shapes the reference sheet carries. A region is either a sum of rectangles, triangles and circles or a whole shape with a piece taken out. Where every side runs horizontally or vertically, the sides running each way add to the same total. So one unlabeled side is what the labeled ones leave over.
  3. Match each number to the letter its formula names, then work one shape to a line. Take the height that meets the base at a right angle, never a slanted edge. Where a length is the unknown, put the known numbers in first, then divide out everything around it, or take a root if it carries a power.
  4. Answer what was asked. When a length was scaled or a unit was converted, count the power that factor carries: once for a length, twice for an area, three times for a volume.

Set up by hand, then check with the calculator

A graphing calculator is built into the testing app and is allowed on every Math question.

Write the formula and match each number to its letter first. Typing a product before that is done is how a stated diameter goes in where a radius belongs.

The calculator earns its keep on a cube root and on a conversion factor that has to be squared or cubed. It earns it again on a check: put a length you found back into the formula it came from and see whether the stated measure comes out.

Practice

Work each item with the method, under a rough clock: about a minute for a bare equation, and up to about two minutes once there is a context to translate or a graph or table to read first. The Math section gives you about 95 seconds a question on average, so that range is test-day pace rather than a drill speed. Press Show solution to see the steps, one to a line.
