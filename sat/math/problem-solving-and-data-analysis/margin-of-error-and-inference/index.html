Margin of Error and Inference

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Inference from sample statistics and margin of error
←PreviousAll Math lessonsNext→

A sample gives one number, and a margin of error turns it into a range of plausible values for the whole group. Decide first which group the sample was drawn from, because the range is a claim about that group and no other.

Key takeaways

  • The plausible range runs from the sample statistic minus the margin of error to that statistic plus it.
  • The statistic sits at the center, the range is twice the margin wide, and both ends belong to it.
  • A margin in percentage points is subtracted from the percent, never taken as a percent of it.
  • The range is a plausible range, not a promise: the population's value can sit outside it.
  • The estimate describes the group the sample was drawn from, and no other group.
  • For random samples drawn the same way from one population, a larger sample generally gives a smaller margin of error.
  • Two ranges that share no value support a claim that the groups differ. Two that overlap leave the question open.

What the SAT may ask

  • Build the range of plausible values from a sample statistic and its margin of error.
  • Report the smallest or the largest value the estimate makes plausible.
  • Read the sample statistic and the margin of error back out of a stated range.
  • Say which group an estimate describes, or which sample supports a claim about a given group.
  • Build two groups' ranges and report the values they share, or that they share none.
  • Say what a larger sample does to the margin of error.

The rule

A sample statistic is a number measured on part of a group. The population is the whole group it came from. A margin of error says how far the population's value might plausibly sit from the sample's.

Subtract the margin of error for the low end, and add it for the high end.

low end=sample statistic−margin of error\text{low end} = \text{sample statistic} - \text{margin of error}low end=sample statistic−margin of error high end=sample statistic+margin of error\text{high end} = \text{sample statistic} + \text{margin of error}high end=sample statistic+margin of error

A reading club has 2,0002{,}0002,000 members. A survey of 350350350 of them, chosen at random, found that 42%42\%42% had finished the current book, with a margin of error of 666 percentage points.

42−6=3642 - 6 = 3642−6=36 42+6=4842 + 6 = 4842+6=48

So 36%36\%36% to 48%48\%48% is the plausible range for all 2,0002{,}0002,000 members. Both ends belong to the range, so 36%36\%36% and 48%48\%48% are themselves plausible values.

The statistic sits at the center, and the range is twice the margin of error wide. Given a range instead, the value in the middle is the statistic and half the width is the margin.

A margin of error in percentage points is subtracted from the percent, not taken as a percent of it. Here it moves the estimate by 666 points, not by 6%6\%6% of 424242.

The range is a claim about the population, not about the sample. The 350350350 members who answered were counted, so their 42%42\%42% is known and needs no range around it.

A percent range becomes a count range on the population's size. Take each end of 36%36\%36% to 48%48\%48% on all 2,0002{,}0002,000 members:

0.36×2,000=7200.36 \times 2{,}000 = 7200.36×2,000=720 0.48×2,000=9600.48 \times 2{,}000 = 9600.48×2,000=960

So between 720720720 and 960960960 members plausibly finished the book, with the sample's own estimate at 840840840.

A plausible range is not a promise. It collects the values the sample supports, and the club's real figure can still sit outside it.

Why the rule exists

A sample is only part of the group, so a different sample can give a different number. The margin of error is how far the sample's number is likely to sit from the group's.

That is why the range is about the population. The sample's people were counted, so their number is already fixed; the number nobody measured is the group's.

For random samples drawn the same way, a larger sample generally gives a smaller margin of error, because more answers pin the group's number down more tightly. The size of the group behind those answers is not what a bigger survey changes.

How sure the report wants to be, and how widely the answers vary, move the margin of error too.

Worked examples

1. Build the plausible range from a sample mean

A tea packer filled 5,0005{,}0005,000 tins on one run. An inspector weighed 808080 tins chosen at random and found a mean weight of 246246246 grams, with a margin of error of 444 grams.

What is the plausible range for the mean weight of all 5,0005{,}0005,000 tins?

Question asks: a plausible range for the population mean.

First move: subtract the margin of error, then add it.

246−4=242246 - 4 = 242246−4=242 246+4=250246 + 4 = 250246+4=250

Answer: 242242242 grams to 250250250 grams

Common mistake: reading the range as a statement about the 808080 tins on the scale. Those tins were weighed, so their mean is 246246246 grams and nothing about it is estimated.

2. Decide whether a value is plausible

A state park sold 6,0006{,}0006,000 camping permits last season. A ranger surveyed 320320320 permit holders chosen at random and found that 60%60\%60% had camped at the park before, with a margin of error of 555 percentage points.

Is 55%55\%55% a plausible value for the percent of all 6,0006{,}0006,000 permit holders who had camped there before? Is 54%54\%54%?

Question asks: whether each value lies in the plausible range.

First move: build the range.

60−5=5560 - 5 = 5560−5=55 60+5=6560 + 5 = 6560+5=65

The range runs from 55%55\%55% to 65%65\%65%.

Check: values were tested against the range, so read both ends. Both ends belong to it, so 55%55\%55% is inside it and 54%54\%54% is below it.

Answer: 55%55\%55% is plausible, and 54%54\%54% is not

Common mistake: treating the ends as excluded, which rules out 55%55\%55% and 65%65\%65% although the estimate reaches both.

3. Compare two groups by their ranges

A company surveyed a random sample of the workers at each of its two warehouses and asked whether they had finished the new safety course.

Warehouse Percent who finished Margin of error (points)
North 46 5
South 52 4

Which percents are plausible for both warehouses, and what does that settle?

Question asks: the values the two ranges share.

First move: build both ranges.

46−5=4146 - 5 = 4146−5=41 46+5=5146 + 5 = 5146+5=51 52−4=4852 - 4 = 4852−4=48 52+4=5652 + 4 = 5652+4=56

North's range is 41%41\%41% to 51%51\%51% and South's is 48%48\%48% to 56%56\%56%.

Check: two ranges were compared, so read both ends. They share every value from 48%48\%48% to 51%51\%51%.

Answer: every percent from 48%48\%48% to 51%51\%51%, and the overlap settles nothing

A figure in that shared band is plausible for both warehouses. Overlapping ranges leave the question open: they do not show the warehouses are level, and they do not show the warehouses differ. Ranges that share no value would support a difference, and these two overlap, so this comparison settles nothing on its own.

Common mistake: comparing 46%46\%46% with 52%52\%52% and stopping there. Two samples of one group can differ, and a gap between two sample percents is not by itself a gap between the groups.

Special cases

What a margin of error does not tell you

It does not say where the population's value is. The range collects the values the sample supports. The real figure can sit outside it, and a survey that reports one is not promising otherwise.

It does not describe the sample. The people who answered were counted, so their result is observed rather than estimated.

It does not repair a sample drawn from the wrong group. A survey of one part of a population estimates that part, however many people answer and however small the margin gets.

It does not cover a leading question or a group that declined to answer. A margin of error measures how far random samples of the same size scatter, and nothing else.

Common traps

The group and the ends

The range belongs to the population the sample was drawn from, and it includes both of its ends.

Trap Quick check
Reading the range as a claim about the sample The people who answered were counted, so their result needs no range
Reading the range as a promise about the population The population's value can sit outside it
Taking the margin as a percent of the estimate Percentage points are subtracted from the percent itself
Reading two overlapping ranges as settling the question Overlap leaves it open, in both directions
Letting the population's size decide the margin The sample's size moves the margin; the population's size does not

The method: Ask, statistic, range, check

  1. Name the ask. A range, one end of it, the sample statistic, the margin of error, a group, a sample, or a comparison. Everything after this step changes with it.
  2. Read the sample statistic, the margin of error, and the group the sample was drawn from. The estimate is a claim about that group and no other, however large the sample is.
  3. Build the range, or read it back. Subtract the margin of error for the low end and add it for the high end. Given a range instead, the value in the middle is the statistic and half the width is the margin.
  4. Answer the ask. When you tested whether a value lies in the range, or whether two ranges meet, check both ends. A value at an end is inside the range, and two ranges that meet at a single value still meet. Skip the check when nothing was tested against a range.

Faster by hand

A graphing calculator is built into the testing app and is allowed on every Math question.

Building a range is one subtraction and one addition, so setting up the calculator costs more time than it saves.

It saves time when several ranges have to be compared. Work out every low end and every high end first, then compare the numbers rather than the wording.

A music festival sold 12,00012{,}00012,000 tickets. A survey of 300300300 ticket holders, chosen at random, found that 75%75\%75% had traveled from outside the state, with a margin of error of 444 percentage points. According to this estimate, what is the smallest plausible value for the percent of all 12,00012{,}00012,000 ticket holders who traveled from outside the state?

Correct. Subtracting the margin of error from the sample percent gives 71%71\%71%, the low end of the plausible range.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is one end of the plausible range, the smallest value in it.

Step 2. The sample percent is 75%75\%75%, the margin of error is 444 percentage points, and the sample was drawn from the festival's ticket holders.

Step 3. Subtract the margin of error for the low end.

75−4=7175 - 4 = 7175−4=71

Step 4. The smallest plausible value is 71%71\%71%.

  • Introductory

An airport served 48,00048{,}00048,000 travelers last week. A surveyor questioned 200200200 of last week's travelers departing on international flights, chosen at random, and asked each whether they had checked a bag. The result and its margin of error give a plausible range. That range is an estimate for which group?

Correct. The sample was drawn at random from that week's international departures at that airport, so that is the group the range estimates.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is a group.

Step 2. The sample was drawn at random from that week's international departures at that airport, so the estimate is a claim about them. It reaches no other week and no other airport.

Step 3. No range has to be built. The group settles the answer on its own.

Step 4. The range estimates all travelers who departed on an international flight from the airport that week.

A transit agency surveyed a random sample of its bus riders. Its report gives 44%44\%44% to 56%56\%56% as the range of plausible values for the percent of all the agency's riders who board at a stop with a shelter. Which result did the agency's sample give?

Correct. The value in the middle of 44%44\%44% to 56%56\%56% is 50%50\%50%, and half the range's width of 121212 points is 666 points.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is the sample statistic and the margin of error, read back out of a stated range.

Step 2. The range runs from 44%44\%44% to 56%56\%56%, and one sample of the agency's bus riders produced it.

Step 3. The sample percent sits in the middle of the range, and the margin of error is half the width.

44+562=50\frac{44 + 56}{2} = 50244+56​=50 56−442=6\frac{56 - 44}{2} = 6256−44​=6

Step 4. The sample gave 50%50\%50% with a margin of error of 666 percentage points.

Practice 4

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

  • Standard

Four inspection teams each measured a random sample of washers from the same batch and reported the mean outer diameter, in millimeters, with a margin of error.

Team Mean diameter (mm) Margin of error (mm)
Team 1 48.6 0.6
Team 2 48.1 0.3
Team 3 48.3 0.9
Team 4 47.6 0.4

Whose report gives the narrowest range of plausible values for the mean diameter of all the washers in the batch?

Correct. Team 2's margin of error of 0.30.30.3 millimeters is the smallest of the four, so its range, 47.847.847.8 to 48.448.448.4 millimeters, is the narrowest.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is which of four ranges is narrowest.

Step 2. Every row reports a mean and its own margin of error, and every row estimates the same group, the whole batch.

Step 3. A range is twice its margin of error wide, so the smallest margin gives the narrowest range. The four margins are 0.60.60.6, 0.30.30.3, 0.90.90.9 and 0.40.40.4 millimeters, and the reported means play no part in the width.

Step 4. Team 2 gives the narrowest range.

Practice 5

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

  • Standard

A town council wants to estimate the percent of all 24,00024{,}00024,000 adult residents who are registered with the town's carpool program. Which sample would best support an estimate for all the town's adult residents?

Correct. Every adult resident has a chance of being chosen, so the estimate covers all 24,00024{,}00024,000 of them.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is a sample, and the group it has to support a claim about is all 24,00024{,}00024,000 adult residents.

Step 2. An estimate describes the group the sample was drawn from. All four samples are chosen at random and all four are the same size, so what separates them is the group behind the choosing.

Step 3. Only the town's list of all adult residents holds every adult in the town, so only a sample drawn from it estimates all of them.

Step 4. The sample drawn at random from the town's list of all adult residents is the one that supports the estimate.

Practice 6

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Inference from sample statistics and margin of error
  • Advanced

A company surveyed 275275275 of its 3,0003{,}0003,000 employees, chosen at random, and found that 64%64\%64% ride the company shuttle, with a margin of error of 777 percentage points. The company will now repeat the survey with 1,1001{,}1001,100 employees, chosen at random by the same method. Which statement about the second survey is best supported?

Correct. A larger random sample tends to give a smaller margin of error, so the second survey's range is likely to be narrower than the first's.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is a statement about a survey that has not been taken.

Step 2. The first survey gives 64%64\%64% with a margin of error of 777 percentage points, from a sample of the company's employees. The second draws 1,1001{,}1001,100 employees from that same group.

Step 3. No range can be built for the second survey, because its percent is not known yet. What is known is that a larger random sample drawn the same way generally gives a smaller margin of error, and 1,1001{,}1001,100 is four times 275275275.

Step 4. The second survey's margin of error is likely to be smaller than 777 percentage points.

Practice 7

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Inference from sample statistics and margin of error
  • Advanced

A public radio station surveyed 175175175 of its listeners, chosen at random, and found that 72%72\%72% listen on a weekday morning. The station wants the survey to support the claim that at least 50%50\%50% of all its listeners do, which needs the whole plausible range to sit at 50%50\%50% or above. If the margin of error is a whole number of percentage points, what is the greatest value it could be?

Correct. A margin of error of 222222 percentage points puts the low end at 50%50\%50%, which is the lowest the claim allows.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is the greatest margin of error the claim allows.

Step 2. The sample percent is 72%72\%72% and the group is all the station's listeners. The claim needs the low end of the range to be 50%50\%50% or above.

Step 3. The low end is the sample percent minus the margin of error, so the margin can grow until the low end lands on 50%50\%50%.

72−50=2272 - 50 = 2272−50=22

Step 4. A value was tested against the range, so read both ends. A low end of exactly 50%50\%50% belongs to the range and meets a claim of at least 50%50\%50%. The greatest margin of error is 222222 percentage points.

Practice 8

Four schools in one district each surveyed a random sample of their own students and asked whether they walk to school.

School Percent who walk Margin of error (points)
Kemp 54 5
Lang 61 4
Moss 44 6
Pike 46 3

For which of the following pairs of schools do the two plausible ranges share no value?

Correct. Lang's range is 575757 to 656565 and Moss's is 383838 to 505050, so the two share no value at all.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is which offered pair of ranges shares no value.

Step 2. Each school surveyed its own students, so each row estimates its own school.

Step 3. Build the two ends that decide each pair: the higher school's low end and the lower school's high end. For Lang and Moss those are:

61−4=5761 - 4 = 5761−4=57 44+6=5044 + 6 = 5044+6=50

Step 4. Two ranges were compared, so read both ends. Lang's range reaches down only to 575757 and Moss's reaches up only to 505050. Of the pairs offered, Lang and Moss is the one whose ranges share no value.
