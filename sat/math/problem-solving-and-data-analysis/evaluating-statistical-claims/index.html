Evaluating Statistical Claims

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Evaluating statistical claims: observational studies and experiments
←PreviousAll Math lessonsNext→

Two questions decide what a study may claim: how the participants were picked, and how they were sorted into groups. Answer both before you judge any conclusion.

Key takeaways

  • Random selection decides who a result covers. Random assignment decides whether it can support a cause.
  • In an experiment the researcher assigns the treatment. In an observational study the researcher records what participants were already doing.
  • On its own, an observational study supports a link, never a cause.
  • A random sample speaks for the group it was drawn from, and no further.
  • Asking more people does not make a group that chose itself stand for everyone.
  • A study can use both randomizations, one, or neither. Check for each one separately.

What the SAT may ask

  • Say which randomizations a described study used.
  • Name the group a result covers.
  • Decide whether a result supports a cause or only a link.
  • Choose the design that answers a stated question.
  • Name the change that would let a claim stand.
  • Judge a reported claim against the study behind it.

The rule

Two randomizations can appear in a study, and they do different jobs.

Random selection means the participants were drawn at random from a named list. It decides who the result covers.

Random assignment means the researcher put each participant into one of the groups at random. It decides whether a difference between the groups can be read as caused by the treatment.

A study in which the researcher assigns the treatment or condition is an experiment. A study that records what the participants were already doing is an observational study, and its groups may be drawn up during the analysis rather than existing beforehand.

A hotel is testing whether a new check-in greeting shortens the wait at its front desk. It has 909090 front-desk staff.

Draw 404040 of the 909090 at random and then assign each one a greeting at random, and both randomizations are in place. A shorter wait in the new-greeting group can be read as caused by the greeting, and the finding reaches all 909090 staff.

Ask for volunteers instead and then assign the greeting at random, and only the assignment is random. The causal reading survives, for the volunteers.

Participants Groups formed at random Groups not formed at random
Drawn at random Can support a cause, for the list Can support a link, for the list
Chose to take part Can support a cause, for them Can support a link, for them

Each cell says what the design permits, not what it proves. Chance alone moves results, so even a well-run experiment supports a causal reading rather than settling it.

On its own, an observational study supports a link and not a cause.

How close a sample estimate sits to the whole group, and what a margin of error means, belong to the topic on inference from sample statistics.

Why the rule exists

Random selection works because every member of the list has the same chance of being picked, which reduces systematic selection bias. It does not remove ordinary variation: two random samples of one list can give two different numbers.

Random assignment works because chance, not any feature of the participants, decides who goes where. Across everything measured and everything nobody thought to measure, the two groups tend to start out alike, so a difference afterward points to the treatment.

When people choose their own group, the groups can differ before the treatment starts. A confounding variable differs between the groups and also moves the outcome. Shoppers who use an online order form may already shop in ways that raise what they spend.

A later difference then has more than one explanation, and the study cannot say which one is right.

Worked examples

1. Read what a random sample without assignment supports

A food co-op has 2,5002{,}5002,500 members. A researcher drew 250250250 of them at random from the membership list and recorded what each one spent on a visit. Members who use the co-op's online order form spent more per visit than members who do not.

What does this result support?

Question asks: what the result supports, and for which group.

First move: find the two randomizations before judging anything.

Work: the 250250250 were drawn at random from the membership list, so the result covers all 2,5002{,}5002,500 members. Nobody was assigned the order form. Members decided for themselves whether to use it, so the two groups were already formed when the researcher arrived.

Answer: among the co-op's 2,5002{,}5002,500 members, using the online order form goes with a higher spend per visit.

The link reaches the whole membership because the draw was random, and it stays a link because the groups were not.

Common mistake: reporting that the order form raises what members spend. Members who order online may shop differently in other ways as well, and nothing in this study separates the form from the people who chose it.

2. Name the change that would support a causal claim

An auto repair shop has 242424 mechanics, and each one decided whether to use the shop's new parts checklist. The mechanics who use it finish a service faster than the mechanics who do not. The shop wants to say that the checklist speeds up a service.

What one change to the study would let the shop say that?

Question asks: the change that would support a causal claim.

First move: find which of the two randomizations is missing.

Work: the claim covers the shop's own mechanics, and all 242424 are in the study. There is no larger list to draw from, so a random draw has nothing to do. The groups, though, were formed by the mechanics themselves, and a mechanic who reaches for a checklist may already work faster.

Answer: assign each mechanic at random to use the checklist or not, then compare the service times.

Check: the answer proposes the design that would support a cause, so the study has to contain words that put the mechanics into their groups at random. After this change it does, and before it there were none.

Common mistake: drawing a random sample of the 242424 mechanics. A random draw decides who a result covers, and this claim already covers everybody in the shop.

3. Choose the sampling plan that answers a share question

A dance studio has 1,8001{,}8001,800 registered students and needs the share of them who take more than one class a week. Two plans are on the table.

Plan 1: post the question on the studio's message board and count the replies.

Plan 2: draw 200200200 students at random from the registration list and ask each one.

Which plan answers the question?

Question asks: which plan answers a question about a share.

First move: name what the question needs. It is about all 1,8001{,}8001,800 students and says nothing about a cause, so it needs a random draw and no assignment at all.

Work: the first plan lets students decide whether to reply. The students who come most often are the likeliest to see the board and answer, so the replies describe the people who answered. The second plan draws from the registration list at random, so every registered student has the same chance of being asked.

Answer: the second plan.

Common mistake: picking the first plan because it can reach more people. Replies from students who chose to reply are a voluntary response, and more of them describe the same self-chosen group.

Special cases

When assignment is impossible, and what a bigger sample cannot fix

Some things cannot be assigned. No researcher can give a person a hometown, an age, or a family background. A study of those has to be observational, so its results stay links however carefully it is run.

A bigger group does not repair a group that chose itself. If the people who agreed to take part differ from the people who did not, adding more of the same kind of volunteer keeps that difference.

A random draw can still miss. The list drawn from may leave out part of the group being asked about. The people who answer may differ from the people who do not. Either one can bias the result however good the draw was, and a bigger sample corrects neither.

Common traps

The two randomizations

A study can carry one randomization without the other, so look for each one separately.

Trap Quick check
Reading chosen at random as random assignment Selection is about who took part; assignment is about which group they landed in
Reading a difference between groups people joined themselves as a cause Ask who decided which group each person was in
Taking random assignment as permission to speak for everyone Ask which list the participants were drawn from
Answering that a bigger group fixes a group that chose itself Size does not change who agreed to take part
Ruling out a cause because the participants were volunteers Volunteers plus random assignment can still support a cause, for the volunteers

The method: Claim, selection, assignment, check

  1. Read the claim. Say what it asserts: that two things go together, or that one causes the other. Then say which group it covers.
  2. Find how the participants were picked. Drawn at random from a named list, or chose to take part. A random draw is what lets a result cover the whole list. Any other way of picking covers the participants and nobody else.
  3. Find how the groups were formed. Assigned at random by the researcher, assigned some other way, or set by what the participants were already doing. Only random assignment lets a difference between the groups be read as caused by the treatment.
  4. Answer the ask. Whenever your answer states a cause, or proposes the design that would support one, point to the words that put the participants into their groups at random. Without those words the answer is a link and nothing more.

Spend the time on two sentences

A graphing calculator is built into the testing app and is allowed on every Math question.

Nothing on this page is computed, so the calculator has no job here. The questions state every number and carry it unchanged. Read the two sentences of the stem that say how the participants were picked and how the groups were formed, and read them twice.

Practice 1

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Evaluating statistical claims: observational studies and experiments
  • Introductory

A language school studied two homework schedules. 606060 of its students volunteered to take part. Each volunteer was then assigned at random to one of the two schedules. Which randomization did the school use?

Correct. The sentence saying each volunteer was assigned at random to one of the two schedules is random assignment, and it is the only randomization the school ran.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is which randomizations the study used, so there is no claim to judge.

Step 2. The students volunteered. Nobody was drawn at random from a list, so the study has no random selection.

Step 3. Each volunteer was assigned at random to one of the two schedules, so the study has random assignment.

Step 4. The answer describes the study rather than stating a cause. The school used random assignment of the students to the two schedules.

A zoo's membership program has 9,0009{,}0009,000 members. The zoo picked 300300300 members at random from the program's membership list and asked each one whether they had visited on a weekday in the past month. 414141 percent said yes. Which is the largest group that estimate describes?

Correct. The 300300300 were drawn at random from the program's membership list, so the estimate covers everyone on that list and nobody past it.

The method, applied

Step 1. The claim is a share, and the ask is the largest group it covers.

Step 2. The 300300300 were drawn at random from the program's membership list, so the result reaches everyone on that list and stops there.

Step 3. No groups were formed and nothing was assigned, so no cause is in question.

Step 4. The answer names a group rather than stating a cause. The largest group the estimate describes is all 9,0009{,}0009,000 members of the program.

Practice 3

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Evaluating statistical claims: observational studies and experiments
  • Standard

A shoe factory studied two stitching stations. 909090 workers at one plant took part, and no worker from the company's other plants was included. 505050 of them worked at the new station and 404040 at the old one. Each worker decided which station to use. Over four weeks the workers at the new station finished more pairs per hour, and the factory concluded that the new station is what made those 909090 workers faster.

Which feature of the study is the reason that conclusion is not supported?

Correct. The workers formed the groups themselves, so the two groups could already have differed in speed before the study began, and the station is not the only explanation left.

The method, applied

Step 1. The claim is that the new station causes faster stitching, and it covers the 909090 workers in the study.

Step 2. The workers all came from one plant rather than being drawn at random from the company, which limits who the result covers. The claim reaches no further than those 909090 workers, so that limit is not the problem here.

Step 3. Nobody assigned the stations. Each worker chose, so the faster workers may have gathered at one station before the study started.

Step 4. The answer names why no cause stands, so there are no random-assignment words to point to. The reason is that each worker decided which station to use.

Practice 4

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

  • Standard

A trainer studied two class formats. 120120120 dog owners volunteered to take part. Each dog was assigned at random to one of the two formats, and after six weeks the dogs in the new format pulled on the leash less often than the dogs in the old one, by more than chance alone would be likely to produce.

Which conclusion does this study support?

Correct. The dogs were put into the two formats at random, which balances everything else on average, and the trainer found the gap unlikely to come from chance, so the difference between the two formats can be read as caused.

The method, applied

Step 1. The claim on offer is about the new format and pulling, and the group is the dogs in the study.

Step 2. The owners volunteered, so no dog was drawn at random from a list. The result covers those dogs and no wider group.

Step 3. Each dog was assigned at random to a format, so a difference between the two groups can be read as caused by the format, and the trainer found the gap unlikely to come from chance.

Step 4. The answer states a cause, so point to the words that put the participants into their groups at random. The stem says each dog was assigned at random to one of the two formats. Among the dogs in the study, the new format led to less pulling than the old format.

Practice 5

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

  • Standard

A stationery company asked for volunteers among its staff, and 808080 people took part. Each volunteer was assigned at random to one of two ways of labeling boxes, and the group using the new way mislabeled fewer boxes. The company wants to report that the new way cuts mislabeling for all 2,0002{,}0002,000 of its staff.

Which one change to the study would let the company report that?

Correct. A random draw from the full staff list is what makes a result cover the whole list, and the random assignment already in the study keeps the cause.

The method, applied

Step 1. The claim says the new way cuts mislabeling, and it covers all 2,0002{,}0002,000 staff.

Step 2. The 808080 took part because they volunteered, so the result reaches those 808080 and no further. This is the half the claim fails on.

Step 3. The volunteers were assigned at random, so a causal reading is already available for them.

Step 4. The answer proposes the design that would support a cause, so point to the words that put the participants into their groups at random. The stem says each volunteer was assigned at random to one of two ways of labeling boxes. What is missing is the draw, so the change is to draw the participants at random from the company's list of all 2,0002{,}0002,000 staff.

Practice 6

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis

  • Advanced

A supermarket chain employs 4,0004{,}0004,000 people. It wants to be able to say that its new scheduling app reduces missed shifts, and to say it about all 4,0004{,}0004,000 employees. Which study design supports that?

Correct. The random draw makes the result cover all 4,0004{,}0004,000 employees, and the random assignment lets the difference between the two groups be read as caused by the app.

The method, applied

Step 1. The claim names a cause, the app reducing missed shifts, and it covers all 4,0004{,}0004,000 employees. It needs both randomizations.

Step 2. Covering all 4,0004{,}0004,000 needs the participants drawn at random from the whole staff list, which rules out any design built on volunteers or on app users.

Step 3. Calling the reduction a cause needs the two groups formed at random, which rules out any design that compares employees who chose for themselves.

Step 4. The answer proposes the design that would support a cause, so point to the words that put the participants into their groups at random. One design has them: draw 250250250 employees at random from all 4,0004{,}0004,000, then assign each of them at random to use the app or not.

Practice 7

  • Problem-Solving and Data Analysis
  • Evaluating statistical claims: observational studies and experiments
  • Advanced

Two studies looked at treated lettuce seed.

The first study: a seed supplier drew 500500500 growers at random from its customer list and compared the yields reported by growers who buy treated seed with the yields reported by growers who do not. The growers buying treated seed reported the higher yield.

The second study: 200200200 growers volunteered, each was assigned at random to treated or untreated seed, and the treated group had the higher yield.

Which conclusion is supported by the first study but not by the second?

Correct. The first study drew its 500500500 growers at random from the customer list, so its link covers that list, and the second study's volunteers were drawn from no list.

The method, applied

Step 1. The ask is a conclusion the first study supports and the second does not, so each conclusion has to be checked against both designs.

Step 2. The first study drew 500500500 growers at random from the customer list, so its result covers that list. The second study's growers volunteered, so its result covers those 200200200 and no more.

Step 3. The first study assigned nothing, since growers were already buying what they buy, so it supports a link. The second study assigned the seed at random, so it supports a cause among its volunteers.

Step 4. The answer is a link rather than a cause, so there are no random-assignment words to point to. Only the first study reaches the customer list, so the conclusion is that among the growers on the supplier's customer list, treated seed goes with a higher yield.

A nature society has 6,5006{,}5006,500 members. A researcher drew 250250250 of them at random from the membership list and asked each one about their birdwatching habits. Members who keep a daily log reported spotting more species than members who do not. The society's newsletter reported the finding this way: Keeping a daily log makes society members spot more species.

Which rewriting carries the finding as far as the study supports it and no further?

Correct. The 250250250 were drawn at random from the membership list, so the finding covers all 6,5006{,}5006,500 members, and with no assignment anywhere a link is all the study gives.
